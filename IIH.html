Step 1 · Symptoms and Clinical Context
IIH is a syndrome of raised intracranial pressure without an identifiable structural, vascular, or metabolic cause, and with normal CSF composition. Classic demographics: young (20s–40s), female (~90%), obese (BMI ≥30). Incidence in obese women of childbearing age: ~22/100,000. Overall US incidence has more than doubled since 2002 (now ~2.4/100,000), closely tracking the obesity epidemic.
Symptoms of raised ICP in IIH — characteristic pattern:
Headache (94%): Daily or near-daily; non-specific but often positional (worse supine, bending forward, Valsalva), pulsatile, bifrontal or periorbital. Frequently the presenting complaint. Does not fully resolve with standard analgesics.
Transient visual obscurations (TVOs, ~72%): Seconds-long episodes of monocular or binocular grey-out/blackout triggered by posture change, Valsalva, or bright light. Pathognomonic of papilledema from any cause — not specific to IIH.
Pulsatile tinnitus (~52%): Whooshing or heartbeat synchronous sound in one or both ears — reflects pulsatile transmission of elevated venous pressure into the sigmoid/transverse sinus region. Cessation with ipsilateral jugular compression is characteristic.
Diplopia (~18%): Typically from unilateral or bilateral CN VI palsy (false localizing sign of raised ICP) — causes horizontal diplopia, worse at distance.
Visual loss: Permanent if untreated. ~25% of IIH patients develop significant visual field loss; severe visual loss in ~5–10%. The major morbidity of IIH — "benign intracranial hypertension" is a misnomer and strongly discouraged.
Presenting symptoms — select all present SELECT ALL THAT APPLY
Headache (daily or near-daily; often positional, worsened by Valsalva or lying down)
IIH headache is non-specific — it can mimic migraine, tension-type, or new daily persistent headache. Key distinguishing features from primary headache: positional component (worse supine), Valsalva aggravation, improvement with CSF drainage at LP, association with papilledema. The IIH headache does NOT have a classic "thunderclap" presentation (consider subarachnoid hemorrhage if so). Morning worsening is common. Headache may improve modestly with weight loss before papilledema fully resolves.
Overdiagnosis risk: Fisayo et al. (Neurology 2016) found IIH overdiagnosis in ~40% of referred patients at a tertiary center, most commonly due to CSF pressure measured in a non-lateral-decubitus position, anxious or tense patients, or headache attributed to borderline CSF pressure without papilledema. Strict adherence to diagnostic criteria is essential.
Transient visual obscurations (TVOs) — seconds-long monocular/binocular greying or blackout of vision, positionally triggered
TVOs are brief (seconds) episodes of monocular or binocular visual loss or greying, typically triggered by change in position (standing, bending forward) or Valsalva maneuver. They reflect transient ischemia of the papilledematous optic nerve from sudden ICP spikes. Present in ~72% of IIH with papilledema. TVOs are a marker of active papilledema with some degree of optic nerve ischemia. Persistent or prolonged visual loss (minutes to permanent) is more concerning and requires urgent evaluation. Distinguish from amaurosis fugax (embolic, carotid disease) — TVOs are typically bilateral, very brief, and positionally triggered.
Pulsatile tinnitus — synchronous with heartbeat; often unilateral; may cease with ipsilateral jugular vein compression
Pulsatile tinnitus (PT) in IIH reflects turbulent blood flow in the transverse/sigmoid sinus from elevated venous sinus pressure or stenosis. Present in ~52% of IIH. The characteristic cessation with ipsilateral internal jugular vein compression is a bedside test worth performing. PT can also be caused by dural arteriovenous fistula (DAVF) — important to exclude with MRA/MRV if PT is prominent and persistent, particularly when atypical for IIH. DAVFs classically cause an objective bruit (heard with a stethoscope over the mastoid or orbit) and can cause pulsatile tinnitus without raised ICP.
Differentiate from DAVF: Dural AV fistulas can cause pulsatile tinnitus, papilledema, and raised ICP mimicking IIH. MRV may show a draining vein but formal DSA is the gold standard for exclusion in high-suspicion cases (prominent PT, focal bruit, asymmetric symptoms).
Diplopia / CN VI palsy — horizontal diplopia worse at distance; unilateral or bilateral abducens palsy (false localizing sign)
CN VI (abducens) palsy is the only focal neurological sign permitted under the Friedman 2013 criteria — it is a recognized false localizing sign of raised ICP, caused by stretching of the nerve over the petrous ridge as the brainstem herniates slightly downward. Unilateral or bilateral CN VI palsy in IIH does not imply a structural lesion at the level of the pons. The Friedman 2013 criteria explicitly allow CN VI palsy without papilledema to establish "Definite IIH without papilledema" if CSF pressure ≥25 cmH₂O and imaging criteria are met. No other focal neurological deficits are permitted — their presence mandates further workup for an alternative diagnosis.
Key rule: Any focal neurological finding other than CN VI palsy (e.g., CN III, IV, facial palsy, long tract signs, cerebellar findings, altered consciousness) requires exclusion of a structural/vascular cause before IIH can be diagnosed. Under no version of the criteria is any other focal neurological deficit consistent with "typical" IIH.
Other focal neurological signs (any CN other than VI, long tract signs, cerebellar signs, altered consciousness, seizures)
The presence of any focal neurological sign other than unilateral or bilateral CN VI palsy is an exclusion criterion under all versions of the IIH diagnostic criteria. Such findings require comprehensive neuroimaging (MRI with contrast, MRV, possibly DSA) to exclude intracranial mass, venous sinus thrombosis, meningitis, encephalitis, or a structural cause of raised ICP. If found, the diagnosis of IIH should not be made until these are excluded.
Patient demographics — typical (female, childbearing age, obese/overweight) or atypical (male, lean, elderly, child)
Typical IIH: Female, age 15–44, BMI ≥30. Accounts for ~90% of IIH. Risk increases linearly with BMI — incidence 6.8/100,000 in women BMI 18.5–24.9, rising to 22/100,000 in women BMI ≥25. Recent weight gain (even in non-obese patients) is an independent risk factor.

Atypical IIH (require broader exclusion workup):
• Males: secondary causes more common — ensure androgen use, sleep apnea, and structural causes are excluded
• Postmenopausal women or age >44
• BMI <30 (lean IIH): consider secondary causes — hypervitaminosis A, medications, endocrine disease, dural venous fistula
• Children prepubertal: CSF pressure thresholds differ; prepubertal children may have IIH without obesity; sedation-adjusted OP norms apply
• Atypical patients require MRV in addition to standard MRI (Friedman 2013 explicit recommendation)
Friedman 2013 imaging rule: For typical patients (obese female of childbearing age), MRI brain with and without gadolinium is sufficient. For atypical patients (male, non-obese, elderly, child, or any patient with atypical features), MRI brain with and without gadolinium PLUS MRV (MR venography) is required to exclude venous sinus thrombosis and other secondary causes.
Step 2 · Optic Nerve Examination — Papilledema Assessment
Papilledema (optic disc swelling from raised ICP) is the cardinal objective sign of IIH. Its presence and severity directly determine which diagnostic tier applies under Friedman 2013 criteria. Formal dilated fundus examination by an experienced ophthalmologist or neuro-ophthalmologist is required. OCT RNFL thickness and serial visual fields are essential for monitoring.
Frisén grading scale for papilledema (Frisén 1982 — used in IIHTT trial):
Grade 0: Normal optic disc — C-shaped halo of blurred margin nasal edge only (physiologic variant). No papilledema.
Grade 1 (Subtle): C-shaped halo with temporal gap; peripapillary radial nerve fiber striations; no disc elevation
Grade 2 (Low grade): Circumferential halo; elevation of nasal border; no major vessel obscuration
Grade 3 (Moderate): Obscuration of ≥1 major vessel segment; elevation of all borders; halo with irregular outer fringe
Grade 4 (Marked): Complete obscuration of a major vessel on disc; full disc elevation; expansion of halo
Grade 5 (Severe): Obscuration of all vessels on/off disc; dome-shaped protrusion of optic nerve head

OCT peripapillary RNFL thickness: Most sensitive objective quantitative measure of papilledema degree and treatment response. IIHTT substudy showed acetazolamide + weight loss significantly reduced RNFL (175µm vs. 89µm reduction at 6 months, p=0.001). Normal RNFL ≤ ~100µm; papilledema typically >150µm; severe >250µm.

Visual field testing ESSENTIAL FOR MONITORING AND TREATMENT DECISIONS
Visual field results — automated perimetry (Humphrey 24-2 or 30-2) or Goldmann perimetry
Visual field defects occur in ~96% of IIH patients with papilledema on Goldmann perimetry and ~92% on automated perimetry. The most common pattern is inferior nasal field loss (arcuate defects, nasal steps), reflecting inferonasal RNFL bundle vulnerability. Enlarged blind spot (due to disc edema distorting the peripapillary retina) is common — largely reversible with treatment.

IIHTT visual field data: PMD (perimetric mean deviation) −2 to −7 dB (mild loss) was the entry criterion. Acetazolamide + diet improved PMD significantly vs. placebo + diet (mean improvement 0.7 dB vs. 0.3 dB, p=0.0257). Central/paracentral defects in ~14% — representing more severe optic nerve injury.

Visual acuity is insensitive: Normal Snellen acuity does not exclude significant visual field loss. Perimetry is the standard for detecting and monitoring IIH-related visual loss. Serial visual fields are mandatory for management decisions.
Treatment trigger (urgent/emergent): Any visual field defect approaching fixation (central/paracentral loss), acuity reduction below 20/40, or rapidly progressive visual loss (over days) requires urgent intervention — ONSF (optic nerve sheath fenestration) or CSF diversion procedure. Do not rely solely on LP alone for vision-threatening IIH.
Step 3 · Neuroimaging — MRI Brain ± MRV
Neuroimaging serves two purposes in IIH: (1) exclusion of mass lesions, hydrocephalus, and venous sinus thrombosis; (2) identification of positive MRI signs of raised ICP that support the diagnosis, particularly in IIH without papilledema. Friedman 2013 criteria specify the minimum imaging required.
Friedman 2013 imaging requirements:
Typical patients (obese female of childbearing age): MRI brain with AND without gadolinium (no MRV required if clinical picture is typical and MRI is normal)
Atypical patients (male, non-obese, elderly, child, atypical features): MRI brain with AND without gadolinium PLUS MR venography (MRV) to exclude CVST

MRI signs of raised ICP (supporting IIH — especially relevant in IIHWOP):
Empty sella / partial empty sella: CSF herniation through the diaphragma sella, compressing pituitary; highly prevalent in IIH
Flattening of posterior sclera (globe flattening): CSF pressure transmitted to posterior globe via the optic nerve sheath space
Distension of perioptic subarachnoid space: Prominent CSF around optic nerve sheaths on fat-saturated MRI
Transverse venous sinus stenosis (TVS): Narrowing of one or both transverse sinuses — present in ~93% of IIH (vs. 7% of controls). May be bilateral. Cause vs. consequence is debated; may be a therapeutic target (venous stenting)
MRI brain with/without contrast — parenchymal and structural assessment REQUIRED — MANDATORY EXCLUSION STEP
MRI brain result — parenchyma, ventricles, meninges, gadolinium enhancement
Required findings for IIH: Normal brain parenchyma; no hydrocephalus (may see slit ventricles — acceptable); no mass lesion; no abnormal meningeal enhancement (excludes meningitis, carcinomatous meningitis, granulomatous disease). Ventricular size is typically small or normal — the high ICP prevents ventricular dilation. Empty sella and optic nerve sheath distension are expected positive findings consistent with IIH and are not exclusionary abnormalities.

What would exclude IIH (require alternative diagnosis): Enhancing parenchymal lesion, white matter abnormalities suggesting demyelination, ependymal/leptomeningeal enhancement, true hydrocephalus (enlarged ventricles from obstruction or malabsorption), mass effect, or any other structural abnormality.
Slit ventricles: Very small ventricles on MRI can be seen in IIH — this was specifically identified in the original Modified Dandy criteria as an acceptable finding. Do not confuse small ventricles with hydrocephalus. Slit-like ventricles are a sign of diffuse cerebral edema or high ICP compressing ventricular walls.
MR venography (MRV) — cerebral venous sinus patency REQUIRED FOR ATYPICAL PATIENTS; CONSIDER IN ALL
MRV result — venous sinus thrombosis, dural AV fistula, intrinsic stenosis vs. secondary stenosis from raised ICP
Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST) can present identically to IIH — headache, papilledema, raised ICP, and CN VI palsy. Excluding CVST is mandatory before diagnosing IIH; this requires MRV (or CT venography). MRV findings in true CVST: absence of flow void in a dural sinus (superior sagittal, transverse, or sigmoid); intraluminal filling defect; collateral venous channels.

Transverse sinus stenosis (TSS) in IIH: TSS is a very common incidental finding in IIH (~93%) and may represent either a secondary phenomenon (raised ICP collapsing the compliant sinus walls) or a primary contributor to raised venous pressure. TSS alone on MRV does not constitute "CVST" — it is an expected IIH finding. True thrombosis (filling defect in the sinus, especially in the superior sagittal sinus) is exclusionary.
Clinical decision: Friedman 2013 explicitly requires MRV in atypical patients. Many experts recommend MRV in all patients with suspected IIH given the clinical overlap with CVST and the relatively low cost. A positive DSA (digital subtraction angiography) is the gold standard for DAVF exclusion — consider if pulsatile tinnitus is prominent or objective bruit is heard.
MRI signs of raised ICP — count of positive signs REQUIRED FOR IIHWOP SUGGESTED TIER: ≥3 OF 4
Positive MRI signs of elevated ICP — how many of the four are present? (Empty sella, posterior globe flattening, perioptic CSF distension, transverse sinus stenosis)
The four MRI signs formalized in Friedman 2013 for IIHWOP diagnosis:
1. Empty sella or partial empty sella — CSF herniates through diaphragma sella, flattening pituitary; highly specific for chronic elevated ICP
2. Flattening of posterior aspect of globes — bilateral posterior scleral flattening visible on axial MRI; reflects transmitted CSF pressure via optic nerve sheath
3. Distension of perioptic subarachnoid space — tortuous or enlarged optic nerve sheaths; best seen on fat-suppressed orbital MRI sequences
4. Transverse venous sinus stenosis — unilateral or bilateral narrowing of transverse sinuses on MRV or MR TOF sequences

A retrospective validation study found ≥3/4 signs has sensitivity ~39% but specificity ~91% for IIH (positive predictive value ~95%). Required for "Suggested IIH without papilledema" tier.
From Korsbæk et al. 2023 (Cephalalgia): For the presence of ≥3/4 MRI signs, sensitivity was 39.2% and specificity was 91.3% (PPV 95.2%, NPV 25.3%). The low sensitivity means negative MRI does not exclude IIH. High specificity means ≥3 signs strongly supports the diagnosis when present.
Step 4 · Lumbar Puncture — Opening Pressure and CSF Composition
LP with opening pressure measurement is required for IIH diagnosis. Critically, the measurement must be performed with the patient in the lateral decubitus position with legs extended (not in fetal position) — seated or anxious patients yield falsely elevated pressures and are a major source of overdiagnosis. Normal CSF composition is required for all IIH tiers.
CSF opening pressure thresholds (Friedman 2013 — lateral decubitus, legs extended):
≥25 cmH₂O (250 mmH₂O): Required for Definite IIH with papilledema, Definite IIHWOP with CN VI palsy, and Suggested IIHWOP tiers
20–24.9 cmH₂O: Borderline — required for Probable IIH with papilledema tier (papilledema present but CSF pressure not reaching 25 cmH₂O)
Children (sedated/awake): Normal is up to 28 cmH₂O in children (Avery et al. 2010); thresholds differ from adults
Obese adults: Mean OP ~20.5 cmH₂O in obese asymptomatic adults (Corbett and Mehta 1983) — the 25 cmH₂O threshold still applies

Measurement technique matters enormously: Patient anxiety, Valsalva, flexed legs, or sitting position significantly elevate measured OP. This is the single most common source of false-positive IIH diagnosis (Fisayo et al. Neurology 2016). Ensure lateral decubitus with legs extended and patient calm before recording OP.

CSF composition: Must be normal (normal cell count, protein, glucose). Abnormal CSF — pleocytosis, elevated protein, hypoglycorrhachia, or positive infectious/cytology studies — requires evaluation for meningitis, inflammatory disease, or malignancy.
CSF opening pressure — lateral decubitus, legs extended REQUIRED
Lumbar puncture opening pressure (cmH₂O) — measured in strict lateral decubitus position
Position requirement: Patient must be in left lateral decubitus, hips and knees extended (not flexed into chest). Correct position lowers OP by 5–15 cmH₂O vs. fetal position. Patient must be calm — Valsalva, coughing, or breath-holding during measurement artificially elevates OP.

Borderline pressure (20–24.9 cmH₂O): These patients with papilledema meet criteria for "Probable IIH" per Friedman 2013. The borderline range is clinically important — papilledema in the absence of definitive OP elevation still requires thorough exclusion of other causes and clinical follow-up. Repeat LP or ICP monitoring may clarify.

Normal OP (<20 cmH₂O) with papilledema: Must prompt evaluation for other causes of papilledema — bilateral optic disc drusen (pseudopapilledema), optic neuritis, infiltrative optic neuropathy, malignant hypertension, hypercapnia. "Probable IIH" requires papilledema with OP 20–24.9; if OP is truly normal, IIH is not supported.
Overdiagnosis data — Fisayo et al. Neurology 2016: In a referral cohort, 40% of patients diagnosed with IIH had been incorrectly diagnosed. Key errors: (1) OP measured in a non-standard position, (2) disc appearance misclassified as papilledema when it was pseudopapilledema (drusen, tilted discs), (3) CSF pressure accepted as elevated on suboptimal measurement. OCT B-scan of the disc and orbital ultrasound for drusen should be performed when papilledema is uncertain.
CSF composition MUST BE NORMAL FOR IIH DIAGNOSIS
CSF cell count, protein, glucose — normal required; also send infectious/malignant studies if clinically indicated
Normal CSF composition is required by ALL versions of the IIH/IIH diagnostic criteria (original Dandy, Modified Dandy, Friedman 2002, Friedman 2013). Any of the following is abnormal and excludes IIH: WBC >5 cells/µL, elevated protein (>45 mg/dL), low glucose, xanthochromia, or positive culture, cytology, or malignant cells.

If CSF abnormal, consider: Meningitis (bacterial, viral, fungal, TB), carcinomatous meningitis, neurosarcoidosis, lymphoma, or inflammatory CNS disease. These may cause raised ICP with papilledema and can mimic IIH clinically.

Routine CSF studies at LP for suspected IIH: Opening pressure (recorded first), cell count with differential, total protein, glucose (with concurrent serum glucose), Gram stain and culture, and any additional studies based on clinical suspicion (cryptococcal antigen, AFB smear/culture, VDRL, cytology, flow cytometry if lymphoma concern).
Step 5 · Exclusion of Secondary Causes and Alternative Diagnoses
IIH is a diagnosis of exclusion. The "idiopathic" designation requires that all identifiable secondary causes of intracranial hypertension have been rigorously excluded. Secondary causes must be excluded before applying the "idiopathic" label — if a cause is found, the diagnosis is "pseudotumor cerebri secondary to [cause]," not IIH.
Secondary causes of intracranial hypertension (pseudotumor cerebri syndrome — secondary):
Venous: Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST), dural AV fistula (DAVF), venous outflow obstruction (bilateral jugular vein thrombosis, radical neck dissection, superior mediastinal obstruction)
Medications (most common secondary cause to exclude): Tetracyclines (minocycline, doxycycline), vitamin A / retinoids (isotretinoin, all-trans retinoic acid), growth hormone, anabolic steroids, corticosteroid withdrawal, levothyroxine excess (especially in children), lithium, amiodarone, cyclosporine, nalidixic acid, danazol, progestin-only contraceptives
Endocrine / metabolic: Hypothyroidism (especially untreated), Addison's disease, hypoparathyroidism, PCOS, Cushing's syndrome (excess or withdrawal), anemia, chronic renal failure
Structural: Chiari malformation type I (can cause papilledema via altered CSF dynamics), intracranial mass (tumor, abscess, cyst), hydrocephalus
Infective/inflammatory: Chronic meningitis (TB, crypto, sarcoid), post-meningitic arachnoiditis, viral encephalitis, Behçet's disease
Medication and substance review CRITICAL — MOST ACTIONABLE SECONDARY CAUSE
Review of current medications — tetracyclines, retinoids (isotretinoin), vitamin A supplements, growth hormone, steroids, oral contraceptives, lithium
Drug-induced intracranial hypertension (DIIH) is an important secondary cause. The offending drug should be discontinued if clinically feasible — this is the most actionable intervention and may resolve the condition.

Highest-risk medications:
• Tetracyclines (minocycline, doxycycline): Most common antibiotic cause — particularly minocycline for acne in young women (the same demographic as IIH). Mechanism unclear but may relate to effects on CSF dynamics or vitamin A pathway.
• Retinoids / vitamin A: Isotretinoin (Accutane) for acne. Hypervitaminosis A from supplements or liver (polar bear liver). All-trans retinoic acid for leukemia treatment.
• Corticosteroid withdrawal: Paradoxically, abrupt steroid discontinuation after chronic use can precipitate IH. Initial IH during high-dose steroids is a separate effect.
• Growth hormone: Supplementation in children and adults — discontinue and monitor.
• Levothyroxine excess: Particularly in children — check thyroid function.
Management if medication is causative: Discontinue the offending agent. Treat with acetazolamide and weight management as for idiopathic cases while awaiting resolution. In cases with vision-threatening papilledema, ONSF or shunting may still be required even if the offending drug is discontinued. The diagnosis becomes "pseudotumor cerebri secondary to [drug]" rather than IIH.
Endocrine and metabolic workup OBTAIN IN ALL ATYPICAL AND IN STANDARD WORKUP
Thyroid function (TSH), morning cortisol / ACTH stim test (if Addison's suspected), calcium/PTH (hypoparathyroidism), CBC (anemia), renal function
Hypothyroidism: Can cause raised ICP and papilledema, particularly when severely undertreated. TSH should be checked in all IIH workups. Correcting hypothyroidism may resolve the IH.

Addison's disease: Adrenal insufficiency is associated with IH — mechanism possibly related to altered mineralocorticoid balance and sodium/water handling. Associated with IIH in case series and case reports.

Anemia: Severe anemia (iron deficiency, sickle cell) has been associated with raised ICP, possibly via compensatory venous engorgement. Check CBC in all IIH patients.

Chronic renal failure: Uremia and dialysis-related fluid shifts can contribute to raised ICP. Less commonly a sole cause but worth considering in appropriate context.
Pseudopapilledema exclusion — is the disc swelling truly papilledema? CRITICAL — MAJOR SOURCE OF OVERDIAGNOSIS
Exclude pseudopapilledema — optic disc drusen (buried or superficial), tilted discs, disc elevation from other causes
Optic disc drusen (ODD) are calcified hyaline deposits within the optic nerve that can cause the disc to appear elevated, mimicking papilledema. ODD prevalence is ~2% of the general population. Distinguishing true papilledema from pseudopapilledema is critical — misclassification is the second most common cause of IIH overdiagnosis (Fisayo et al. 2016).

Features suggesting TRUE papilledema (vs. ODD): Peripapillary flame hemorrhages; venous engorgement; absence of spontaneous venous pulsations (SVP); disc hyperemia; nerve fiber layer hemorrhages; lumbar puncture elevated OP. OCT enhanced depth imaging (EDI-OCT) can visualize buried drusen as hyperreflective bodies.

Tests for pseudopapilledema: B-scan orbital ultrasound (highly sensitive for drusen — hyperechoic signal at optic nerve head at low gain); OCT optic nerve head; fundus autofluorescence (surface drusen appear autofluorescent); fundus fluorescein angiography (ODD show late autofluorescence without disc hyperfluorescence typical of papilledema).
SVP (spontaneous venous pulsations): Presence of SVP on funduscopy suggests ICP is not markedly elevated (SVP are absent when ICP exceeds ~20 cmH₂O). Absence of SVP is not specific for raised ICP (SVP are absent in ~20% of normal individuals). Do not use SVP alone to diagnose or exclude papilledema.

Step 1 · Is This a Seizure?
Obtain detailed history, eyewitness account, and review prior medical records. The history is the most important diagnostic tool.
Features Favoring Seizure Select All Present
Witnessed tonic-clonic movements or unresponsiveness
Generalized tonic-clonic activity with bilateral limb involvement; may include stiffening followed by rhythmic jerking. Unresponsiveness during and after the episode is strongly suggestive.
Lateral tongue biting
Lateral tongue biting is highly specific for tonic-clonic seizure (specificity ~99.6%, sensitivity ~45%; Benbadis & Wolgamuth, Neurology 1995). Tip-of-tongue biting is less specific and may occur in syncope. Cheek biting also favors seizure.
Key point: Lateral tongue biting is one of the most specific clinical features distinguishing seizure from syncope.
Urinary or bowel incontinence
Postictal confusion or amnesia
Postictal state typically lasts minutes to hours. Patient may be confused, combative, or aphasic. Amnesia for the event is common and supports seizure over psychogenic event.
Prodromal aura (déjà vu, epigastric rising, olfactory, autonomic)
Auras represent focal seizure onset before secondarily generalized spread. Common auras: déjà vu, jamais vu, epigastric rising sensation, fear, gustatory/olfactory hallucinations, visual phenomena, visceral sensations, "impending doom." Aura implies focal-onset seizure.
Todd's paralysis (focal weakness after event)
Transient focal neurological deficit after a seizure (typically minutes to hours, rarely up to 48h). Present in ~13% of complex partial or generalized tonic-clonic seizures. When present, strongly suggests focal-onset seizure — particularly consider structural etiology and proceed urgently to MRI (Kellinghaus et al. 2004). Consider urgent MRI.
Features Favoring Seizure Mimic Red Flags — Consider Alternative Diagnosis
Chest pain, lightheadedness, tunnel vision, palpitations before event
These prodromal symptoms suggest vasovagal or cardiac syncope. Note: brief convulsive movements (myoclonic jerks) can occur in syncope due to brief cerebral hypoperfusion — this is "convulsive syncope" and does NOT indicate epilepsy. Obtain orthostatic BP, ECG, and cardiac monitoring as appropriate.
Action: Obtain 12-lead ECG, orthostatic vital signs, Holter monitor. Refer to cardiology if indicated.
Fluctuating course, eyes closed, alternating movements, no postictal state
Features suggesting psychogenic nonepileptic episodes (PNEE): eyes closed during the event, side-to-side head movements, pelvic thrusting, ictal crying, alternating limb movements, gradual onset/offset, rapid recovery without postictal confusion, recall of the event. PNEE is not epilepsy — it requires psychiatric management, not ASMs.
Gold standard for diagnosis: Video-EEG monitoring capturing a typical episode without ictal correlate.
Step 2 · Is the Patient Stable?
Assess whether the patient has returned to their neurologic baseline. Active or recent seizures with persistent AMS require urgent evaluation before routine workup.
Neurologic Status Critical Branch Point
Has the patient returned to neurologic baseline?
⚠ Urgent Evaluation Required
Patient has not returned to baseline. Initiate urgent workup simultaneously.
Urgent Workup Checklist Initiate Immediately
ABCs — airway, breathing, circulation; IV access; fingerstick glucose
Emergent CT head non-contrast — rule out hemorrhage, mass, herniation
CT head without contrast is the first-line urgent imaging. Identifies: intracranial hemorrhage, large space-occupying lesions, hydrocephalus, herniation. MRI is more sensitive for epileptogenic lesions but CT is faster in the acute setting.
Stat labs: BMP (Na, Ca, Mg, glucose), CBC, LFTs, ammonia, tox screen, BAL
Consider LP if fever, immunocompromise, meningismus, or persistent AMS
LP indications after first seizure: fever + seizure (meningitis/encephalitis), HIV/immunocompromised, meningismus on exam, persistent AMS unexplained by imaging or metabolic workup, CSF-VGKC or NMDAR antibodies suspected. Always obtain CT first to rule out mass effect if any concern.
Ongoing seizure activity assessment
Status epilepticus: seizure lasting ≥5 minutes or ≥2 seizures without return to baseline between events. First-line: IV/IM benzodiazepine (lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg IV or midazolam 10 mg IM). If refractory, escalate per SE protocol.

Altered mentation without clinical seizure: Consider nonconvulsive status epilepticus (NCSE) — obtain LTM video EEG urgently. NCSE can present as subtle eye deviation, automatisms, fluctuating AMS, or unexplained encephalopathy. Diagnosis requires EEG correlation.

Low suspicion: Patient is postictal with expected gradual improvement — no ongoing seizure activity, monitoring appropriate.
Step 3 · Provoked vs. Unprovoked?
Approximately 25–40% of first seizures are associated with a provoking factor. Identifying and treating the precipitating cause is the primary intervention for provoked seizures. (AAFP 2022; Hauser WA et al., BMJ 2004)
Common Provoking Factors — Identify and Address ~25–40% of First Seizures
Metabolic: Hyponatremia, hypoglycemia, hypocalcemia, hypomagnesemia, uremia, hepatic encephalopathy — reversible; very low subsequent unprovoked seizure risk once corrected (≤3%, Hauser BMJ 2004)
Toxic: Alcohol withdrawal, benzodiazepine withdrawal, cocaine, amphetamines
Medications: Tramadol, bupropion (dose-dependent), fluoroquinolones, theophylline, isoniazid (always give pyridoxine)
Structural (acute): Ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, TBI, CNS infection, tumor
Systemic: Eclampsia (if pregnant), hypertensive encephalopathy (PRES), sepsis, autoimmune encephalitis
Is a clear provoking factor identified and addressable?
Provoked seizure (acute symptomatic seizure): A seizure occurring in close temporal relationship with an acute CNS insult or systemic provoking factor. Long-term ASM therapy is generally NOT required once the provoking factor is treated. A first seizure caused by an acute disturbance of brain function is unlikely to recur as an unprovoked event: recurrence risk as an unprovoked seizure is ≤3% for truly reversible metabolic/toxic causes (Hauser WA et al., BMJ 2004). Patients with acute symptomatic seizures due to reversible causes have an ~80% decreased likelihood of subsequent unprovoked seizure compared to remote symptomatic seizures (Rizvi et al. 2017). However, this low recurrence applies only when the provoking factor is fully corrected.

Exception — structural lesions: Even if the seizure appears "provoked" by an acute structural event (e.g., stroke, TBI), a chronic structural lesion confers ongoing epileptogenesis risk. These patients may require ASM therapy even after the acute event is treated — reclassify as high-risk and consider treatment per Step 5.

Unprovoked seizure: No identifiable correctable precipitating factor. 2-year recurrence risk 21–45%.
Provoked seizure — treat the underlying cause. Long-term ASM therapy is generally NOT indicated. Recurrence risk is low once the provoking factor is corrected. Schedule outpatient neurology follow-up for reassessment.
⚠ Structural lesion identified — treat the acute cause AND proceed to risk stratification (Step 5). Structural lesions confer ongoing epileptogenesis risk that may warrant ASM therapy even after the acute event resolves.
Step 4 · Diagnostic Workup
Order the following for all patients with a first unprovoked seizure. These results directly inform treatment decisions in Steps 5 and 6.
Essential Studies — All Patients Required
12-lead ECG
Essential for two reasons: (1) Rule out cardiac arrhythmia as a seizure mimic; (2) Obtain baseline PR interval, QRS duration, and QTc before starting ASMs — many ASMs affect cardiac conduction.
Key intervals: PR prolongation is relevant for lamotrigine and lacosamide. QTc prolongation relevant if considering any ASM with cardiac effects.
EEG — routine EEG if back at baseline; LTM video EEG if not at baseline
Routine EEG — when patient has returned to neurologic baseline. Ideally within 24 hours of the seizure (yield declines over days). Epileptiform abnormalities significantly increase recurrence risk: ILAE/IFCN meta-analysis (2024, PMC12605794) — pooled OR 2.32 (95% CI 1.69–3.17); adults OR 1.55 (95% CI 1.08–2.21); children OR 3.24 (95% CI 2.19–4.79). Overall recurrence probability 60% with IEDs vs. 40% without. If initial routine EEG is normal, consider sleep-deprived EEG or ambulatory EEG within 7 days — recording yield increases with sleep deprivation and prolonged recording duration.

Long-term monitoring (LTM) video EEG — when patient has NOT returned to baseline, or when NCSE must be excluded. Video EEG allows correlation of clinical behavior with electrographic activity. Nonconvulsive status epilepticus (NCSE) can present as subtle motor activity, eye deviation, or unexplained prolonged AMS — EMU/LTM vEEG is the gold standard for diagnosis.
Result interpretation: Generalized spike-wave → generalized epilepsy syndrome. Focal discharges → focal-onset epilepsy. Normal EEG does not exclude epilepsy — sensitivity of routine EEG for epileptiform discharges is ~50%; repeat or prolonged recording increases yield.
MRI brain with and without contrast, seizure protocol — unless a clear benign structural etiology is already known (e.g. encephalomalacia, MTS) or clinical context is certain
MRI brain is significantly more sensitive than CT for epileptogenic lesions — 12% of patients with a normal CT had an epileptogenic lesion on subsequent MRI (PMC5765827, 2018). Mesial temporal sclerosis, cortical dysplasia, cavernomas, focal gliosis, and low-grade tumors are routinely missed on CT.

On contrast — when to order with and without: For a first-time seizure where the underlying etiology is unknown or uncertain, MRI with and without contrast should be ordered to evaluate for infection (e.g. encephalitis, abscess), inflammation (autoimmune encephalitis, demyelination), and neoplasia — all of which may enhance and would be missed on non-contrast MRI alone. This is the clinically safe default when you do not know the cause.

When non-contrast alone may be sufficient: If a clear, benign structural etiology is already established (e.g. known mesial temporal sclerosis on prior imaging, encephalomalacia from a prior stroke in the appropriate territory, known cavernoma), contrast adds little. Nelson et al. (J Neuroimaging 2021): 100% of epileptogenic lesions without suspected neoplasia/infection were detected on non-contrast sequences. ACR Appropriateness Criteria (2020): MRI without contrast is "usually appropriate" for new-onset seizure; with-and-without is "may be appropriate." Clinical judgment required — when in doubt, include contrast.

Seizure protocol (institution-dependent): There is no single universal protocol — sequences vary by institution. Most commonly includes: thin-cut (≤1.5 mm) coronal T2/FLAIR through the hippocampi (perpendicular to the long axis of the hippocampus), axial FLAIR, DWI, T2* or susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) for microhemorrhage and calcification, and post-contrast T1. Some institutions add 3D MPRAGE volumetric sequences. Always verify with your neuroradiology department. CT first if emergent; follow with MRI electively for all first unprovoked seizures.
Baseline labs: BMP, CBC, LFTs, albumin, renal function
Albumin and renal function are critical before ASM selection — many ASMs are protein-bound (affected by hypoalbuminemia) or renally cleared (requiring dose adjustment). LFTs guide selection of hepatically-metabolized agents. CBC baseline before carbamazepine or valproate.
Context-Specific Studies Order as Clinically Indicated
HLA-B*15:02 testing — patients of Asian descent
REQUIRED before prescribing carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, eslicarbazepine, phenytoin, or lamotrigine in patients of Asian descent (especially Han Chinese, Southeast Asian). HLA-B*15:02 allele is strongly associated with Stevens-Johnson syndrome/TEN with aromatic AEDs. Prevalence ~6–8% in Han Chinese, lower in other populations. If positive: AVOID all implicated agents.
Pregnancy test — women of childbearing potential
If positive: AVOID valproate (up to 10% major congenital malformations, up to 40% neurodevelopmental delay). Preferred agents in pregnancy: levetiracetam (lowest teratogenic risk data), lamotrigine (monitor levels — decrease in pregnancy). First-line for generalized epilepsy in women of childbearing potential: levetiracetam.
Toxicology screen — if substance use suspected
Creatinine clearance — if renal impairment suspected
Levetiracetam requires dose adjustment if CrCl <80 mL/min. Topiramate, gabapentin, and pregabalin also require renal dose adjustment. Valproate, lamotrigine, and carbamazepine are primarily hepatically metabolized and generally safe in renal disease.
Step 5 · Risk Stratification — To Treat or Not?
2-year recurrence risk after a single unprovoked seizure is 21–47% (Hauser et al. 1998; Marson et al. MESS 2005). Immediate ASM therapy reduces recurrence by ~30% relative risk in the first 2 years (MESS, Lancet 2005: HR 1.4, 95% CI 1.2–1.7) but does NOT improve long-term remission — no difference in seizure-free rates between years 3–5 (MESS; FIRST Group, Neurology 1997 & 2006).
Counseling points for shared decision-making:
Recurrence reduction: MESS trial (Marson et al., Lancet 2005; n=1,847): immediate ASM reduced risk of first seizure (HR 1.4, 95% CI 1.2–1.7) and second seizure (HR 1.3, 95% CI 1.1–1.6) compared to deferred treatment. Absolute difference modest — not elimination of risk.
Long-term prognosis: MESS trial: no evidence of effect on long-term remission rates — no difference in seizure-free rates between years 3–5 regardless of when treatment was started (Marson et al. 2006, Lancet Neurol). FIRST Group (Musicco et al., Neurology 1997; Leone et al., Neurology 2006): treatment of first tonic-clonic seizure does not affect long-term remission.
ASM adverse effects: Occur in 7–31% of patients in clinical trials, mostly mild and reversible. MESS quality-of-life analysis: benefits of reduced recurrence were offset by adverse events and stigma in low-risk patients (Kim et al. 2006). Weigh against patient-specific consequences of seizure recurrence (driving, employment, falls, SUDEP).
Patient-specific factors: Risk of seizure recurrence vs. risk of medication side effects; occupation, driving status, social context.
High-Risk Features — Consider Starting ASM Any Present → Favor Treatment
Epileptiform abnormalities on EEG (OR 2.32 for recurrence; ILAE/IFCN meta-analysis 2024)
Structural brain lesion on MRI (remote symptomatic: recurrence up to 48% at 5 years, Hauser 1998)
Prior brain insult (stroke, TBI, CNS infection)
Nocturnal seizure (independent predictor of recurrence; Berg & Shinnar meta-analysis)
Focal neurologic findings on examination
Family history of epilepsy

Step 6 · Antiseizure Medication Selection
Select seizure type to see first-line options, then review drug-specific contraindications based on the patient's profile from the workup steps.
Seizure Type Required — Determines First-Line Choice
Focal-Onset Seizure
Begins in one hemisphere; may secondarily generalize. EEG shows focal discharges. Includes focal aware, focal impaired awareness, focal to bilateral tonic-clonic.
Generalized-Onset Seizure
Involves both hemispheres simultaneously from onset. Includes generalized tonic-clonic, absence, myoclonic, atonic. EEG shows generalized discharges.
Myoclonic Seizure
Brief, shock-like muscle jerks (single or repetitive). Often occurs on waking. May be part of juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (JME) or other generalized syndrome.
Unclassified / Unknown Onset
Onset could not be determined from history/EEG. Use a broad-spectrum agent effective for both focal and generalized epilepsies.
⚠ Avoid in Myoclonic Seizures: Carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, phenytoin, gabapentin, and pregabalin can worsen myoclonic and absence seizures.

Step 1 · Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS)
The ESS (Johns MW. Sleep 1991;14:540–545) measures average daytime sleepiness across 8 situations on a 0–3 scale. Total range 0–24. ESS is a screening tool, not a diagnostic test — over 50% of patients with moderate-to-severe OSA score <10 (Sleep Heart Health Study). A normal ESS does NOT exclude OSA. Rate the chance of dozing in each situation, even if you haven’t done it recently.
Rating scale for each item: 0 = Would never doze  •  1 = Slight chance  •  2 = Moderate chance  •  3 = High chance of dozing

ESS interpretation: 0–10 = Normal daytime sleepiness  •  11–15 = Mild-to-moderate excessive sleepiness  •  16–24 = Severe sleepiness (consider narcolepsy, severe OSA)

Critical caveat (Johns 1991; Sleep Heart Health Study): ESS has limited sensitivity and specificity for OSA. >50% of patients with AHI ≥15 score ≤10. ESS is useful for monitoring treatment response (ESS falls with effective CPAP) and assessing functional impairment, not for diagnosis.

Step 2 · STOP-BANG Questionnaire
The STOP-BANG questionnaire (Chung F et al. Anesthesiology 2008;108:812–821) is an 8-item yes/no screening tool for OSA risk. Score 0–8. It was developed and validated in surgical populations and subsequently validated in general populations and commercial drivers (meta-analysis, Nagappa M et al. PLOS ONE 2015). High sensitivity, lower specificity — designed to rule out moderate-to-severe OSA, not to confirm it.
STOP-BANG performance (Chung F, Anesthesiology 2008 & Chest 2016 review):
Score ≥3 sensitivity: 84% for any OSA (AHI >5), 93% for moderate-to-severe (AHI >15), 100% for severe (AHI >30). Corresponding specificities: 56%, 43%, 37%.
Score 0–2: Low risk — moderate-to-severe OSA confidently ruled out (NPV 94.8% for severe OSA).
Score 3–4: Intermediate risk — upgrade to high risk if BMI >35, neck >40 cm, or male + STOP ≥2.
Score 5–8: High risk — sleep study indicated.

Step 3 · Comorbidities & Risk Factors
OSA is independently associated with major cardiovascular and metabolic comorbidities. Their presence influences the threshold for treatment initiation, aggressiveness of follow-up, and choice of diagnostic testing (in-lab PSG vs. home sleep apnea test). Comorbidities may also contribute to residual sleepiness despite adequate CPAP therapy.
OSA comorbidity burden:
Hypertension: OSA is present in ~30% of hypertensive patients and ~50% of resistant hypertension. CPAP reduces BP by 2–3 mmHg (modest but clinically meaningful in resistant HTN).
Atrial fibrillation: OSA in ~50% of AF patients. Untreated OSA doubles AF recurrence after cardioversion/ablation. CPAP improves AF rhythm control outcomes.
HFrEF: OSA in ~20–40% of heart failure patients. Cheyne-Stokes respiration (central sleep apnea) is distinct — home sleep testing may miss CSA; PSG preferred in HF.
T2DM: OSA impairs insulin sensitivity independently. Bidirectional relationship with obesity.
Driving / occupation: Untreated moderate-to-severe OSA carries 3× higher motor vehicle accident risk. Mandatory reporting requirements vary by jurisdiction. Commercial drivers (DOT) require documentation of treatment adequacy.

Anatomical / structural upper airway factors INFLUENCES SURGERY CANDIDACY
Upper airway anatomical findings — tonsillar hypertrophy (grade III–IV), retrognathia, macroglossia, deviated septum, nasal polyps, large tongue
Anatomical factors significantly influence surgical candidacy. Tonsillar hypertrophy (grade III–IV): Tonsillectomy is highly effective in adult OSA with large tonsils (AHI reduction ~50%). Retrognathia: Mandibular advancement surgery (MMA) may produce dramatic AHI reduction in carefully selected patients. Friedman tongue position: High tongue (class I–II) with tonsillar hypertrophy predicts better surgical outcomes. Nasal obstruction: Nasal surgery alone rarely cures OSA but may improve CPAP tolerance by reducing required pressure.
Drug-induced sleep endoscopy (DISE): Required before hypoglossal nerve stimulation (Inspire) to exclude complete concentric velopharyngeal collapse — a contraindication. DISE visualizes collapse pattern during pharmacologically induced sleep and guides surgical approach. Complete concentric collapse (CCC) at palatal level predicts poor surgical outcomes.
Step 4 · AHI Severity Classification & Diagnostic Testing
OSA diagnosis requires objective sleep testing. The apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) is the number of apneas + hypopneas per hour of sleep. AASM severity classification: Normal <5; Mild 5–14.9; Moderate 15–29.9; Severe ≥30 events/hour. The AHI alone does not fully capture OSA severity — oxygen desaturation, arousal burden, and symptoms also matter.
Diagnostic testing options:
In-lab polysomnography (PSG, Type 1): Gold standard. Full EEG, EOG, EMG, respiratory, cardiac, oximetry monitoring. Required for complex cases (heart failure, neuromuscular disease, hypoventilation, suspected central sleep apnea).
Home sleep apnea test (HSAT, Type 3): Portable monitoring without EEG. Records airflow, respiratory effort, SpO2 ± HR. Appropriate for patients with high pre-test probability of uncomplicated OSA and no significant comorbidities. Typically underestimates AHI vs PSG (uses total recording time not total sleep time — AHI may be falsely low).
AHI limitations: Equates apneas (complete obstruction) with hypopneas (partial) — physiological effects differ. Does not account for event duration, body position, or sleep stage. Oxygen desaturation index (ODI) and T90 (time SpO2 <90%) provide complementary severity data.

Oxygen desaturation & nocturnal hypoxemia SUPPLEMENTS AHI FOR SEVERITY ASSESSMENT
Significant nocturnal oxygen desaturation — T90 (time SpO2 <90%) >1% of sleep time or nadir SpO2 <85%
Nocturnal hypoxemia adds independent prognostic weight beyond AHI. T90 >1% (cumulative sleep time with SpO2 <90%) correlates with cardiovascular risk and is used in some insurance criteria for PAP approval. Nadir SpO2 <85% indicates severe intermittent hypoxemia — heightens urgency for treatment. In mild OSA with significant desaturation, treatment threshold should be lowered. Oxygen desaturation index (ODI) = number of ≥3% (or ≥4%) SpO2 drops per hour; correlates with AHI but may diverge in REM-predominant or positional OSA.
Supplemental oxygen is NOT recommended as monotherapy for OSA. Oxygen supplementation corrects desaturation but does not address airway obstruction, arousals, or cardiovascular consequences of the underlying OSA (fragmented sleep, sympathetic surges). CPAP or PAP therapy is required. Supplemental oxygen may be added in selected cases with residual nocturnal hypoxemia despite adequate PAP therapy.
Testing modality used AFFECTS AHI INTERPRETATION
Sleep study type — in-lab PSG (full polysomnography) vs. home sleep apnea test (HSAT/portable monitoring)
HSAT underestimates AHI: HSAT divides respiratory events by total recording time (not total sleep time). A patient who takes 1 hour to fall asleep and wakes early may have actual AHI ~25% higher than HSAT reports. If HSAT is negative but clinical suspicion is high (high STOP-BANG, symptomatic), proceed to in-lab PSG. HSAT is not appropriate for: heart failure, suspected central sleep apnea, hypoventilation disorders, neuromuscular disease, prior stroke, or severe COPD.
Step 5 · Treatment Decision Tree
Treatment selection is guided by OSA severity (AHI), symptom burden (ESS), comorbidities, patient anatomy, and patient preference. PAP therapy (CPAP or APAP) is first-line for moderate-to-severe OSA. Oral appliance therapy (OAT) is an evidence-based alternative, especially for mild-to-moderate OSA or CPAP-intolerant patients. Weight loss, positional therapy, and surgery are adjunctive or alternative options.
CPAP / PAP adherence status REQUIRED FOR INSURANCE COVERAGE IN MOST JURISDICTIONS
PAP adherence — ≥4 hours/night on ≥70% of nights (standard AASM definition). Adherence data downloadable from most modern PAP devices.
CPAP adherence definition: Standard = ≥4 h/night on ≥70% of nights over a 30-day period. CMS definition for coverage: ≥4 h/night on ≥70% of nights during any consecutive 30-day period within first 90 days of use.

Optimising adherence: Auto-CPAP (APAP) adjusts pressure breath-by-breath — often better tolerated than fixed CPAP. Heated humidification reduces nasal dryness (major cause of non-adherence). Mask fit is critical (nasal pillow > full face for most; full face if mouth breathing). Pressure relief (EPR, C-Flex) reduces expiratory discomfort. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for CPAP adherence improves long-term use. Telemedicine & remote monitoring programs improve 90-day adherence by ~20–30%.
Residual AHI on CPAP (download data): Residual AHI ≤5 on PAP = well-controlled. Residual AHI >10 = inadequate control — check mask leak, pressure settings, and consider BPAP. If patient remains sleepy despite AHI <5, investigate: insufficient sleep duration, non-restorative sleep disorder, PLMD, depression, hypothyroidism, or narcolepsy (MSLT if suspected).
Inspire / hypoglossal nerve stimulation eligibility FOR CPAP-INTOLERANT MODERATE-SEVERE OSA
Inspire UAS eligibility check — AHI 15–65, BMI ≤32 kg/m², documented CPAP failure, age ≥18, predominantly obstructive events (<25% central/mixed)
Hypoglossal nerve stimulation (Inspire Medical Systems) is FDA-approved second-line therapy for moderate-to-severe OSA (AHI 15–65) in patients who have failed or are intolerant of CPAP. The stimulator senses respiratory effort and delivers mild tongue nerve stimulation to keep the airway open during sleep.

Eligibility criteria (FDA / CMS): AHI 15–65 events/hour; BMI ≤32 kg/m² (some centers ≤35); documented CPAP failure or intolerance (<4h/night on <70% nights); predominantly obstructive AHI (central + mixed <25% of total AHI); age ≥18 (FDA); no complete concentric velopharyngeal collapse on drug-induced sleep endoscopy (DISE).

Efficacy: STAR trial (Strollo et al. NEJM 2014): AHI reduced from median 29.3 to 9.0 events/hour at 12 months (68% AHI reduction); ESS improved from 11.6 to 7.0. 5-year outcomes stable. 2024 extended AHI range up to 100 in selected patients (BMI ≤40).
AHI >65 or BMI >35: Standard Inspire eligibility is exceeded. Bariatric surgery should be discussed for patients with BMI >35 and moderate-to-severe OSA. Weight loss of 10% predicts ~26% reduction in AHI. Bariatric surgery can achieve sustained AHI reduction >50% in morbidly obese patients but does not reliably cure OSA and PAP therapy is still required until post-operative PSG confirms resolution.

Step 1 · Presentation & FND Subtype
FND (functional neurological disorder) is diagnosed based on positive clinical signs demonstrating internal inconsistency or incongruence with recognised neurological disease. It is not a diagnosis of exclusion. FND accounts for 4–12% of neurology outpatient referrals. Diagnostic stability is comparable to MS and epilepsy when positive signs are used (Stone J et al. 2005, 2012). Select the primary presenting subtype — multiple subtypes frequently coexist.
Conceptual framework (Aybek & Perez, BMJ 2022; Espay et al. JAMA Neurol 2018):
FND reflects altered brain network function — not structural damage, not malingering, not purely psychogenic. Functional neuroimaging demonstrates abnormal connectivity in motor control networks (supplementary motor area, anterior cingulate cortex) and altered predictive processing. Symptoms are involuntary and genuine. The biopsychosocial model incorporates predisposing (genetics, prior neurological disease), precipitating (physical injury, illness, trauma), and perpetuating (avoidance, fear, attention) factors.

Key terminology shift: “Functional” is preferred over “psychogenic,” “conversion,” or “hysteria.” DSM-5-TR (2022) term: Functional Neurological Symptom Disorder. The DSM-5-TR no longer requires a psychological stressor for diagnosis.
Select primary FND subtype(s) MULTIPLE SUBTYPES MAY COEXIST
Symptom onset pattern INFORMS PROGNOSIS
Onset character — abrupt/paroxysmal onset vs. gradual; associated trigger or precipitant (physical injury, illness, stressor, medical procedure)
Acute onset (days to weeks) is associated with better prognosis than insidious onset. Physical injury or illness as a precipitant is common (present in ~37% of cases; Aybek & Perez 2022) and does NOT invalidate the FND diagnosis — FND can coexist with genuine structural injury. Latrogenic precipitants (FND emerging after neurosurgery, spinal procedure, minor injury) are well-documented. Psychological trauma as a precipitant is important but is no longer required by DSM-5-TR.

Prognosis data (Gelauff J et al. J Neurol Neurosurg Psychiatry 2014): In functional motor disorders, ~40% of patients improve or remit. Poor prognostic factors: longer symptom duration at diagnosis, high somatic symptom burden, disability benefits/litigation, and comorbid personality disorder.
Positive feedback loop: Attention to symptoms and fear of movement (kinesiophobia) perpetuate FND through reinforcement of aberrant predictive brain models. This is why physiotherapy that encourages movement paradoxically improves symptoms — Hoover sign positive patients who can run, walk backwards, or cycle may have preserved function in automatic vs. voluntary movement contexts.
Prior neurological disorder — does the patient have a documented, confirmed structural neurological disease that may coexist with FND?
FND commonly coexists with genuine neurological disease — this is one of the most important paradigm shifts in modern FND diagnosis. Up to 20–30% of patients with epilepsy also have functional seizures (PNES). FND has been described alongside MS, Parkinson’s disease, stroke, and peripheral neuropathy. The presence of an underlying neurological disease does NOT exclude FND, but does raise the bar for positive sign identification and increases the risk of diagnostic error in both directions. Document clearly what is attributable to each condition.
FND and Parkinson’s disease: Functional tremor can overlay PD tremor. Clues to functional overlay: (1) tremor that entrains or suppresses beyond what is expected for PD; (2) variable frequency different from the characteristic 4–6 Hz rest tremor; (3) symptoms disproportionate to dopaminergic deficits on DATscan. Functional parkinsonism (without PD) shows excessive slowness without true decrement-fatigue, inconsistent rigidity, and normal DATscan.
Step 2 · DSM-5-TR Diagnostic Criteria (APA 2022)
The DSM-5-TR (2022) criteria for Functional Neurological Symptom Disorder (FNSD) require all four criteria to be met. The most important update from DSM-5 (2013): Criterion B now explicitly states that positive signs demonstrating internal inconsistency or incongruence are the diagnostic standard — no longer requires a psychological stressor (removed from all criteria), and does not require confirmation that symptoms are not intentionally produced.
DSM-5-TR Criteria for Functional Neurological Symptom Disorder (F44.x):
Criterion A: One or more symptoms of altered voluntary motor or sensory function
Criterion B: Clinical findings provide evidence of incompatibility between the symptom and recognised neurological or medical conditions. Incompatibility is demonstrated by positive signs (internal inconsistency or incongruence)
Criterion C: The symptom or deficit is not better explained by another medical or mental disorder
Criterion D: The symptom or deficit causes clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other important areas of functioning, or warrants medical evaluation

Specify subtype, onset (acute vs. persistent), and whether with/without psychological stressor (optional)
Criterion A — Altered voluntary motor or sensory function REQUIRED
One or more neurological symptoms: weakness/paralysis, tremor, dystonic posturing, myoclonus, gait disorder, sensory loss/altered sensation, vision/hearing loss, speech/swallowing, non-epileptic seizures, dissociative episodes, or cognitive symptoms
FND symptoms span the full range of the nervous system. Criterion A is generally met by clinical presentation. Symptoms must affect voluntary motor or sensory function — this includes movement (tremor, weakness, dystonia, myoclonus, jerks, gait), sensation (numbness, pain, altered touch), special senses (functional blindness, diplopia, hearing loss), speech (functional dysphonia, dysarthria, foreign accent syndrome), swallowing, or episodic/paroxysmal symptoms (functional seizures, episodes of unresponsiveness).

Important: Multiple symptom types commonly coexist. Functional cognitive symptoms (memory complaints, attention) may also form part of the FND spectrum (functional cognitive disorder, FCD), though this remains an evolving area distinct from dementia.
Criterion B — Positive clinical evidence of incompatibility REQUIRED — CORE OF MODERN FND DIAGNOSIS
Positive signs demonstrating internal inconsistency (symptom varies based on attention/distraction) OR incongruence with known neurological disease patterns
Internal inconsistency: The symptom improves, disappears, or changes character when the patient’s attention is directed away from it. Examples: Hoover sign (hip extension normal when tested indirectly via contralateral hip flexion); tremor that entrains, pauses, or suppresses with contralateral rhythmic movement; weakness that is “give-way” (collapses suddenly) but not smooth/sustained as in UMN/LMN lesions; gait that improves when walking backwards or running despite severe apparent impairment walking forwards.

Incongruence: The pattern of symptoms does not fit any known neurological disease. Examples: sensory loss that splits exactly at the midline (anatomically impossible given shared nerve supply); “tubular” visual field constriction that remains the same size regardless of viewing distance (impossible in genuine visual field loss); tremor that is present in all postures equally without the characteristic postural/rest/kinetic pattern of organic tremors.

This criterion is now the diagnostic cornerstone (Aybek & Perez BMJ 2022): a single, clear positive sign in the right context is sufficient. See Step 3 for the full positive signs checklist.
Criterion C — Not better explained by another disorder REQUIRED
Symptoms are not fully accounted for by another medical or mental disorder. Note: FND CAN coexist with genuine neurological disease — Criterion C does not require excluding all other conditions
Criterion C is NOT an exclusion criterion in the historical “diagnosis of exclusion” sense. Rather, it asks whether the specific FND symptoms can be fully explained by another condition. A patient may have both MS and FND-superimposed symptoms — if positive FND signs are present and the symptoms are not fully explicable by MS lesion location/burden, Criterion C can be met.

Conditions to consider excluding before confirming FND: metabolic encephalopathy (thyroid, glucose, calcium, B12), autoimmune encephalitis (NMDAR, LGI1, CASPR2 — especially in young patients with new psychiatric symptoms + movement disorder), CNS vasculitis, complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS), Wilson’s disease (young onset dyskinesia), Huntington’s disease (early chorea), cervical/lumbar myelopathy/radiculopathy.
Autoimmune encephalitis: Anti-NMDAR encephalitis in particular can closely mimic FND (and FND can be misdiagnosed as autoimmune encephalitis). Key differentiators: NMDAR encephalitis typically has progressive course, altered consciousness, MRI/CSF/EEG abnormalities, and responds to immunotherapy. A careful history and appropriate antibody testing (serum and CSF) should precede an FND diagnosis in atypical presentations, particularly young women with rapid onset psychiatric/movement symptoms.
Criterion D — Clinically significant distress or impairment REQUIRED
Symptoms cause significant distress, functional impairment (work, social, ADLs), or prompt medical evaluation
Step 3 · Positive Sign Examination
Positive signs are bedside examination findings that demonstrate internal inconsistency (symptom changes with attention/distraction) or anatomical/physiological incongruence. A single unequivocal positive sign in the appropriate clinical context is sufficient to meet Criterion B. Signs should be demonstrated to the patient as part of the diagnostic communication — this is both diagnostically and therapeutically valuable.
Evidence quality for positive signs: HIGH Sn/Sp = well-validated (sensitivity >80%, specificity >90%)   MODERATE = useful but less validated   EMERGING = clinically useful, limited formal data

Key principle: No single sign is 100% specific. Clinical confidence comes from the combination of positive signs, clinical context, and symptom pattern incongruence. Signs should be reproducible, and ideally confirmed by a second examiner in diagnostically challenging cases.
Motor weakness signs FOR FUNCTIONAL WEAKNESS SUBTYPE
Hoover sign Sn 63%, Sp 100%
Hip extension weak voluntarily but normal when tested indirectly via contralateral hip flexion against resistance
Technique: Patient supine. Examiner places one hand under the heel of the “weak” leg. Ask patient to lift the unaffected leg against resistance applied by examiner’s other hand. A positive sign = the examiner feels downward pressure from the weak leg (involuntary hip extension accompanying synergistic contralateral flexion). Now ask patient to lift the weak leg — the pressure is absent (no effort transmitted).

Evidence: McWhirter L et al. J Psychosom Res 2011: Sensitivity 63% (95% CI 24–91%), specificity 100% (95% CI 97–100%) in 337 patients presenting with suspected stroke. High specificity confirms functional aetiology when positive. Moderate sensitivity means a negative Hoover does not exclude FND.
Pitfalls: False positive if hip pain on the affected side limits voluntary extension effort. Apraxia (especially post-stroke) can cause failure of voluntary hip extension with preserved synergistic extension. Always interpret in the clinical context.
Hip abductor sign (Sonoo sign) HIGH Sp
Involuntary hip abduction of the “weak” leg during testing of the unaffected leg
Technique (Sonoo M. J Neurol Neurosurg Psychiatry 2004): Patient supine, examiner resists hip abduction of both legs simultaneously. In functional leg weakness: the apparently weak leg abducts strongly when the unaffected leg is being tested against resistance (involuntary synergistic contraction), but abduction of the weak leg against direct resistance fails. This mirrors the same principle as the Hoover sign but applied to abductor muscles.

Abductor finger sign (Tinazzi M. Mov Disord 2008): Analogous principle applied to the hand. Finger abduction on the affected side improves when the unaffected hand is tested against resistance.
Give-way / collapsing weakness MODERATE Sp
Sudden give-way of resistance during strength testing, unlike smooth UMN or LMN weakness
In genuine UMN or LMN weakness, the limb gives way smoothly and is graded consistently. In functional weakness, there is a characteristic “give-way” or “collapse” pattern — the limb starts with normal or near-normal strength then suddenly gives way completely. This may also vary between repeated tests (motor inconsistency). Important caveat: Give-way weakness is also seen in pain-limited effort. It is not specific for FND unless combined with other positive signs. Always consider pain (CRPS, radiculopathy) as an alternative.
Motor inconsistency: Strength that varies markedly between repeated measurements within the same examination — e.g., MRC 0/5 one moment and 4/5 the next for the same movement when the patient is distracted — is highly suggestive of functional weakness.
Drift without pronation HIGH Sp
Arm drifts downward without the pronation seen in genuine pyramidal (UMN) weakness
Technique (Daum & Aybek, validated 2013): Patient holds both arms extended in front with palms up and eyes closed. In genuine UMN (pyramidal) weakness, the weak arm drifts downward AND pronates (palm rotates downward) — this is the classic “pronator drift.” In functional arm weakness, the arm may drift downward (or the whole hand may drift sideways as a unit) but WITHOUT the characteristic pronation. This dissociation is highly specific for functional weakness.
Tremor signs FOR FUNCTIONAL TREMOR SUBTYPE
Tremor entrainment test HIGH Sp
Tremor changes frequency, entrains to, or stops during contralateral rhythmic movement
Technique: Ask patient to copy a rhythmic movement with the unaffected hand (e.g., tap at a specific cadence set by the examiner). Observe the tremulous limb. Positive result: the tremor changes frequency to match the tapping rhythm (entrainment), briefly pauses/suppresses, or becomes irregular. Organic tremors (ET, PD) maintain their characteristic frequency independent of contralateral rhythmic tasks.

Electrophysiology (Schwingenschuh P et al. Mov Disord 2011): EMG/accelerometry battery validated for functional tremor: sensitivity 89.5%, specificity 95.9%. Positive features include: tonic coactivation before tremor burst, coherence between bilateral arm tremors (both arms trembling at identical frequency), tremor frequency shift with contralateral tapping.
Longstanding functional tremor resistance: Patients with chronic functional tremor (>2 years) may have developed automatised patterns resistant to simple distraction. More complex or unexpected tasks may be needed (mental arithmetic, serial subtractions, cranial nerve testing). Resistance to easy distraction does not exclude FND.
Tremor distractibility MODERATE Sp
Tremor amplitude decreases or stops when patient is engaged in complex mental tasks
Ask the patient to perform serial subtractions (100 − 7, etc.), name months backwards, or describe a complex memory. Positive result: tremor diminishes during the demanding cognitive task and returns when the task is completed. Caution: Tremor variability is not unique to FND (psychogenic tremor is not the only cause of variable tremor) — this sign is best combined with entrainment and other positive signs.
Gait signs FOR FUNCTIONAL GAIT SUBTYPE
Dragging monoplegic gait MODERATE Sp
Leg dragged as a whole unit, without circumduction or typical hemiplegic posture
Genuine hemiparesis typically produces circumduction (swinging the leg out laterally to clear the floor) and the classic hemiplegic posture (arm flexed, leg extended). Functional dragging gait pulls the leg behind as a limp unit without circumduction, often with the hip externally rotated in an exaggerated fashion. The “ploughing” appearance differs from UMN gait.
Gait improvement with backwards walking, running, or cycling HIGH Sp
Bypasses overlearned voluntary gait pattern; automatic movement circuits preserved
Functional gait disorder often preferentially impairs forwards walking while preserving backwards walking, running, and cycling. This paradox reflects that automatic (subcortical) motor circuits are functionally intact but the voluntary (cortical) pathway is dysfunctional. Demonstrating this discrepancy to the patient is both diagnostically informative and therapeutically useful (physiotherapists use this as a starting point for gait rehabilitation).
Sensory signs FOR FUNCTIONAL SENSORY SUBTYPE
Midline splitting sensory loss HIGH Sp
Sensory loss that splits exactly at the midline of the face, trunk, or genitalia
Anatomically, bilateral nerve supply overlaps at the midline by several millimetres (e.g., bilateral trigeminal territory overlaps at the philtrum of the lip). A sensory deficit that abruptly and sharply splits at the midline — especially when the patient can identify the exact midline with pinpoint accuracy — is anatomically incongruent with any known structural lesion. This is one of the most specific sensory signs for FND.
Non-anatomical sensory loss / inconsistent pattern MODERATE Sp
Distribution does not correspond to any nerve root, peripheral nerve, or CNS pathway
Sensory loss that changes between examinations, shifts in location when patient is unaware they are being retested, or follows a “glove-and-stocking” pattern without nerve length dependency — these are signs of incongruence. Important caveat: sensory testing is subjective and variable, so this sign should be confirmed multiple times and combined with other positive signs before anchoring the diagnosis.
Functional seizure signs FOR FUNCTIONAL SEIZURE (PNES) SUBTYPE
Eyes closed during episodes Sp ~98%
Eyes are typically forced closed during functional seizures; open during true epileptic seizures
Clinical observation (Chung SS et al. Seizure 2006): During generalised tonic-clonic seizures and most focal seizures with LOC, the eyes are open (or deviated). During functional seizures, eyes are characteristically closed — often forcefully closed with active resistance to opening. This sign has high specificity (~98%) for functional vs. epileptic seizures. Note: some types of absence seizure may have eyes open but blank stare.

Ictal semiology features favouring functional seizures: Eyes closed, asynchronous thrashing movements of the limbs (not the synchronised tonic-clonic of epilepsy), pelvic thrusting, side-to-side head movement, episodes longer than 2 minutes without post-ictal confusion, waxing/waning course, preserved consciousness despite bilateral motor activity, normal post-ictal EEG.
Gold standard for functional seizures: Video-EEG (vEEG). Diagnostic certainty levels (Reuber M & Elger CE 2003): Possible = clinical history only; Probable = witnessed episode without vEEG; Clinically established = witnessed semiology by experienced clinician; Documented = vEEG capturing a habitual event with no epileptiform correlate. Gold standard = Documented. Discontinuation of AEDs after documented vEEG diagnosis is appropriate and improves outcomes.
Video-EEG (vEEG) capture — habitual episode recorded with no epileptiform correlate GOLD STANDARD
vEEG is the gold standard for diagnosing functional seizures. A habitual seizure captured on simultaneous video and EEG, showing clinical ictal semiology (or semiology consistent with patient’s reported events) without epileptiform EEG activity, establishes the “documented” level of diagnostic certainty. Important: EEG artefact during motor activity can obscure the trace — post-ictal EEG should also be reviewed for suppression (epilepsy) vs. normal alpha return (functional). Prolonged vEEG monitoring may be required if events are infrequent.
Step 4 · Red Flags & Diagnostic Pitfalls
FND is a diagnosis of inclusion based on positive signs, but diagnostic errors occur in both directions: over-diagnosis (attributing genuine neurological disease to FND) and under-diagnosis (labelling FND as “medically unexplained” without identifying positive signs). Careful assessment of these red flags is essential before confirming FND.
Misdiagnosis rate (Stone J et al. J Neurol Neurosurg Psychiatry 2005): In a systematic review of 1,466 patients diagnosed with “hysteria”/conversion disorder, 4% were subsequently found to have an alternative neurological diagnosis after long-term follow-up. This is comparable to the misdiagnosis rate for MS and epilepsy, confirming that positive-sign-based FND diagnosis has acceptable diagnostic stability. However, this figure does not mean red flags can be ignored — it means the positive-sign approach itself is validated when applied correctly.
Structural / organic red flags ANY POSITIVE = REASSESS DIAGNOSIS
Progressive, unrelenting course without any fluctuation — FND typically fluctuates; relentlessly progressive symptoms are atypical
MRI brain/spine abnormality that could explain the symptoms — structural lesion at appropriate location
MRI is typically normal in FND. An abnormal MRI does NOT automatically exclude FND (the lesion may be incidental), but does require careful correlation: does the lesion’s location, size, and expected functional deficit match the patient’s symptoms? If a thalamic stroke explains sensory loss in the same distribution as the patient’s symptoms, FND may not be the primary diagnosis. If an incidental white matter lesion is found in a patient with functional seizures and classic PNES semiology, vEEG should confirm before labelling as MS-related.
Onset >50 years with de novo movement disorder — new onset dystonia, tremor, or parkinsonism after age 50 has higher prior probability of structural/degenerative cause
Autoimmune encephalitis features — rapid psychiatric change, movement disorder, autonomic instability, altered consciousness, young female; check CSF and antibody panel
Anti-NMDAR encephalitis is the most commonly misdiagnosed condition as FND (and vice versa). Features suggestive of autoimmune encephalitis over FND: subacute onset (<3 months) with behavioural/psychiatric change; movement disorder (orofacial dyskinesia, chorea, or catatonia); autonomic instability (tachycardia, hyperthermia); reduced consciousness or seizures; CSF pleocytosis or elevated protein; MRI abnormalities (T2/FLAIR signal in limbic regions); response to immunotherapy. Check serum and CSF antibodies (NMDAR, LGI1, CASPR2, AMPAR, GABA-B) in atypical presentations.
Epileptiform activity on routine EEG or vEEG — interictal spikes, sharp waves, or ictal correlate during captured episode
Symptoms fully explicable by identified structural disease — lesion location and deficit match perfectly; no positive FND signs demonstrable
Step 5 · Comorbidities, Predisposing Factors & Context
The biopsychosocial model is central to FND understanding. Comorbidities influence prognosis, treatment selection, and diagnostic confidence. They do not need to be present for FND diagnosis (DSM-5-TR removed the psychological stressor requirement), but should be documented as they inform the multidisciplinary treatment plan.
Biopsychosocial framework (Perez DL et al. CNS Spectr 2021; Aybek & Perez BMJ 2022):
Predisposing: Prior neurological disease, adverse childhood experiences, comorbid psychiatric disorder, alexithymia, prior somatoform symptoms
Precipitating: Physical injury or illness (~37%), psychological trauma/acute stress, medical procedure, iatrogenic exposure
Perpetuating: Symptom-focused attention, avoidance behaviours, kinesiophobia, medically unexplained framing, social reinforcement, secondary gain (does not imply malingering)
Psychiatric comorbidities IMPORTANT FOR TREATMENT PLANNING
Major depressive disorder or persistent depressive disorder — common in FND (~30–50%); treat independently; does not explain FND symptoms
Anxiety disorder, panic disorder, or health anxiety — present in ~40% of FND; catastrophising and symptom monitoring perpetuate FND
PTSD or trauma history — present in ~30–40% of FND; trauma-focused CBT / EMDR indicated if present
Chronic pain, fibromyalgia, or functional somatic syndrome — frequently coexists; multidisciplinary pain management often needed
Contextual & perpetuating factors INFORMS TREATMENT APPROACH
Ongoing diagnostic uncertainty — patient still searching for a “real” diagnosis; has not accepted FND explanation; multiple specialist opinions
Ongoing diagnostic uncertainty is one of the strongest perpetuating factors in FND. Patients who have not accepted the diagnosis — or whose family has not — are unlikely to engage with physiotherapy or CBT. Clear, compassionate, and confident explanation of the FND diagnosis at the point of diagnosis is associated with better outcomes. The explanation should: (1) name the problem clearly; (2) validate that symptoms are real; (3) explain the mechanism (software vs. hardware); (4) emphasise reversibility; (5) demonstrate positive signs to the patient.
Avoidance behaviours / kinesiophobia — avoiding activities due to fear of worsening; using wheelchair/aids beyond functional need
Medico-legal claim / disability benefits in progress — does not imply malingering but is associated with poorer prognosis
Adequate social support — supportive family/network is a positive prognostic factor; social isolation worsens prognosis

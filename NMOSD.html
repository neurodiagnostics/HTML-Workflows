Step 1 · AQP4-IgG Serologic Status
Diagnosis requires at least 1 discrete clinical attack of CNS symptoms. Asymptomatic seropositivity alone — even with compatible MRI — is not sufficient. AQP4-IgG stratification determines downstream criteria stringency. Cell-based assay (CBA) is strongly recommended.
Assay performance (Wingerchuk 2015 IPND):
Cell-based assay (CBA): Sensitivity ~76.7%, false-positive rate ~0.1% — recommended method
IIF / ELISA: Lower sensitivity (63–64%), higher false-positive rate (0.5–1.3%) — low-titer ELISA positives require CBA confirmation
Retest indication: Seronegative patients who relapse; before B-cell / antibody-targeted therapy; if initially tested by non-CBA method
What is the patient’s AQP4-IgG (aquaporin-4 IgG) serostatus by best available assay?
AQP4 is the water channel protein expressed predominantly on CNS astrocyte end-feet at the blood-brain barrier. AQP4-IgG (anti-aquaporin-4 antibody) is the specific autoantibody that causes NMOSD in the majority of patients by activating complement and NK cell-mediated astrocyte destruction. Seropositivity is diagnostic when any core feature is present.

Pathophysiology: AQP4-IgG binds to AQP4 on astrocyte end-feet → complement activation (C3d, C5b-9 membrane attack complex) → astrocyte death → secondary oligodendrocyte and axon injury. This explains the preference for areas of high AQP4 expression: spinal cord central canal, optic nerves, area postrema, diencephalon, corpus callosum periventricular surfaces.

Seronegative NMOSD: ~20–25% are AQP4-IgG negative on best available assay. A subset (~10% of seronegative) are MOG-IgG positive — distinct pathophysiology, younger onset, less female predominance, different relapse risk and treatment response. MOG-IgG testing should be considered in seronegative patients.
Titre matters: High AQP4-IgG titre is associated with more severe attacks and higher relapse rate. Low-titre CBA positives are still diagnostically significant. Titres do not reliably track disease activity or predict relapse timing.

Additional clinical notes & CSF guidance
• No single characteristic is pathognomonic — clinical judgment essential
• Altitudinal visual field defects may also result from ischaemic optic neuropathy
• Bilateral simultaneous optic neuritis can occur in MS (less common)
• Gradually progressive course (vs. attack-based) occurs in only 1–2% of NMOSD — a red flag if present
• “Dissemination in space” for seronegative criteria = ≥2 different core features from ≥2 distinct neuroanatomic regions

CSF supportive of NMOSD over MS:
• Pleocytosis >50 WBC/μL (~35% of NMOSD attacks)
• Neutrophils >5/μL (~44%) or eosinophils >5/μL (~10%)
• Elevated CSF GFAP (peaks days–weeks post-attack; reflects astrocyte injury)
• Absence of oligoclonal bands (OCBs present in <20% NMOSD vs >80% MS)

MRI characteristics reference & MS-atypical red flags
LETM lesion characteristics:
• T2 hyperintense ≥3 contiguous vertebral segments (sagittal)
• Central gray matter predominance (>70% of lesion axially)
• May show T1 hypointensity and gadolinium enhancement
• Cervical lesions may extend rostrally into brainstem
• Cord swelling in acute phase; longitudinally extensive atrophy chronically

Optic nerve MRI (NMOSD-typical):
• Fat-suppressed T2/STIR with gadolinium T1 enhancement
• Posterior predominance, >½ nerve length, or chiasmal involvement

Area postrema / brainstem:
• Dorsal medulla lesions: small, often bilateral, or contiguous with cervical cord
• Periependymal fourth ventricular, pontine, and dorsal midbrain lesions

Brain MRI red flags (MS-typical, not NMOSD):
• Dawson’s fingers (perpendicular periventricular lesions on sagittal T2/FLAIR)
• Inferior temporal lobe periventricular lesions
• Cortical / juxtacortical lesions
• Persistent enhancement >3 months

Supportive features that increase NMOSD confidence
CSF supportive of NMOSD (vs. MS):
• Absence of oligoclonal bands (OCBs present in <20% NMOSD vs >80% MS)
• Pleocytosis >50 WBC/μL (~35% incidence in NMOSD)
• Neutrophils >5/μL (~44%) or eosinophils >5/μL (~10%)
• Elevated CSF GFAP (peaks days–weeks post-attack)

Systemic autoimmunity (strengthens NMOSD confidence):
• Coexisting SLE or Sjögren syndrome — makes NMOSD (not CNS vasculitis) the more likely cause
• Myasthenia gravis or detectable serum AChR antibodies

Seronegative NMOSD considerations:
• Minority have detectable MOG-IgG (younger, less female-predominant, lower relapse risk, possibly distinct pathogenesis and treatment response)
• Consider retesting: antibody titres increase with relapse, decrease with immunosuppression

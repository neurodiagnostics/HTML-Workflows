Step 1 · Patient Age and Presenting Blood Pressure
Patient age and initial BP at stroke presentation. Age is one of the strongest modifiers of pre-test probability for specific etiologies — it shifts the differential significantly, particularly for uncommon causes like APLS, PFO-related paradoxical embolism, and genetic arteriopathies.
Clinical relevance of presenting BP:
Markedly elevated BP (>220/110): Supports hypertensive small vessel disease (lipohyalinosis, arteriolosclerosis) particularly with perforator-territory infarcts (basal ganglia, pons, internal capsule, thalamus). The vascular centrencephalon transmits systemic pressure with minimal dampening — lacunar infarction in these territories is the direct result of hypertensive arteriolar injury.

Moderately elevated BP (180–220): Hypertension is the dominant modifiable risk factor for both ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke; consistent with SVD pathophysiology but non-specific.

Normal or mildly elevated BP: Does not support primary SVD; other etiologies (CE, large vessel disease) more likely if other features absent.
Patient age REQUIRED — modifies etiology probability
Patient age at time of stroke
Age is one of the most powerful pre-test probability modifiers in stroke etiology classification. In young patients (<50y), the prior probability of cardioembolic, large vessel atherosclerotic, and SVD etiologies is substantially lower — uncommon causes (dissection, APLS, PFO-paradoxical embolism, genetic arteriopathy, vasculitis, hypercoagulable state) account for a disproportionately large share. Atrial fibrillation as the underlying mechanism is also less common in young patients, though not impossible.

In older patients (≥65y), atrial fibrillation, large vessel atherosclerosis, and small vessel disease together account for the majority of strokes. Cryptogenic stroke in this group should prompt especially aggressive prolonged cardiac monitoring — paroxysmal AF detection rates increase substantially with longer monitoring duration (CRYSTAL-AF, Sanna T et al. NEJM 2014: 8.9% AF detected at 6 months and 12.4% at 12 months with ICM vs. 2% with standard monitoring).

The 45–65y range is transitional — all mechanisms are common, and the clinical picture must guide the workup. SVD from hypertension becomes increasingly prevalent in this group.
Age <50y with cryptogenic stroke: dissection is the single most common etiology (consider CTA/MRA neck + vessel wall MRI). APLS: typically diagnosed <50y, often with prior thrombotic events. PFO-paradoxical embolism: RoPE score strongly favors attribution in younger patients (age <45y, no vascular risk factors).
Initial Systolic BP REQUIRED
Initial SBP
Acute ischemic stroke frequently presents with reactive hypertension from catecholamine surge, Cushing reflex, and pain/anxiety — not necessarily reflecting chronic hypertensive burden. However, severely elevated BP (>220 mmHg) at presentation, especially in the absence of a known cardioembolic or large vessel atherosclerotic source, strongly supports hypertensive small vessel disease as the mechanism. BP should not be treated unless SBP >220 or DBP >110 mmHg, unless the patient is a thrombolysis candidate (treatment threshold >185/110).
Ref: AHA/ASA Acute Ischemic Stroke Guidelines. Owens WB. Blood pressure control in acute cerebrovascular disease. J Clin Hypertens 2011;13(3):205–211. StatPearls — Lacunar Stroke (NBK563216). Management threshold: permissive hypertension until SBP >220 mmHg or DBP >110 mmHg; lower by 15% in first 24h if exceeded.

Step 2 · Infarct Size and Number
DWI-MRI characteristics of the acute infarct(s). Size and multiplicity are critical for TOAST classification — they directly inform whether SVD or an embolic mechanism is more likely.

⚠ If acute stroke intervention was performed (thrombectomy or IV tPA with successful recanalization): base your answers on the estimated infarct size and territory if intervention had not improved outcome — i.e., the territory at risk based on pre-intervention DWI core, CT perfusion maps, or the territory of the occluded vessel. A small residual DWI infarct after successful M1 thrombectomy should be classified by the M1 territory, not the residual infarct size.
TOAST size criterion: The traditional lacunar size cutoff is <15 mm diameter (Adams 1993). Some authors use <20 mm; classical Fisher lacunes are <15 mm. Infarcts ≥15 mm are inconsistent with pure lipohyalinosis and may require explanation beyond SVD.

Multiplicity: Multiple infarcts in different vascular territories — particularly bilateral or anterior + posterior — carry OR 2–3× for cardioembolic source vs. large vessel disease (LVD). Multiple small lesions in a single territory suggest proximal artery-to-artery embolism. Multiple DWI lesions in clinically lacunar syndromes are associated with an embolic source in up to 27% of cases (Kang DW et al., Stroke 2003;34:2599).

Important caveat on size cutoffs: Traditional lacunar size thresholds (<15 mm, <20 mm) were established before modern DWI and before the current understanding of embolic mechanisms. These should not be applied rigidly:
• A lesion >20 mm in the basal ganglia or internal capsule may suggest embolism or large-vessel disease over pure lipohyalinosis — but size alone is insufficient.
• Emboli can produce very small lesions, including punctate cortical and juxtacortical infarcts (<5 mm) in embolic shower patterns.
• Location matters as much as size: A tiny juxtacortical or cortical infarct is more consistent with embolism than lacunar SVD; a 10 mm deep white matter lesion in a hypertensive patient is far more likely SVD than a 10 mm cortical lesion in a young patient with no vascular risk factors.
• Multiple lesions in different vascular territories, regardless of individual lesion size, argue strongly against SVD. (Ay H et al. Stroke 2005;36:1818)

Maximum single infarct diameter on DWI-MRI
Punctate (<5 mm): Tiny lesions often seen as part of an embolic shower — multiple small cortical or subcortical DWI foci scattered across one or more vascular territories. Punctate infarcts in multiple different territories are characteristic of a proximal embolic source (cardioembolic or hypercoagulable state) and should NOT be equated with SVD lacunes, even though each individual lesion is small. Select "Punctate" if the infarcts are tiny (<5 mm) and multiple; select "<15 mm" only for a single or few lacunar-sized infarcts suggestive of perforator disease.

15 mm threshold: Distinguishes classical lacunar infarcts (perforator occlusion from SVD) from territorial infarcts. Infarcts 15–20 mm are a grey zone. TOAST criteria specify <15 mm. Infarcts >15 mm in perforator territory may represent branch atheromatous disease (BAD).
Ref: Adams HP et al. Stroke. 1993;24:35–41. Lacune definition: <15 mm; Fisher CM original series: 0.5–15 mm diameter. Punctate embolic shower: Kang DW et al. Stroke 2003;34:2599 — multiple small DWI lesions strongly associated with embolic source (CE or hypercoagulable) rather than SVD.
Number and distribution of acute DWI lesions REQUIRED
Number and territorial distribution of acute DWI infarcts
DWI multiplicity is one of the most diagnostically useful imaging features. Multiple infarcts in different vascular territories are a strong signal for a central cardiac or proximal aortic embolic source. Multiple infarcts in the same territory with a cortical pattern suggest artery-to-artery embolism from a proximal stenosis or unstable plaque. Bilateral anterior circulation infarcts or anterior + posterior combination argue strongly for cardioembolism over large vessel disease (LVD).
Ref: Yaghi S et al. Patterns of infarction on MRI in patients with acute ischemic stroke and cardio-embolism: systematic review. Front Neurol. 2020. Kang DW et al. Stroke. 2003;34:2599–2602 — embolic source in 27% with multiple DWI lesions vs. 6% with single lacunar lesion.
Step 3 · Vascular Territories
Location of the infarct on DWI. Topography is among the strongest imaging predictors of stroke mechanism — cortical location, watershed pattern, and multiterritory scatter each carry specific etiologic implications.
Evidence: Systematic review (Yaghi S et al. Front Neurol 2020) — cortical infarcts are 2× more frequent in cardioembolic stroke. Lacunar infarcts (OR 0.49, 95% CI 0.31–0.80) are significantly less likely with a cardioembolic source.

Watershed/border-zone: The radiologic hallmark of hemodynamic ischemia from high-grade carotid stenosis — linear subcortical or cortical "beading" in the ACA/MCA or MCA/PCA border zones.
Strictly perforator territory (striatocapsular, pons, thalamus): Classic SVD signature when <15 mm and isolated. Branch atheromatous disease (BAD) produces similar topography but larger infarcts.
Bilateral cortical scatter: Argues strongly for central cardiac embolism over any unilateral atherosclerotic source.

ACA/MCA cortical border-zone — infarcts at the parasagittal strip between the ACA and MCA cortical territories. Seen with:
• Severe ipsilateral ICA stenosis or occlusion (unilateral)
• Systemic hypotension or cardiac arrest (bilateral symmetric)
DWI: linear gyral lesions along the vertex, parasagittal. Classically affects the medial face/arm representation (between the ACA leg area and MCA arm area). Not embolic — emboli tend to produce cortical territorial lesions, not border-zone strips.
PCA/MCA cortical border-zone — infarcts at the posterior junction between the MCA and PCA territories in the parieto-occipital region.

• Unilateral: ipsilateral ICA or PCA stenosis
• Bilateral: global hypoperfusion
DWI: gyral lesions at the posterior convexity, parieto-occipital junction. May produce visual field deficits or Balint syndrome if bilateral.
Internal (subcortical) watershed — linear "string of beads" lesions in the centrum semiovale or corona radiata, between the deep MCA perforators and the superficial MCA territory.

• Most specific marker of hemodynamic ischemia from ipsilateral high-grade ICA stenosis or ICAD
• DWI: small punctate lesions arranged linearly in the deep white matter
• Bilateral: cardiac arrest, severe hypotension
• Distinct from embolic deep WM lesions which are more random in distribution. (Caplan LR, Hennerici M. Arch Neurol 1998)
Fetal posterior cerebral artery (fetal PCA) RELEVANT WHEN POSTERIOR / BOTH CIRCULATIONS INVOLVED
Is there an ipsilateral fetal-type PCA (fetal PCA / fetal PComA) on CTA or MRA — ipsilateral to the side with carotid or anterior circulation disease?
What is a fetal PCA? In a fetal-type posterior cerebral artery (FPCA / fetal PComA), the distal PCA territory is supplied predominantly by the internal carotid artery (ICA) via a large posterior communicating artery, rather than from the basilar artery. The P1 segment of the PCA is hypoplastic or absent. Prevalence: 20–30% of the general population (range 11–46% across studies); can be unilateral or bilateral. Identified on CTA/MRA as a large PComA (≥1 mm, often ≥1.5 mm) with a hypoplastic P1 segment.

Why it matters for stroke etiology: In the presence of an ipsilateral fetal PCA, the PCA territory is perfused by the ICA — meaning carotid artery disease (stenosis, unstable plaque, carotid web) can cause ipsilateral PCA territory infarction in addition to anterior circulation infarction. This is the pathophysiologic basis of "carotid-fetal-posterior (CFP) syndrome" — PCA territory stroke caused by ipsilateral carotid disease via the fetal PComA.

Clinical implications:
  • Anterior + ipsilateral PCA infarction WITH fetal PCA: Can be entirely explained by a single ipsilateral carotid source — does NOT require a central (cardiac) embolic source. Changes apparent "multi-territory" infarct to effectively a single carotid-territory event.
  • Anterior + posterior infarction WITHOUT fetal PCA: A single ipsilateral carotid source cannot explain the posterior circulation infarct — strongly favours a central cardioembolic source, aortic embolism, or separate posterior circulation disease.
  • Bilateral posterior infarcts: Cannot be explained by fetal PCA regardless of its presence — requires a central embolic source or bilateral posterior circulation disease.
  • CFP syndrome prevalence: Among patients with ≥50% carotid stenosis, ~9% who have ipsilateral fetal PCA and posterior circulation symptoms fulfil criteria for CFP syndrome (Nedeltchev 2024, PMC10733935).

Bilateral Carotid / Anterior Circulation Disease Watershed / Multi-territory — Hemodynamic vs. Embolic
Clinical distinction: Bilateral watershed or bilateral cortical + subcortical infarcts can arise from two distinct mechanisms that require very different workup and management:

• Hemodynamic collapse (bilateral severe carotid stenosis / occlusion): Flow failure in the distal perfusion zones of both ACAs and MCAs → bilateral symmetric watershed infarcts. NOT cardioembolic. Key features: bilateral symmetric infarction, severe bilateral ICA disease on CTA/MRA, often perioperative or hypovolemic trigger.
• Cardioembolism (shower embolism): Multiple infarcts in different arterial territories simultaneously → classic CE pattern. Key features: different ages/stages of infarcts, bilateral but asymmetric, cortical branches affected, no hemodynamic trigger.

Ref: Caplan LR & Hennerici M, Arch Neurol 1998 — hemodynamic vs embolic stroke mechanisms. NASCET Collaborators, NEJM 1991 — bilateral carotid stenosis outcomes.
Bilateral significant carotid stenosis / occlusion on CTA or MRA?
Bilateral severe (≥70%) ICA stenosis or one occlusion + contralateral severe stenosis is a rare but important cause of bilateral watershed infarction via hemodynamic failure. This is classified as large vessel disease (bilateral), NOT cardioembolism, despite the bilateral territory involvement. Note: unilateral carotid stenosis with compensatory contralateral flow failure can also cause ipsilateral watershed infarcts without cardiac source.

Degree of ICA stenosis — ipsilateral extracranial ICA (NASCET method)
NASCET risk data:
Symptomatic ≥70% stenosis: 26% 2-year ipsilateral stroke risk on medical therapy (NASCET trial). Absolute risk reduction from CEA = 16% at 5 years (NNT ~6). CEA benefit requires surgical risk <6% perioperative mortality/stroke.
Symptomatic 50–69%: Absolute benefit from CEA ~5% at 5 years (NNT ~20) — marginal; not universally recommended.
Asymptomatic 70–99%: Modern pooled annual ipsilateral stroke rate = 0.88% (95% CI 0.62–1.02) after 2010 with intensive medical therapy (Howard DPJ et al., Lancet Neurol 2021). 70–99% carries 2–3× higher risk than 50–69% in all decades studied (OR 2.1, 95% CI 1.7–2.5).

Key principle: Symptomatic stenosis is defined as prior ipsilateral TIA or stroke — this is the current presentation, so the question is whether the stenosis is ipsilateral and plausibly causal.
NASCET criteria are specific to extracranial ICA stenosis. The residual lumen is divided by the normal ICA diameter distal to the bulb. A 70% NASCET stenosis ≈ 82% ECST stenosis. For symptomatic carotid disease, ACC/AHA guidelines recommend CEA when surgical risk <6% and stenosis ≥50% (Class I for 70–99%). Symptomatic stenosis = prior ipsilateral TIA or stroke (this current presentation).

Carotid web
What it is: Carotid web (CaW) is an intimal variant of fibromuscular dysplasia — a thin, shelf-like protrusion from the posterior wall of the ICA bulb that causes flow disruption, blood stasis, and mural thrombus formation, leading to ipsilateral artery-to-artery thromboembolism. It does NOT cause flow-limiting stenosis (<50% by NASCET in virtually all cases). It is a non-atherosclerotic lesion.

Who to suspect it in: Young patient (<60y), female sex, Black or Afro-Caribbean ancestry (disproportionately affected), ESUS or cryptogenic ipsilateral anterior circulation stroke, absence of conventional vascular risk factors. Prevalence ~9.4% in cryptogenic stroke (Coutinho 2019) and 2.5% in large vessel occlusion (MR-CLEAN post-hoc, Compagne 2018).

How to identify it: CTA of the neck (thin-slice, multiplanar reconstruction) is the most reliable modality — look for a shelf-like filling defect projecting posteriorly into the ICA bulb lumen. DSA is the gold standard. May be missed on MRA or if CTA is not specifically reviewed for it. Can be bilateral in 58% of symptomatic cases (Sajedi 2019).

Stroke risk data:
  • Prevalence in ESUS ipsilateral to large intracranial artery occlusion is higher than in patients with an identified embolic source (Coutinho 2019, ESO).
  • Recurrence rate on medical therapy alone: 6.9%/year; cumulative 10.8% at 1 year, 19.8% at 2 years, 27.3% at 5 years (Joux 2019, Stroke). This is substantially higher than ESUS trials (3–5%/year).
  • Recurrent stroke adjusted RR: 4.38 (95% CI 1.38–13.85) vs. patients without carotid web undergoing thrombectomy (Takasugi 2024, J Neurol).
  • In high-risk angioarchitecture (≥2 high-risk web angles): OR 5.91 (95% CI 4.25–8.24) per additional high-risk angle for stroke (preprint 2024).
  • 29% of symptomatic webs have superimposed thrombus (Sajedi 2019).
  • CEA or carotid stenting appear effective — 0 recurrences in 23 surgically/stented patients over median 39.8 months (Joux 2019). ESVS 2023 guidelines recommend CEA or CAS for symptomatic carotid web when no other stroke cause is identified after detailed workup.
Key refs: Joux J et al. Stroke 2019;50:3447 — recurrence 6.9%/year, surgery effective. Sajedi SA et al. Stroke 2019;50:3143 — bilateral in 58%, thrombus in 29%. Compagne KCJ et al. Stroke 2018 — 2.5% in MR-CLEAN LVO. Coutinho JM et al. Int J Stroke 2019 — 9.4% in cryptogenic stroke. Takasugi J et al. J Neurol 2024 — RR 4.38 for recurrence. ESVS 2023 guidelines.
Arterial dissection — wall hematoma or dissection flap
Dissection is a distinct "Other Determined Etiology" per TOAST — not LVD. Most common: ICA or vertebral artery. CTA findings: intimal flap, double lumen, tapered occlusion ("flame sign"), pseudoaneurysm, mural hematoma (hyperintense on fat-sat T1). MRA: tapered stenosis or occlusion. Vessel wall MRI: intramural hematoma. Mechanism: artery-to-artery embolism from thrombus on the dissected intima, or local occlusion at the dissection site. Imaging for dissection: CTA/MRA showing arterial narrowing, intimal flap, or pseudoaneurysm. High-resolution black blood MRA (vessel wall MRI, T1 fat-saturated sequences) is the gold standard for detecting intramural hematoma — the T1-hyperintense crescentic signal in the vessel wall. Request specifically when dissection is suspected and CTA is equivocal.
Young patients (<50y), neck trauma, chiropractic manipulation, connective tissue disorders (EDS, Marfan), fibromuscular dysplasia — high suspicion for dissection. Headache or neck pain at stroke onset is characteristic. Horner syndrome ipsilateral to stroke suggests cervical ICA dissection.

Moyamoya — Anterior Circulation
Moya moya disease / syndrome — bilateral or unilateral ICA/MCA occlusion with "puff of smoke" collaterals
Moya moya disease (MMD): idiopathic, progressive bilateral steno-occlusion of terminal ICA + proximal MCA/ACA with compensatory "puff of smoke" basal collaterals. More common in East Asians; RNF213 gene variant. Annual stroke risk 3.2–15% without revascularization; post-STA-MCA bypass: 0–1.6%. Both ischemic (hemodynamic hypoperfusion) and hemorrhagic (fragile collateral rupture) presentations. 2021 Japanese guidelines: diagnosis requires bilateral findings or unilateral + contralateral evidence.
Moya moya syndrome (MMS): same radiologic pattern but associated with identifiable condition — Takayasu arteritis, Down syndrome, NF-1, sickle cell anemia, cranial irradiation, APLS, atherosclerosis. Treat underlying condition first.
VW-MRI in Moya moya: No/minimal vessel wall enhancement in idiopathic MMD (quiescent). Active inflammatory MMS (e.g. Takayasu) may show concentric enhancement in affected large vessels.

Vertebrobasilar disease findings
Clinical distinction: Brainstem and thalamic infarcts arise almost exclusively from the posterior circulation — basilar artery atherosclerosis or perforator occlusion:

Thalamic blood supply — important anatomy: All four thalamic arterial territories arise from the posterior circulation:
• Anterior thalamus → tuberothalamic (polar) artery from PComA (absent in 30–60% of individuals; when absent, territory supplied by paramedian artery from same side)
• Paramedian / posterior thalamus → thalamoperforating arteries from P1 segment of PCA
• Inferolateral thalamus → thalamogeniculate arteries from P2-PCA
• Posterior thalamus / pulvinar → posteromedial choroidal artery from PCA
⚠ Common misconception: The recurrent artery of Heubner (from A1-A2 ACA junction) supplies the caudate head, anterior putamen, and anteroinferior internal capsule — NOT the thalamus. Anterior thalamic infarcts are PComA-territory (posterior circulation), not ACA territory.

• Basilar artery stenosis / hemodynamic: Anteromedial brainstem / paramedian thalamic bilateral infarcts in basilar perforator territory. Crescendo TIAs with standing/exertion (hemodynamic). CTA/MRA shows basilar stenosis or VBD.
• Top-of-basilar embolism: Simultaneous bilateral thalamic + occipital + rostral midbrain infarction. Abrupt onset. Bilateral "butterfly" thalamic infarcts = top-of-basilar embolism until proven otherwise.

Ref: Voetsch B et al. Arch Neurol 2004. Caplan LR, Posterior Circulation Stroke. EPOS ECR 2019 (thalamic vascular territories). StatPearls — Neuroanatomy, Cerebral Blood Supply 2023.
Degree of stenosis — Vertebrobasilar system (WASID grading; ≥50% = ICAD)
Basilar artery stenosis ≥50% is associated with anteromedial brainstem and bilateral thalamic infarction via perforator occlusion or hemodynamic failure. Top-of-basilar embolism typically causes a distinctive syndrome: bilateral thalamic, occipital, and rostral midbrain infarction simultaneously. Vertebral artery disease (stenosis, dissection, dolichoectasia) can cause lateral medullary / PICA territory infarcts via artery-to-artery embolism or local thrombosis.
VBD is an independent risk factor for ischemic stroke and all-cause mortality beyond conventional vascular risk factors (PubMed 14707300; JSTROKE 2008). Primarily relevant in posterior circulation stroke. Mechanisms: (1) local thrombosis in low-flow/turbulent regions; (2) artery-to-artery embolism from ectatic basilar; (3) penetrating artery compression/stretching — pontine/midbrain infarcts (deep perforator compression).

Recurrence risk (BMC Neurology 2019, n=115): BA diameter ≥5.3 mm: HR 4.744 (95% CI 1.718–13.097). Diffuse dolichoectasia: HR 3.603 (95% CI 1.367–9.496). Overall 22-month recurrence: 19.1%.
Vertebrobasilar dolichoectasia — tortuous/ectatic vertebrobasilar arteries; posterior circulation stroke
Consider as contributing or dominant etiology when: (1) posterior circulation stroke without clear atherosclerosis, cardioembolism, or dissection; (2) MRA/CT demonstrates ectatic, tortuous basilar or vertebral arteries; (3) BA diameter ≥4.5–5.3 mm (≥5.3 mm = 90th percentile = highest-risk group).

VBD is classified as a large-vessel disease process — tortuous, enlarged arteries with turbulent flow, endothelial damage, and susceptibility to thrombosis and embolism. This is distinct from atherosclerotic stenosis but shares the parent vessel mechanism category. Not associated with atherosclerotic plaque or stenosis specifically — an ectatic/dilated vessel with normal or even enlarged lumen is the hallmark.
Ref: PubMed 14707300 (JSTROKE 2008) — VBD independent stroke risk. BMC Neurology 2019 (PMC6636033) — HR 4.744 for BA ≥5.3 mm. Passero SG & Rossi S, Neurology 2008 — natural history VBD.
Arterial dissection — wall hematoma or dissection flap
Dissection is a distinct "Other Determined Etiology" per TOAST — not LVD. Most common: ICA or vertebral artery. CTA findings: intimal flap, double lumen, tapered occlusion ("flame sign"), pseudoaneurysm, mural hematoma (hyperintense on fat-sat T1). MRA: tapered stenosis or occlusion. Vessel wall MRI: intramural hematoma. Mechanism: artery-to-artery embolism from thrombus on the dissected intima, or local occlusion at the dissection site. Imaging for dissection: CTA/MRA showing arterial narrowing, intimal flap, or pseudoaneurysm. High-resolution black blood MRA (vessel wall MRI, T1 fat-saturated sequences) is the gold standard for detecting intramural hematoma — the T1-hyperintense crescentic signal in the vessel wall. Request specifically when dissection is suspected and CTA is equivocal.
Young patients (<50y), neck trauma, chiropractic manipulation, connective tissue disorders (EDS, Marfan), fibromuscular dysplasia — high suspicion for dissection. Headache or neck pain at stroke onset is characteristic. Horner syndrome ipsilateral to stroke suggests cervical ICA dissection.
PCA findings
Stenosis on the vessel supplying the infarct territory
Relevant parent vessels: ipsilateral ICA (for anterior circulation), ipsilateral vertebral or basilar (for posterior circulation), MCA M1/M2, PCA, ACA. Stenosis graded by NASCET criteria for extracranial ICA (see Step 5). Intracranial vessels graded by WASID criteria. Reversible Cerebral Vasoconstriction Syndrome (RCVS) Other Determined Etiology
RCVS considerations
RCVS is a rare but important cause of ischemic stroke, particularly in young adults (predominantly women). Characterized by recurrent thunderclap headaches (reaching peak intensity within ≤1 minute) with multifocal segmental vasoconstriction resolving within 3 months. Accounts for a significant proportion of cryptogenic stroke with thunderclap headache history.

Ischemic mechanism: Hypoperfusion in watershed territories during severe vasoconstriction; "string of beads" or "sausage on a string" appearance on angiography.
Complications: ~30–50% develop complications — convexity subarachnoid hemorrhage, lobar hemorrhage, ischemic stroke (watershed), PRES (7–38% overlap). (Singhal AB. Int J Stroke 2023; StatPearls RCVS 2024)
RCVS2 score: High accuracy distinguishing RCVS from PACNS — recurrent thunderclap headache (+3), carotid artery involvement (−2), intracranial carotid involvement (+1), sex (female +1), subarachnoid hemorrhage (+1). Score ≥5 strongly favors RCVS (Neurology 2019).
Triggers: Vasoactive drugs (triptans, SSRIs, cocaine, amphetamines), postpartum/eclampsia, surgical procedures.
Treatment: Nimodipine (vasodilation); remove triggers; avoid vasoconstrictors. Long-term prognosis favorable — 71% no disability (PMC systematic review). ICD-10: I67.841.

How to assess suspicion level:
High suspicion: Young cryptogenic stroke + at least 2 of: multiterritory infarcts unexplained by cardiac or large vessel source, prominent headache, CSF pleocytosis, elevated inflammatory markers (ESR/CRP/ANA/ANCA), known systemic autoimmune disease, "beading" on vessel wall MRI or catheter angiography. Takayasu Arteritis (TA) Large-Vessel Vasculitis — Stroke in Young
Takayasu arteritis — known diagnosis or features strongly suggestive (young patient, aortic arch / branch vessel involvement, constitutional symptoms)
Takayasu arteritis (TA) is a chronic granulomatous large-vessel vasculitis predominantly affecting the aorta and its major branches. Primarily young women (<40). Stroke affects 10–20% of TA patients (7× general population risk; Duarte 2016 meta-analysis). Infarct patterns: large lobar (33%), cortical border-zone (29%). Mechanisms: (1) hemodynamic hypoperfusion from ICA/subclavian/aortic stenosis; (2) artery-to-artery embolism; (3) inflammatory intracranial vessel involvement.

TA + Moya moya syndrome: TA can cause progressive inflammatory ICA/MCA occlusion → secondary Moya moya syndrome (acquired, not idiopathic). Treat underlying TA first.

Diagnosis: ESR/CRP elevated in active disease; "macaroni sign" on carotid US (mural thickening); PET-CT detects active inflammation. ACR 1990 criteria: age ≤40, claudication, decreased brachial pulse, BP difference >10 mmHg, subclavian/aortic bruit, angiographic abnormality.

VW-MRI in Takayasu: Concentric long-segment mural enhancement in large vessels (carotid, aortic branches) — same pattern as vasculitis but in large not small vessels. Distinguishes from atherosclerosis (eccentric) and RCVS (no enhancement).

Treatment: Corticosteroids (prednisone 1 mg/kg/day); MTX/azathioprine/MMF for maintenance; tocilizumab for refractory disease. Antiplatelet for stroke prevention. Revascularization during remission.
Ref: Duarte MM et al. J Stroke Cerebrovasc Dis 2016;25:781. Uchiyama S et al. AJNR 2017;38:766.
Post-infectious vasculopathy RECENT INFECTION HISTORY
Post-infectious vasculopathy — VZV/HSV (zoster, herpes simplex), COVID-19, other preceding infection within 6 months
Mechanism: Several infections can trigger inflammatory vasculopathy as a delayed or concurrent complication — distinct from direct septic embolism. The vessel wall is injured by direct viral invasion, immune-mediated inflammation, or hypercoagulable state induced by infection.

Varicella Zoster Virus (VZV) / HSV (herpes simplex/zoster virus): Most well-established post-infectious vasculopathy. VZV travels transaxonally from cranial ganglia (especially trigeminal) to cerebral artery adventitia, causing transmural granulomatous inflammation → intimal hyperplasia, thrombosis, and arterial stenosis/occlusion. Stroke risk is 1.3–4× higher after HSV (herpes simplex/zoster virus), peaking in the first 6 months (Gilden et al.; Langan 2014). Risk is highest in HSV (herpes simplex/zoster virus) ophthalmicus (ophthalmic division → ipsilateral anterior circulation). Can affect both large and small vessels. Treat with IV acyclovir + corticosteroids.

COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2): Associated with stroke through several mechanisms: hypercoagulable state (elevated D-dimer, antiphospholipid antibodies in ~50% of severe COVID-19), endothelial injury and ACE-2-mediated vascular inflammation, cardioembolism from COVID-19 myocarditis/cardiomyopathy, and direct CNS vasculitis (rare). Thromboinflammatory pathway predominates (Romero et al. 2021).

Other: Bacterial meningitis (septic vasculitis), Lyme disease, HIV vasculopathy, rickettsia, mycoplasma, influenza (rare). Consider in appropriate clinical and geographic context.
Key refs: Gilden DH et al. VZV vasculopathy. Neurology 2019. Langan SM et al. Lancet 2014 — 1.3× stroke risk after zoster, highest 1st year. Romero JR et al. J Neurol Sci 2021 — COVID-19 thromboinflammatory stroke mechanisms. VZV stroke risk in <40y: OR up to 4× in first 6 months post-zoster.
CSF Analysis — VZV/HSV Workup Critical to Distinguish VZV/HSV Vasculopathy from PACNS
Why CSF testing is essential: VZV/HSV vasculopathy and PACNS are clinically and radiologically nearly identical (both cause concentric VW-MRI enhancement, similar infarct patterns, and CSF pleocytosis in ~67% of VZV cases). The only way to definitively confirm or exclude VZV vasculopathy is virologic CSF analysis.

Test performance (Nagel MA et al. Neurology 2007; PMC2938740 — 30 cases of VZV vasculopathy):
• Anti-VZV IgG antibody in CSF: 93% sensitive (28/30 patients positive). Requires intrathecal synthesis confirmation: serum/CSF VZV IgG ratio ≤10.4. This is the primary diagnostic test.
• VZV DNA by PCR in CSF: 30% sensitive (9/30 patients positive). Useful if positive (specific for VZV), but a negative PCR does NOT exclude VZV vasculopathy.
• Both VZV DNA and VZV IgG negative: excludes VZV vasculopathy — shifts diagnosis toward PACNS or other CNS vasculitis.
• Note: 37% of VZV vasculopathy patients have no rash history; 33% have no CSF pleocytosis. Do not require rash to test CSF.
• Intrathecal IgG appears 1–2 weeks after infection onset and persists; sensitivity is higher when LP is performed >2 weeks after symptom onset.

HSV vasculopathy: CSF HSV PCR and HSV IgG (both HSV-1 and HSV-2) should be tested. HSV PCR has ~95% sensitivity in acute herpetic encephalitis but data specific to HSV-related vasculopathy (rare) is limited — negative PCR does not exclude HSV vasculopathy.

Ref: Nagel MA et al. Neurology 2007;68:1031 (n=14); Nagel MA et al. J Neurol Sci 2008;272:39 (n=30). Gilden D et al. PMC2938740.

Genetic Arteriopathy CONSIDER IN YOUNG CRYPTOGENIC STROKE
Genetic arteriopathy considerations
Genetic arteriopathies are rare but important in young cryptogenic stroke. Consider when no other etiology is identified:

CADASIL (NOTCH3 mutation) — most common hereditary stroke disease in adults. Characteristic MRI: T2/FLAIR white matter hyperintensities in anterior temporal poles and external capsule (Dichgans M et al. Lancet Neurol 2019). Recurrent lacunar strokes + migraine with aura + cognitive decline. Prevalence ~4–5/100,000; diagnosis by NOTCH3 gene testing or skin biopsy (GOM deposits). No proven treatment; BP control, antiplatelet for stroke prevention.

Fabry Disease (GLA gene, X-linked) — α-galactosidase A deficiency. Stroke in young adults (mean age ~38y in males). Posterior circulation strokes common (vertebrobasilar dolichoectasia is frequent). Associated features: neuropathic pain, angiokeratoma, cornea verticillata, renal/cardiac involvement. Prevalence in cryptogenic stroke: ~1–4% (Rolfs A et al. Lancet 2005). Diagnosed by enzyme assay (males) or GLA gene testing (females). Treatment: enzyme replacement therapy.

MELAS (Mitochondrial Encephalomyopathy, Lactic Acidosis, Stroke-like episodes) — maternally inherited mitochondrial DNA mutation (most commonly m.3243A>G in MT-TL1, ~80% of cases). Stroke-like episodes typically posterior > anterior; do NOT follow vascular territories (crossing vascular boundaries on DWI is a key imaging clue). Associated with: elevated serum/CSF lactate, hearing loss, migraine, seizures, short stature, ophthalmoplegia, diabetes. Mean onset of stroke-like episodes ~35y. MRI: diffusion-positive lesions in cortex and subcortical regions NOT conforming to arterial territories — key distinguishing feature from arterial ischemic stroke. Diagnosis: mitochondrial genetic panel, muscle biopsy (ragged red fibers). Treatment: L-arginine infusion acutely, supplementation; no proven disease-modifying therapy (Yamamoto et al. Neurology 2021).
Vessel Wall MRI — If Performed INFLAMMATORY / HIGH-GRADE STENOSIS
High-resolution black blood vessel wall MRI (VW-MRI) is the key non-invasive discriminator for intracranial stenosis etiology:
Concentric wall enhancement → vasculitis / PACNS or Takayasu arteritis (circumferential inflammatory mural thickening). Distinguish: Takayasu = long-segment, large vessels (carotid/aorta/vertebral), extracranial predominant; PACNS = short-segment, medium/small intracranial vessels. Both show concentric enhancement. Also seen in: lymphoma, reversible cerebral angiopathy
Eccentric wall enhancement → atherosclerotic ICAD (plaque at one wall; vulnerable plaque with lipid-rich necrotic core; matches stenosis side)
No wall enhancement → RCVS (smooth vessel narrowing with no/minimal mural enhancement; vasospasm without wall inflammation)
Crescentic T1-hyperintense mural hematoma → arterial dissection (intramural blood creates characteristic T1 bright crescent along vessel wall; best seen on fat-suppressed T1 black-blood MRI). Eccentric and does NOT enhance post-contrast (unlike plaque). Look for: false lumen, double lumen, pseudoaneurysm.
Ref: Mandell DM et al. Stroke 2017; Obusez EC et al. AJNR 2016; Swartz RH et al. Neurology 2009; Saremi F et al. Vessel Wall MRI in Dissection. AJNR 2018.
Vessel wall MRI result — if performed
VW-MRI sensitivity and specificity for intracranial vasculopathies:

CNS vasculitis (PACNS / secondary including VZV): Concentric wall enhancement identifies medium-vessel CNS vasculitis with sensitivity 94%, specificity 95% (Frontiers in Stroke 2022;1:973517). Enhancement is intense and concentric in 92.3% of CNS vasculitis cases (both PACNS and secondary vasculitis; MDPI Diagnostics 2024;14:927). Enhancement patterns are similar between PACNS and infectious vasculitis (VZV, bacterial, fungal) — VW-MRI cannot reliably distinguish VZV vasculopathy from PACNS; CSF analysis (VZV PCR or anti-VZV IgG) is required for this differentiation (AJNR case collection 2023).

VZV/HSV vasculopathy specifically: Shows concentric mural enhancement, preferentially involving terminal ICA and M1 MCA. Eccentric enhancement can occur in a minority. Enhancement resolves on follow-up VW-MRI at 3–21 months after antiviral treatment (ScienceDirect 2018). Vessel wall thickening may precede stenosis — VW-MRI detects early disease before luminal compromise appears on MRA.

Atherosclerotic ICAD: Eccentric enhancement — plaque at one vessel wall with fibrous cap (T2 hyperintense, enhancing) and lipid core (T2 hypointense, non-enhancing). Distinguishable from vasculitis in most cases by asymmetry and layered plaque architecture (Mandell DM et al. Stroke 2017).

RCVS: No or minimal wall enhancement despite severe segmental vasoconstriction. The "string of beads" appearance on MRA with absent VW-MRI enhancement distinguishes RCVS from vasculitis. Absence of enhancement on VW-MRI in the context of vasoconstriction is highly suggestive of RCVS (Swartz RH et al. Neurology 2009).

Takayasu arteritis: Long-segment concentric mural thickening and enhancement in large vessels (carotid, vertebral, aortic branches) — same pattern as PACNS but at a larger vessel scale and distribution (Uchiyama S et al. AJNR 2017).

Limitations: VW-MRI requires 3T scanner, gadolinium contrast, and experienced neuroradiologist interpretation. Small vessel vasculitis (<1mm) is below VW-MRI spatial resolution. A normal VW-MRI does not exclude vasculitis — small-vessel PACNS may not be detectable.
Small Vessel Disease — Imaging Assessment SVD LOCATIONS IDENTIFIED
Striatocapsular / brainstem / thalamic location identified — SVD imaging features are relevant to etiology classification.
White Matter Hyperintensities — Fazekas Scale
Why these SVD imaging questions matter for stroke etiology:

Context: Striatocapsular, brainstem, and thalamic infarcts are the classic SVD (small vessel disease) locations — perforator territory lesions from lipohyalinosis or microatheroma. These four questions establish the SVD imaging burden, which substantially shifts the pre-test probability toward SVD as the primary mechanism.

White Matter Hyperintensities (Fazekas): Periventricular and deep WMH represent chronic small vessel ischemia. Fazekas ≥2 (confluent) = high SVD burden (Wardlaw 2013 STRIVE criteria). Independently predicts recurrent lacunar stroke and dementia.

Chronic lacunes: Prior DWI-negative punctate lesions in deep gray/white matter = established SVD signature. Multiple lacunes strongly support SVD as the mechanism — but also raise the differential of multiple small emboli (consider ESUS vs SVD).

Cerebral microbleeds (SWI/GRE): Deep/basal ganglia microbleeds = hypertensive SVD; cortical/corticosubcortical = cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA). Location is critical: mixed pattern suggests more than one SVD pathology.

Vascular risk factor burden: Established hypertension, DM, and hyperlipidemia are the substrate for SVD. Multi-factorial risk establishes the "vascular milieu" that supports chronic small vessel injury as the mechanism.

Refs: STRIVE criteria (Wardlaw JM et al. Lancet Neurol 2013). Fazekas scale (Fazekas F et al. AJNR 1987). Jokinen H et al. Stroke 2014.

Metabolic Biomarkers — HbA1c and LDL Differentiates SVD vs. LVD Atherosclerotic Drivers
HbA1c and LDL have distinct but overlapping contributions to stroke etiology:

HbA1c (glycemic control): Severe hyperglycemia drives microvascular endothelial dysfunction and lipohyalinosis → preferentially promotes SVD. HbA1c >9% is associated with accelerated SVD burden and lacunar infarction (Shah AD et al. BMJ 2015, n=1.9M patients: HR for lacunar stroke 1.54 per 1% HbA1c increase). Poorly controlled diabetes also accelerates macrovascular atherosclerosis contributing to LAA, but its primary microvascular effect is SVD.

LDL cholesterol: Markedly elevated LDL preferentially drives large vessel atherosclerosis (LAA). LDL >160 mg/dL is independently associated with LAA stroke (OR 2.1 in INTERSTROKE study, n=26,919; O'Donnell MJ et al. Lancet 2016). High-intensity statin therapy reduces LAA stroke recurrence by 16–36% (SPARCL trial: atorvastatin 80mg; HR 0.84 for any stroke). Note: LDL <70 mg/dL on high-intensity statins does not eliminate atherosclerotic plaque burden — calcified plaques persist. Conversely, low LDL with well-controlled diabetes shifts away from atherosclerotic mechanisms.

Refs: Shah AD et al. BMJ 2015;351:h4244. O'Donnell MJ et al. Lancet 2016;388:761. SPARCL Investigators NEJM 2006;355:549. Emerging Risk Factors Collaboration, JAMA 2014;311:1337.

HbA1c 7–9% = suboptimally controlled → moderate microvascular risk. HbA1c <7% on treatment = adequate control; reduces but does not eliminate SVD risk if diabetes is longstanding. (JACC 2018;71:1788)

High risk from aortic cross-clamping, atheromatous embolism, prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass, and circulatory arrest (deep hypothermic circulatory arrest strokes cluster in first 24h). (JACC 2018; STS database)

CEA / TCAR / carotid stenting: CEA 30-day stroke/death: ~1.85% non-octogenarians, ~2.04% octogenarians (meta-analysis 47 studies, N=107,587; Touzé et al. Stroke 2023). TCAR: ~2.0% (VQI; Schermerhorn 2023). TF-CAS: ~3.7% in symptomatic patients (JAHA 2023). Mechanism: plaque embolism from carotid manipulation; watershed from clamping hypoperfusion.

Cardiac ablation / cardioversion / EP study: Ablation stroke: 0.1–0.5% (varies by type; AF ablation 0.2–0.5%). Cardioversion: <0.1% when properly anticoagulated. Mechanism: thrombus formation on catheter, air embolism, pre-existing LAA thrombus mobilization.

When to classify as periprocedural: Stroke temporally related to procedure (strongest within 72h; defined as ≤30 days) AND infarct pattern consistent with embolic mechanism (multiple territories, posterior circulation bias) or hemodynamic watershed. If independent etiology is more parsimonious (e.g. confirmed LAA thrombus pre-existing AFib), prioritize that.

Step 6 · Transcranial Doppler (TCD)
TCD provides real-time intracranial hemodynamic data and microembolic signal detection that can critically distinguish between competing etiologies — particularly carotid vs. cardiac sources, PFO vs. local arterial disease, and ICAD vs. proximal stenosis. Enter results if TCD was performed.
TCD Normal Values and Diagnostic Thresholds:

Normal mean flow velocities (MFV): MCA 55±12 cm/s  |  ACA 50±11 cm/s  |  PCA 39±10 cm/s  |  Basilar 41±10 cm/s  |  Vertebral 38±10 cm/s  |  ICA (siphon) 47±14 cm/s

ICA Stenosis — Velocity Thresholds (NASCET correlation):
• ≥50% stenosis: MCA MFV ≥100 cm/s (sensitivity 91%, specificity 87%) or PSV ≥160 cm/s
• ≥70% stenosis (severe): MCA MFV ≥120–150 cm/s; Lindegaard Ratio (MCA/ICA) >3 suggests vasospasm
• Near-occlusion / pre-occlusive: Blunted waveform with reduced pulsatility, tardus-parvus pattern
• Occlusion: Absent or reversed flow; collateral signals via ACoA/PCoA

Intracranial stenosis (ICAD):
• MCA M1 stenosis ≥50%: MFV ≥100 cm/s; ≥70%: MFV ≥120 cm/s
• Basilar stenosis: MFV ≥80 cm/s suggests ≥50%; ≥100 cm/s suggests severe

Vasospasm (post-SAH):
• MCA MFV ≥120 cm/s = mild; ≥150 cm/s = moderate; ≥200 cm/s = severe
• Lindegaard Ratio >3 = vasospasm (not hyperemia); >6 = severe vasospasm

Right-to-left shunt (PFO / IPAH):
• Curtain pattern (≥10 microembolic signals within 40s of agitated saline) = large shunt
• Spencer classification: Grade I–V based on MES count; Grade IV–V = high-risk PFO

Refs: Alexandrov AV et al. J Neuroimaging 2007; Ringelstein EB et al. Stroke 1992; Aaslid R et al. J Neurosurg 1982; Sloan MA et al. Neurology 1994 (vasospasm thresholds)

HITS / Microembolic Signals (spontaneous emboli monitoring) HIGHEST ETIOLOGIC VALUE
Spontaneous HITS / MES on bilateral MCA monitoring (30–60 min)
Position TCD probes bilaterally over MCAs. Record spontaneous embolic signals for 30–60 minutes. Key interpretive rules:

Bilateral / multiterritory HITS: Strongly implicates a central embolic source (cardiac, aortic arch). A unilateral carotid plaque or web cannot produce simultaneous bilateral MCA HITS. This finding effectively rules out carotid web or unilateral carotid atherosclerosis as the primary mechanism — even if a carotid web is present on imaging.

Unilateral ipsilateral HITS only: Favours a local arterial source — ipsilateral carotid stenosis/plaque, carotid web, or ICAD. Supports LVD etiology in the symptomatic territory.

MES signal characteristics (if detailed analysis available): Cardiac-source MES have longer duration, higher total signal power, and occur predominantly in diastole. Arterial-source MES (carotid, ICAD) are predominantly systolic and shorter in duration (Ringelstein 1998).
Key refs: Ringelstein EB et al. Ann Neurol 1998;44:186 — cardiac vs. arterial MES differentiation. CARESS trial (Markus 2005, Lancet Neurol) — MES as therapeutic monitoring in carotid stenosis. Abbott AL et al. Stroke 2005 — asymptomatic carotid stenosis: >2 MES/hour ipsilateral MCA = 3.6% vs. 0.7% 2-year stroke risk.
TCD Bubble Study — Right-to-Left Shunt IF PFO SUSPECTED
Agitated saline injection with bilateral MCA TCD monitoring
Method: 9 mL normal saline + 1 mL air agitated × 10 between two syringes, injected IV while monitoring bilateral MCAs at rest and during Valsalva. HITS within 5–25 cardiac cycles = intracardiac RLS (PFO). HITS after 25 cycles = intrapulmonary shunt (pulmonary AVM or hepatopulmonary syndrome).

Spencer grading scale (shunt size):
Grade 1: 1–10 HITS (small)  ·  Grade 2: 11–30  ·  Grade 3: 31–100 (large)  ·  Grade 4: 101–300  ·  Grade 5: >300 / "curtain" (massive)

Grade ≥3 or curtain pattern = large shunt → increases PFO attribution probability (higher RoPE score contribution). TCD bubble study sensitivity for PFO ~90–95%, comparable to TEE with Valsalva.
Refs: Spencer MP et al. J Ultrasound Med 2004 — grading scale. Di Tullio M et al. Stroke 1993 — TCD bubble study sensitivity. Mas JL et al. CLOSE trial — PFO closure benefit in Grade ≥3 shunt + atrial septal aneurysm.
Intracranial Flow Velocities ICAD / HEMODYNAMIC COMPROMISE
Intracranial flow velocities and collateral patterns
Focal velocity elevation suggesting ICAD: MCA mean velocity >80 cm/s (or peak systolic >120 cm/s), basilar >100 cm/s, ACA/PCA >80 cm/s, with focal turbulence. Lindegaard ratio (ipsilateral MCA/extracranial ICA) >3 = ICAD vs. hyperemia.

Reduced velocity / hemodynamic compromise from ICA stenosis: Ipsilateral MCA mean velocity reduced >50% vs. contralateral = significant hemodynamic compromise. Reversed ophthalmic artery flow = severe ICA stenosis with orbital collateral. Impaired vasomotor reactivity (breath-hold index <8%) = exhausted autoregulatory reserve — supports watershed/hemodynamic mechanism (Silvestrini 2000, HR ~3.7 for ipsilateral stroke at 2 years).

Collateral flow patterns: Increased contralateral ACA velocity (cross-filling via anterior communicating artery), reversed ipsilateral ACA, or increased PCA velocity (posterior communicating collateral) — indicate severe proximal ICA stenosis/occlusion.
Lindegaard KF et al. Stroke 1986 — Lindegaard ratio. Silvestrini M et al. Neurology 2000 — impaired VMR predicts stroke in asymptomatic ICA stenosis.
Step 7 · Hypercoagulable States
This step refines undetermined-source embolic strokes. Applies when no dominant mechanism has been identified in prior steps. Address D-dimer, malignancy screening, and hypercoagulable states — post-discharge testing increases HCS diagnosis 3-fold (Gordo et al. Front Neurol. 2025).
D-Dimer Assessment Critical Biomarker in ESUS
D-dimer level
D-dimer in ESUS: Elevated D-dimer is associated with increased risk of recurrent embolic stroke and should raise suspicion for underlying malignancy or hypercoagulable state.
• D-dimer ≥2,785 FEU ng/mL: sensitivity 51%, specificity 99% for differentiating embolic stroke with cancer vs. without cancer (Mishra RK et al. J Neurosci Rural Pract. 2024).
• D-dimer quartile risk (ESUS cohort, Choi KH et al. Stroke 2021, n=1,431):
  Q1 (200–520 ng/mL): Reference · Q2 (540–1,040): HR 2.42 · Q3 (1,060–2,480): HR 3.12 (95% CI 1.07–9.07) · Q4 (≥2,500): HR 7.29 (95% CI 2.59–20.52)

Cancer Screening Status (USPSTF) Malignancy-Associated Stroke
Known active malignancy or newly suspected cancer-associated stroke
Adenocarcinoma, sarcoma, and cardiac fibroelastoma/myxoma carry the highest risk of cancer-associated stroke (Sener U et al. Mayo Clin Proc 2022). Mechanisms include: systemic hypercoagulability, NBTE (non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis), circulating microparticles, atrial cardiomyopathy, and treatment-related events.

USPSTF age-appropriate cancer screening (2024):
• Breast: Mammogram every 2 years, age 40–74 (Grade B, USPSTF 2024)
• Colorectal: Colonoscopy or equivalent, starting age 45 (Grade B, USPSTF 2021; gold standard is colonoscopy)
• Lung: Annual LDCT, age 50–80, ≥20 pack-year history, currently smoking or quit within 15 years (Grade B, USPSTF 2021)
• Cervical: Pap smear q3y (age 21–65) or HPV co-test q5y (age 30–65) (Grade A, USPSTF)
• Prostate: Shared decision-making, age 55–69 (Grade C, USPSTF) — discuss with patient
Note: if active malignancy is already known, document type and staging.
Hypercoagulable States APLS / Factor V Leiden / PTG / Protein C,S
Multiple-hit hypothesis: Symptomatic thrombosis is uncommon with only one hypercoagulable state — two or more concurrent HCS, one HCS + atrial septal abnormality, or one HCS + persistent migraine with aura significantly increase risk.

APLS antibody risk (OR for arterial events): Lupus anticoagulant OR 3.58 · Anti-β2-glycoprotein-I OR 3.12 · Anti-cardiolipin OR 2.65 (Horbach 1996; Zen et al. Rheumatology 2025). Triple-positive = highest risk — warfarin strongly preferred over DOACs (meta-analysis of RAPS/TRAPS/Ordi-Ros/ASTRO-APS: DOACs OR 5.43 for arterial events vs. warfarin, p<0.001; Khairani CD et al. JACC 2023). Single-positive: DOACs possibly safe (Liu A et al. Eur J Haematol 2022).

Factor V Leiden (FVL): OR 2.7 (95% CI 1.9–3.8) in young adults with cryptogenic stroke; unlikely risk factor in older adults (Hamedani AG et al. Stroke 2010). Subgroup analysis: <50 years, cryptogenic.

Prothrombin G20210A: OR 1.5 (95% CI 1.1–2.0) in young adults — meta-analysis of 18 case-control studies. Not a significant risk in older adults (Ridker et al. 1999, n=15,000, RR 1.1).

Post-discharge testing: Testing post-discharge (off anticoagulation) increases HCS diagnosis 3-fold and decreases cryptogenesis by half (Gordo et al. Front Neurol. 2025;15:1393999).
Antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (APLS) — lupus anticoagulant, anti-β2GPI, anti-cardiolipin
APLS diagnosis requires: (1) clinical event (thrombosis or pregnancy morbidity) AND (2) positive laboratory test on TWO occasions ≥12 weeks apart. Lupus anticoagulant is the most thrombogenic — even as a single positive it is considered "high-risk profile." Triple-positivity (LA + aCL + anti-β2GPI) carries OR 5.43 for arterial events with DOAC vs. warfarin — warfarin is the only guideline-recommended agent for triple-positive APLS stroke.
Other hypercoagulable states — Factor V Leiden, prothrombin G20210A, protein C/S deficiency, antithrombin deficiency
Inherited thrombophilias most relevant in: age <55, cryptogenic stroke, prior DVT/PE, family history of thrombosis. Testing ideally done off anticoagulation (acute-phase proteins can affect results).

Factor V Leiden (FVL): Most common inherited thrombophilia (~5% of Caucasians). Activates APC resistance. Risk for arterial stroke: OR 2.7 (95% CI 1.9–3.8) in young adults with cryptogenic stroke (Hamedani AG et al. Stroke 2010; meta-analysis of 19 case-control studies). FVL is unlikely to be a significant risk factor in adults >60y — competing vascular risk factors dominate. Heterozygous FVL: 3–7× increased VTE risk; homozygous: 50–80×.

Prothrombin G20210A (PTG): Second most common thrombophilia (~2% of Caucasians). OR 1.5 (95% CI 1.1–2.0) for ischemic stroke in young adults — meta-analysis of 18 case-control studies (IPSYS Investigators, Circulation 2014). Not a significant risk in older adults (Ridker et al. NEJM 1999, n=15,000 men; RR 1.1, 95% CI 0.6–2.1). Consider testing in young cryptogenic stroke, especially with prior VTE or family history.

Protein C/S deficiency: Reduced anticoagulant activity. Both cause increased venous and arterial thrombosis; protein S deficiency may be more relevant for arterial events. Levels can be reduced acutely by inflammation, sepsis, DIC, liver disease, warfarin — test at least 3 months post-stroke off anticoagulation for reliable results.

Antithrombin deficiency: Rare (<1% of VTE patients) but high thrombotic risk. Acquired causes: nephrotic syndrome, liver disease, heparin use (heparin requires antithrombin as co-factor — deficiency causes heparin resistance).

JAK2 V617F: Myeloproliferative neoplasm marker. Associated with cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, unusual arterial thrombosis, Budd-Chiari syndrome. Consider in young stroke + abnormal CBC (elevated Hgb, Hct, platelets).

Teaching Strategy
Sessions 1–2 are best taught in the EMG lab with equipment running — let residents feel the electrode, watch a CMAP form on screen, and handle the needle before the didactic session. Session 4 is the conceptual pivot point — once residents can categorise any result as axonal/demyelinating/NMJ/myopathic, everything else follows. Session 6 requires them to synthesise all four dimensions simultaneously.
The most dangerous misunderstanding in junior residents approaching EMG: they treat it as a binary test (normal vs abnormal). Redirect immediately — EMG is a physiological study that answers four questions about a neuromuscular complaint. If you don't know what question you're asking, you cannot interpret the answer. Before every case, ask: "What is the clinical question we are sending this patient to EMG to answer?"
Lesson 01 · 40 minutes

EMG Fundamentals

Everything in EMG interpretation flows from a clear understanding of the motor unit and the difference between axon and myelin pathology. Teach these two concepts before touching any numbers.

Opening Frame — 5 min
What EMG and NCS Actually Measure

Ask residents: "What does an EMG measure?" Most will say "muscle electricity" or "nerve signals." The precise answer: EMG/NCS measures the electrical behaviour of nerve and muscle in response to stimulation and voluntary contraction, allowing physiological characterisation of the peripheral nervous system from anterior horn cell to neuromuscular junction to muscle fibre.

This is different from imaging. An MRI shows structure. An EMG shows function. A nerve can look intact on MRI and be physiologically dead on EMG. A nerve can show signal change on MRI and be functionally normal on EMG. They are complementary, not interchangeable.

Nerve Conduction Studies (NCS)

Electrically stimulate a nerve at the skin surface. Record the resulting electrical response downstream — in muscle (motor NCS) or in nerve (sensory NCS). Measures: amplitude (how many axons are conducting), latency (how fast the signal arrives), and conduction velocity (speed through the nerve segment). Done before needle EMG.

Needle EMG

Insert a needle electrode directly into muscle. Record electrical activity at rest (spontaneous activity) and with voluntary contraction (motor unit action potentials). Detects denervation, reinnervation, myopathic changes, and NMJ dysfunction. Done after NCS — NCS data guides which muscles to sample.

The Motor Unit — 10 min
The Fundamental Unit of EMG Interpretation

The motor unit consists of a single anterior horn cell, its axon, the neuromuscular junction, and all muscle fibres it innervates. Everything seen on needle EMG is a recording of motor unit behaviour. Understanding how disease changes the motor unit explains every EMG finding.

The Two Pathological Axes
Axon loss (wallerian degeneration): the axon itself dies. Consequences — reduced CMAP/SNAP amplitude (fewer conducting fibres), denervation on needle EMG (fibrillations, PSWs), reinnervation changes (large polyphasic MUAPs). Takes weeks to manifest fully.

Demyelination: the myelin sheath is damaged but axons survive. Consequences — slowed conduction velocity, prolonged latencies, conduction block, temporal dispersion. CMAP/SNAP amplitude initially preserved. Needle EMG may be normal early.
The 4-Question Framework — 8 min
Ask These Before Reading Any EMG Report
  1. 1
    Is it neurogenic or myopathic?Neurogenic = problem in nerve, anterior horn cell, or NMJ. Myopathic = problem in muscle. These produce opposite findings on needle EMG. This is the first branch point.
  2. 2
    If neurogenic — is it axonal or demyelinating?Axonal loss reduces amplitude. Demyelination slows velocity/prolongs latency. The NCS pattern answers this. It determines the diagnostic differential entirely.
  3. 3
    What is the distribution?Focal (single nerve/root), multifocal (multiple separate nerves), or diffuse (all nerves)? Symmetric or asymmetric? Length-dependent (distal worse) or not? Distribution drives localisation.
  4. 4
    Is it acute, subacute, or chronic?Fibrillations and PSWs appear 2–3 weeks after denervation. Reinnervation (large polyphasic MUAPs) takes weeks to months. The needle EMG findings encode the timeline of the injury.

Lesson 02 · 45 minutes

Nerve Conduction Studies

NCS is always performed first, before needle EMG, because it provides the structural framework — which nerves are affected and how. Residents must be able to read a NCS table and identify axonal vs demyelinating patterns before proceeding to needle interpretation.

Motor NCS — 12 min
The CMAP: Three Numbers That Localise the Problem

Stimulating a motor nerve at the skin surface produces a Compound Muscle Action Potential (CMAP) — the summed electrical response of all muscle fibres in the recording muscle that were activated. Three measurements are made from the CMAP waveform.

Compound Muscle Action Potential (CMAP) — Annotated
Amplitude (baseline to negative peak, in mV) reflects the number of conducting axons. Distal latency (stimulus to CMAP onset, in ms) reflects conduction through the distal segment. Conduction velocity (distance between two stimulation sites divided by the latency difference) reflects myelin integrity across the proximal segment. These three numbers answer Questions 1 and 2 of the 4-question framework.

Axonal vs Demyelinating — 12 min
The Single Most Important NCS Distinction
Normal NCS
Normal CMAP: good amplitude (full complement of axons conducting), short distal latency, sharp narrow waveform (synchronous conduction), conduction velocity >49 m/s.
Axonal Neuropathy
Axonal neuropathy: CMAP amplitude reduced (fewer conducting axons) but shape is preserved, latency is normal, conduction velocity is normal or only mildly slowed (>70% of lower limit of normal). The surviving axons conduct normally.
Demyelinating Neuropathy
Demyelinating neuropathy: prolonged distal latency (delayed onset), markedly slowed conduction velocity (<70% LLN), and temporal dispersion (broader, lower-amplitude waveform as fibres conduct at different speeds). Amplitude may be relatively preserved early.
Conduction Block
Conduction block: distal stimulation produces a normal-amplitude CMAP, but proximal stimulation across a segment of demyelination produces a markedly reduced CMAP (>50% amplitude drop). The impulse cannot cross the demyelinated segment. Classic in multifocal motor neuropathy, GBS, focal compression.
The Diagnostic Rule — Axonal vs Demyelinating
Demyelination criteria: conduction velocity <70% LLN, distal latency >130% ULN, F-wave latency >130% ULN, or conduction block/temporal dispersion. If two or more criteria met in two or more nerves = demyelinating polyneuropathy (think GBS, CIDP, CMT1). If amplitude reduced with normal or near-normal velocity = axonal (think diabetic, toxic, hereditary axonal). This single distinction drives the entire differential.
Sensory NCS + Late Responses — 10 min
SNAPs and the Sensory/Motor Dissociation Rule

The sensory nerve action potential (SNAP) is recorded directly from a sensory nerve after stimulation. SNAPs are much smaller than CMAPs (microvolts vs millivolts) and are more sensitive to early axonal loss. The critical teaching point is the sensory/motor dissociation rule.

Sensory/Motor Dissociation — The Most Useful Localisation Tool in NCS
Sensory fibres have their cell bodies (dorsal root ganglia — DRG) in the dorsal root ganglion, which sits outside the spinal canal. Motor fibres have their cell bodies in the anterior horn, inside the cord.

Rule: A lesion proximal to the DRG (e.g., root avulsion, plexopathy affecting the dorsal root, spinal cord lesion) — the SNAP remains normal because the DRG and its peripheral processes are intact, even if the patient has sensory loss clinically.

A lesion distal to the DRG (e.g., plexopathy distal to DRG, peripheral neuropathy) — the SNAP is abnormal because the sensory axon from the DRG peripherally has degenerated.

Clinical application: Normal SNAPs in a patient with sensory symptoms = lesion is proximal to the DRG = root, cord, or brain. Abnormal SNAPs = lesion is in the plexus or peripheral nerve.
F-Wave and H-Reflex
F-wave: A supramaximal stimulus antidromically activates motor neurons; the resulting backfiring produces a late CMAP. F-wave latency probes the proximal nerve segment (nerve root, proximal trunk). Prolonged in proximal demyelination (GBS early), root compression, and hereditary demyelinating neuropathies. Normal F-waves with abnormal distal latency = distal demyelination (CTS).

H-reflex: The electrophysiological equivalent of the ankle jerk. Submaximal stimulus activates Ia afferents → dorsal horn → S1 motor neuron → H response in soleus. Absent or prolonged H-reflex = S1 radiculopathy or peripheral neuropathy affecting the S1 arc. Most sensitive test for early S1 root dysfunction.

  • B. Brachial plexopathy (postganglionic lesion distal to DRG)
  • C. Cervical spinal cord injury
  • D. Peripheral polyneuropathy
Lesson 03 · 45 minutes

Needle EMG

Needle EMG is the most information-dense part of the study. Residents need to learn to read two things in sequence: what happens at rest (spontaneous activity), and what happens with contraction (MUAPs and recruitment). Teach these as distinct assessments of distinct pathological processes.

The single best way to teach needle EMG is at the machine with a volunteer's muscle on screen. The sounds matter as much as the visuals — fibrillations have a distinctive "rain on a tin roof" sound, fasciculations sound like "a distant thunder clap", and CRDs have a characteristic machine-gun sound that cannot be described adequately but is immediately recognisable when heard. If possible, record 30-second audio clips to play in the classroom.
Spontaneous Activity — 15 min
What You Hear and See at Rest

A completely relaxed, normal muscle is electrically silent — no spontaneous discharges. Any activity at rest (beyond the brief insertional activity from needle movement) is abnormal and must be categorised.

Fibrillation Potential
Fibrillation potential: A spontaneously firing single denervated muscle fibre. Small amplitude (50–200 µV), short duration (<2 ms), biphasic or triphasic, initially positive deflection. Fire at regular intervals. Appear 2–3 weeks after denervation and indicate active denervation. Graded 0–4+ based on density.
Positive Sharp Wave (PSW)
Positive sharp wave: Same clinical significance as fibrillation potentials — denervated muscle fibre. Caused when the needle electrode tip touches a fibrillating fibre and blocks its propagation, producing the characteristic initial positive (downward) deflection followed by a slow return. Fibrillations and PSWs are graded and reported together.
Fasciculation Potential
Fasciculation potential: A spontaneously firing entire motor unit (not just a single fibre). MUAP-sized and morphologically complex. Fires at irregular intervals — the irregular rate distinguishes it from fibrillations. Visible as a muscle twitch on the skin. Isolated fasciculations can be benign; fasciculations + active denervation (fibrillations + PSWs) = ALS until proven otherwise.
Complex Repetitive Discharge (CRD)
CRD: High-frequency (20–150 Hz), complex, repetitive polyphasic discharge with abrupt onset and abrupt cessation — the abruptness is pathognomonic. Sounds like a machine gun starting and stopping. Results from ephaptic (non-synaptic) coupling between adjacent chronically denervated or diseased fibres. Seen in chronic neuropathies and chronic myopathies.
Myotonic Discharge
Myotonic discharge: The pathognomonic finding of myotonic disorders. A run of potentials that waxes and wanes in both amplitude and frequency — the "dive-bomber" sound is unmistakeable. Caused by prolonged muscle membrane depolarisation due to abnormal chloride or sodium channel function. Seen in myotonic dystrophy 1 and 2, myotonia congenita, paramyotonia, and acid maltase deficiency.

MUAP Analysis — 15 min
Reading Motor Unit Action Potentials

With minimal voluntary contraction, individual MUAPs can be analysed. Three parameters are assessed — and the changes in each parameter point to different pathological processes.

Normal MUAP
Normal MUAP: triphasic (initial positive, large negative, small positive), duration 8–15 ms, amplitude 0.3–5 mV, ≤4 phases. The negative spike reflects synchronous firing of fibres within the motor unit's territory.
Neurogenic MUAP (Large, Polyphasic)
Neurogenic/reinnervated MUAP: Large amplitude (>5 mV), long duration (>15 ms), polyphasic (>4 phases). Reflects a motor unit that has adopted orphaned muscle fibres from a dead neighbour through collateral sprouting — its territory is now larger. Indicates chronic neurogenic disease: radiculopathy, neuropathy, ALS.
Myopathic MUAP (Small, Polyphasic)
Myopathic MUAP: Short duration (<5 ms), low amplitude, polyphasic. In myopathy, individual muscle fibres within a motor unit drop out (necrosis, fibrosis) — the motor unit territory shrinks and its remaining fibres fire asynchronously. Many MUAPs are recruited for a small force output (early recruitment pattern).
Recruitment — The Other Half of MUAP Analysis
Recruitment describes how many motor units fire as contraction force increases. Normal: MUs recruited in order of size (Henneman's size principle); rate increases before new MUs are added.

Reduced recruitment (neurogenic): fewer MUs available. Each surviving MU fires at high frequency to compensate. A single MUAP firing at >20 Hz alone is "reduced recruitment." Seen in neuropathy, radiculopathy, ALS.

Early recruitment (myopathic): all MUs are present but weak. Many MUs recruited at low force to achieve minimal movement. A full interference pattern at minimal effort is "early recruitment." Seen in myopathies.

  • B. Chronic neurogenic process with active denervation and reinnervation — reduced recruitment
  • C. Normal EMG study

Pattern Recognition

Once residents can read individual NCS and EMG parameters, they need to synthesise them into diagnostic patterns. These six patterns cover the vast majority of neuromuscular presentations encountered on rounds.

Teach this lesson using a summary table from a real EMG report — with the diagnosis blinded. Have residents classify it into one of the six patterns before revealing the impression. This is the only way to develop the synthetic reasoning the 4-question framework demands.

NCS Findings

Reduced CMAP and SNAP amplitudes, often length-dependent (worse distally). Conduction velocity normal or mildly slowed (>70% LLN). Distal latencies normal or mildly prolonged. No conduction block. Sural nerve (pure sensory) often most affected early.

Needle EMG Findings

Fibrillations + PSWs in distal muscles (if acute/subacute). Large polyphasic MUAPs with reduced recruitment in chronic disease. Findings worst distally — tibialis anterior, gastrocnemius before proximal muscles.

Clinical Correlation
Most common causes: diabetic sensorimotor neuropathy (length-dependent), toxic (alcohol, chemotherapy, heavy metals), hereditary axonal (CMT2 — NEFL, MFN2), nutritional (B12 deficiency). The key question: is it predominantly sensory, motor, or both? Pure motor axonal neuropathy is concerning for multifocal motor neuropathy (MMN) or ALS.
NCS: ↓ Velocity + ↑ Latency + Temporal Dispersion

NCS Findings

Markedly slowed conduction velocities (<70% LLN or <38 m/s upper limb, <30 m/s lower limb). Prolonged distal latencies (>130% ULN). Prolonged F-wave latencies. Conduction block and/or temporal dispersion. CMAP amplitude may be relatively preserved early (axons intact).

Needle EMG Findings

Often normal early in pure demyelination (axons intact — no denervation). If axonal degeneration occurs secondarily, fibrillations develop. MUAPs may show reduced recruitment if conduction block is severe. No large polyphasic MUAPs unless secondary axon loss and reinnervation occur.

Clinical Correlation
Acquired demyelinating: GBS (acute, symmetric, proximal + distal, albumino-cytological dissociation in CSF), CIDP (chronic, symmetric, responds to treatment), MMN (asymmetric, pure motor, anti-GM1 antibodies). Hereditary demyelinating: CMT1A (PMP22 duplication, uniform slowing), Dejerine-Sottas (severe infantile onset). The acquired vs hereditary distinction matters enormously — acquired demyelinating neuropathies are treatable.
NCS: Normal SNAP (proximal to DRG)
Needle EMG: Paraspinal Fibs (Key!)

NCS Findings

CMAP amplitude may be mildly reduced if severe motor root involvement. SNAP amplitudes are normal (lesion proximal to DRG — sensory axons peripheral to DRG remain intact). This normal SNAP in the context of clinical sensory symptoms is the hallmark of radiculopathy vs plexopathy. F-wave may be prolonged or absent if the affected root is involved.

Needle EMG Findings

Fibrillations + PSWs in muscles sharing the affected myotome — in both limb AND paraspinal muscles. Paraspinal fibrillations localise the lesion to the root level proximal to the posterior primary ramus. Needle EMG is the most sensitive test for radiculopathy and changes management when imaging is equivocal.

Repetitive Nerve Stimulation (RNS)

MG (postsynaptic AChR): Low-frequency RNS (3 Hz) → decremental response ≥10% (CMAP amplitude falls with successive stimuli). High-frequency RNS (50 Hz) → no increment (or small increment). Post-exercise facilitation may occur transiently.

LEMS (presynaptic VGCC): Low-frequency RNS → decrement. High-frequency RNS (50 Hz) → marked INCREMENT ≥100% (presynaptic calcium accumulates). CMAP amplitude at baseline is reduced (unlike MG).

Needle EMG + SFEMG Findings

Standard needle EMG may show varying MUAP morphology. Single-fibre EMG (SFEMG) is the most sensitive test: increased jitter (variability in neuromuscular transmission) ± blocking. Jitter >55 µs = abnormal. SFEMG is abnormal in 95% of MG (even seronegative) and 100% of LEMS. No fibrillations, no large polyphasic MUAPs.

MG vs LEMS — The Critical Distinction
Both produce fatigable weakness and NMJ dysfunction. Key differentiators: LEMS has areflexia that transiently improves with exercise (post-tetanic potentiation), ocular sparing is common (unlike MG), and is paraneoplastic in 50–60% (SCLC). LEMS CMAP amplitude is reduced at baseline — this differentiates it from MG on standard NCS. The high-frequency RNS increment is the electrophysiological signature of LEMS.
Myopathic MUAP — Short · Low · Polyphasic
Early Recruitment — Many MUAPs at Low Force

NCS Findings

NCS is typically normal or near-normal in pure myopathy — nerves are unaffected. CMAP amplitudes may be mildly reduced in severe myopathy (few surviving motor fibres to activate) but latency and conduction velocity are normal. Sensory NCS is entirely normal. This is an important distinguishing feature from neuropathy.

Needle EMG Findings

Short-duration, low-amplitude, polyphasic MUAPs. Early recruitment (full interference pattern at minimal effort — many weak units recruited early). Fibrillations + PSWs present in active inflammatory myopathies (PM, DM, IBM, necrotising myopathy). CRDs present in chronic myopathies. Myotonic discharges in DM1/2, myotonia congenita.

Clinical Correlation
Proximal weakness + normal NCS + myopathic EMG = myopathy. Elevate CK. The inflammatory myopathies (PM, DM, IBM, necrotising autoimmune myopathy) show fibrillations because individual fibres undergo necrosis and become transiently denervated. IBM has a unique mixed pattern — myopathic MUAPs + large neurogenic MUAPs (secondary neurogenic component). Absence of fibrillations in a myopathic EMG suggests a non-inflammatory/non-necrotising process.

NCS Findings

Motor NCS: mildly reduced CMAP amplitudes as motor axons are lost. Sensory NCS: completely normal — ALS is a pure motor neuron disease. Normal SNAPs in a patient with profound weakness and fasciculations is the electrophysiological signature of ALS.

Needle EMG — Awaji Criteria

Active denervation (fibrillations + PSWs) + chronic neurogenic changes (large polyphasic MUAPs, reduced recruitment) in ≥2 of 4 regions (bulbar, cervical, thoracic, lumbar). Fasciculations can substitute for fibrillations in meeting Awaji criteria. Paraspinal fibrillations at thoracic level are highly specific for ALS. The combination of upper AND lower motor neuron signs on clinical exam + EMG denervation in multiple regions = ALS until proven otherwise.

The ALS EMG Rule
Normal sensory NCS + widespread active denervation (multiple regions, including paraspinals) + large polyphasic MUAPs + fasciculations + normal (or near-normal) conduction velocities = ALS until proven otherwise. The most common mimics that must be excluded: multifocal motor neuropathy (conduction block on NCS, anti-GM1 Ab), Kennedy disease (SBMA — sensory involvement on NCS), and multifocal radiculopathy (nerve root disease, paraspinal fibrillations limited to one level).

Lesson 05 · 40 minutes

Key Diseases on EMG

These are the diagnoses residents will encounter on neuromuscular consults and rounds. For each one, they should know the specific EMG findings that confirm or support the diagnosis and the one finding that changes management urgently.

Focal Neuropathies — 10 min
CTS, Ulnar, and Peroneal — The Three Compressions You Must Know
Carpal Tunnel Syndrome (CTS)▼

Key NCS finding: Prolonged median distal motor latency (>4.4 ms) and/or prolonged median sensory peak latency (>3.7 ms), with normal ulnar sensory latency. The median-ulnar sensory latency difference (>0.5 ms) is the most sensitive comparator. Conduction velocity across the wrist is slowed; conduction above the wrist is normal.

Needle EMG: Normal in mild-moderate CTS. Fibrillations in APB (abductor pollicis brevis) = severe CTS with axon loss — indicates surgical urgency. The EMG tells you whether the patient needs observation/splinting or urgent decompression.

The one finding that changes management: Fibrillations in APB on needle EMG = axon loss = surgical referral urgently. Do not delay with conservative measures.

Ulnar Neuropathy at the Elbow▼

Key NCS finding: Slowed ulnar conduction velocity across the elbow segment (<50 m/s, or >10 m/s slower than the forearm segment). Conduction block at the elbow (CMAP amplitude drop >20% across the elbow). Reduced ulnar sensory amplitude to the little finger.

Localisation pitfall: Stimulating too close above and below the elbow gives falsely slowed velocity — use a 10 cm segment across the elbow minimum. Inching studies (1 cm increments) localise the focal point of block/slowing within the cubital tunnel.

Needle EMG: Denervation in first dorsal interosseous (FDI) and ADM (ADM innervated by ulnar), with sparing of FCU (flexor carpi ulnaris, innervated by ulnar but above elbow). FCU sparing with FDI denervation = lesion below FCU branch takeoff, consistent with cubital tunnel.

Common Peroneal Neuropathy at the Fibular Head▼

Key NCS finding: Peroneal CMAP amplitude drop and/or focal conduction slowing across the fibular head. Short-segment stimulation (2 cm increments across fibular head) identifies the focal lesion. Tibial motor NCS and sural sensory NCS are normal — this localises the lesion to the peroneal nerve specifically.

Needle EMG: Denervation in tibialis anterior (deep peroneal) and peroneus longus (superficial peroneal) with sparing of short head of biceps femoris (sciatic-innervated peroneal division, above the knee). Short head biceps femoris sparing = lesion at fibular head, not sciatic nerve. Short head biceps femoris denervation = sciatic neuropathy or L5 root.

The localisation rule: Fibular head compression (foot drop from leg crossing, positioning) = short head BF normal. High sciatic or L5 root = short head BF denervated.

GBS — The EMG-Urgent Scenario
Absent F-waves bilaterally in the context of ascending weakness, areflexia, and CSF albumino-cytological dissociation = GBS until proven otherwise. Repetitive Nerve Stimulation — 8 min
Reading RNS for MG and LEMS
RNS at 3 Hz — Myasthenia Gravis (Decrement)
MG RNS: Each successive stimulus at 3 Hz produces a smaller CMAP because acetylcholine quanta are progressively depleted at the postsynaptic AChR. A decrement ≥10% from the first to the fourth stimulus is abnormal. The fifth often stabilises (safety factor partially maintained). Most sensitive in proximal muscles (trapezius, nasalis) and with post-exercise testing.
RNS at 50 Hz — Lambert-Eaton (Increment)
LEMS RNS: Baseline CMAP amplitude is reduced (fewer quanta released per stimulus — presynaptic VGCC deficiency). High-frequency stimulation (50 Hz) causes calcium accumulation in the presynaptic terminal, transiently restoring quantal release — CMAP amplitude increments ≥100%. This increment is the electrophysiological signature of presynaptic NMJ failure.
A 65-year-old smoker presents with proximal leg weakness and areflexia. RNS at 3 Hz shows 15% decrement. RNS at 50 Hz shows 180% increment. Baseline CMAP amplitude of the peroneal nerve is 1.2 mV (normal >3 mV). What is the diagnosis and what must be urgently excluded?
  • A. Myasthenia gravis — start pyridostigmine
  • B. Lambert-Eaton Myasthenic Syndrome — urgently exclude small cell lung cancer
  • C. GBS — start IVIG
  • D. Inflammatory myopathy — start steroids
Lesson 06 · 45 minutes

Case-Based Consolidation

Four cases requiring full synthesis of NCS and needle EMG findings. Present the clinical picture and the NCS/EMG table. Ask residents to answer the four questions in order before offering any interpretation.

For each case: present the history, ask "What are you expecting to find on EMG based on the clinical picture?" — then show the data and ask them to work through the 4-question framework systematically. Correct any short-cuts. The goal is to make the 4-question framework as automatic as the Salzburg criteria or the Lindegaard Ratio.
Case 01
62F · Progressive Distal Leg Numbness and Weakness

History: 62-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes for 18 years. Gradually progressive bilateral foot numbness, worse at night, over 3 years. Now noticing difficulty walking on heels (foot drop). Exam: reduced vibration and pinprick to mid-shin bilaterally, absent ankle jerks, mild bilateral toe extensor weakness.

→ Reveal Interpretation & Report Language

Q1 (Neurogenic vs myopathic): Neurogenic — fibrillations, PSWs, and large polyphasic MUAPs with reduced recruitment.

Q2 (Axonal vs demyelinating): Axonal — reduced SNAP and CMAP amplitudes, normal conduction velocity (44 m/s, above 40 m/s LLN). No prolonged latencies. No conduction block.

Q3 (Distribution): Length-dependent, symmetric, sensorimotor. Distal (sural, peroneal) worse than proximal (tibial CMAP preserved). Proximal muscles (vastus lateralis) normal. Hands mildly involved (median SNAP reduced).

Q4 (Timing): Chronic — large polyphasic MUAPs indicate reinnervation (weeks–months process). Active denervation still present (fibrillations, PSWs), meaning ongoing axon loss.

Report impression: "Electrodiagnostic findings are consistent with a chronic, length-dependent, symmetric, sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy with active denervation in distal lower extremity muscles, most severely affecting gastrocnemius. The pattern is most consistent with diabetic sensorimotor polyneuropathy. No evidence of superimposed compressive neuropathy. Ongoing axon loss is indicated by active denervation — optimisation of glycaemic control and risk factor management is recommended."

Case 02
38M · Acute Ascending Weakness over 10 Days

History: 38-year-old man, preceded by GI illness 3 weeks ago. Now unable to walk, bilateral facial weakness, absent reflexes throughout. FVC 62% on admission, declining. Lumbar puncture: protein 98 mg/dL, WBC 2 cells. Neurology urgently consulted for EMG.

Before data — ask residents: What is the single most important thing to assess before starting treatment? Should we wait for EMG to be dramatically abnormal before treating? Pause.

→ Reveal Interpretation

EMG diagnosis: Early demyelinating neuropathy — absent F-waves bilaterally, mildly prolonged latencies, borderline slowed CV, normal needle EMG (no axon loss yet). This is early AIDP (GBS).

Critical teaching point: The needle EMG is normal — no fibrillations, normal MUAPs. This does NOT mean the study is normal. Absent F-waves + clinical picture = GBS. The EMG may be near-normal in the first 1–2 weeks of GBS because the process is pure demyelination and axon degeneration has not yet occurred (fibrillations take 2–3 weeks to develop after denervation).

Management decision: FVC 62% and declining = impending respiratory failure. Do not wait for a "more positive" EMG. Start IVIG (2 g/kg over 5 days) immediately. Admit to ICU-level monitoring. Serial FVC, NIF, and bulbar function assessment. Intubation threshold: FVC <20 mL/kg, NIF <−25 cmH₂O, or clinical deterioration.

The EMG changes management not by being dramatic, but by being consistent with the diagnosis and providing confidence to treat urgently.

Thoracic paraspinal fibrillations are present. These findings meet Awaji criteria for definite electrodiagnostic support of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. Urgent neuromuscular specialist referral is strongly recommended."

Case 04
34F · Proximal Arm Weakness After Shoulder Surgery

History: 34-year-old woman, 6 weeks after right shoulder arthroscopy performed in lateral decubitus position. Woke from anaesthesia with right hand numbness and weakness. Now 6 weeks post-op — right hand grip 3/5, right thumb extension weak, right ring and little finger numbness. Median and ulnar territories both affected on the right. No neck pain, no Horner's.

Before data — ask: Median AND ulnar territory involvement on one side following a positional procedure is never a single nerve. What structure could explain both? What NCS finding would localise to plexus vs roots? Pause.

Localisation step by step:

1. Absent SNAPs (median + ulnar + radial) bilaterally: The sensory cell bodies (DRG) are intact but their peripheral processes have degenerated → lesion is DISTAL to the DRG → plexus or peripheral nerve, not root.

2. Normal cervical paraspinals: No fibrillations at root level → the lesion is not at the root. Paraspinals are innervated by the posterior primary ramus which branches proximal to the brachial plexus — normal paraspinals confirm the lesion is distal to the root.

3. Distribution — median, ulnar, AND radial territories: No single peripheral nerve explains all three. The medial cord (median/ulnar) AND posterior cord (radial) are both affected → lower trunk or medial + posterior cord brachial plexopathy.

4. Context: Lateral decubitus positioning during shoulder surgery with stretch of the brachial plexus against the operating table or arm board = iatrogenic brachial plexopathy (lower trunk/medial cord predominant). No Horner's (T1 preganglionic fibres not affected, or lesion distal to sympathetic fibres).

Prognosis: Axonal plexopathy with significant SNAP loss — recovery is slower and less complete than pure demyelinating plexopathy. Physical therapy + serial EMG at 3–6 months to track reinnervation. Discuss prognosis honestly — full recovery may take 12–24 months and may be incomplete.

The Three Rules They Must Never Forget
1. Normal SNAPs in a patient with sensory symptoms = lesion proximal to the DRG (root, cord, brain) — not a peripheral nerve problem.
2. Fibrillations + PSWs require 2–3 weeks to develop after denervation — a negative needle EMG in the first two weeks does not exclude an acute nerve injury.
3. Thoracic paraspinal fibrillations are red-flag for ALS — no disc herniation, no radiculopathy explains them bilaterally at a thoracic level. When you find them, call neuromuscular.

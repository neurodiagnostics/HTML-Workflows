Step 1 · Clinical Context and Suspected Presentation
CIDP is a chronic autoimmune demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy. Diagnosis rests on a combination of clinical, electrodiagnostic, and laboratory features with exclusions. Select the presentation type before proceeding.
Background (Van den Bergh et al. 2021):
Key 2021 revision changes from 2010: "Atypical CIDP" replaced by "CIDP variants" (well-characterised entities); diagnostic certainty levels reduced from three (definite/probable/possible) to two (CIDP/possible CIDP) — sensitivity/specificity of probable vs. definite did not significantly differ; sensory nerve conduction studies now mandatory; autoimmune nodopathies reclassified as a distinct separate disease

2021 criteria performance: Sensitivity 77–83%, specificity 94–98% across validation cohorts. Up to 20% of patients with clinically typical CIDP may not fulfil minimal electrodiagnostic criteria and require supportive criteria

Key principle: Misdiagnosis in CIDP is common, particularly in variants. The criteria aim to improve specificity. Patients fulfilling criteria based on correctly interpreted results may still not have CIDP.
Presentation type REQUIRED
Which presentation pattern best fits this patient?
Typical CIDP: Progressive or relapsing, symmetric, proximal AND distal muscle weakness of upper and lower limbs + sensory involvement ≥2 limbs, developing over ≥8 weeks, absent/reduced tendon reflexes in all limbs.

CIDP variants (one of the following):
— Distal CIDP (DADS): Distal sensory loss + muscle weakness predominantly in lower limbs. ~2/3 have IgM paraproteinaemia. Anti-MAG neuropathy is outside CIDP scope if confirmed.
— Multifocal CIDP (Lewis-Sumner/MADSAM): Sensory loss + weakness in multifocal asymmetric pattern, upper limb predominant, >1 limb. Cranial nerve involvement more common.
— Focal CIDP: Sensory loss + weakness in only one limb; usually brachial or lumbosacral plexus.
— Motor CIDP: Motor symptoms and signs without sensory involvement. May deteriorate with corticosteroids — IVIg preferred as first-line.
— Sensory CIDP: Sensory symptoms and signs without motor involvement. In ~70% of patients, weakness develops during long-term follow-up.

Not CIDP (separate diseases): Autoimmune nodopathy (anti-NF155, anti-CNTN1, anti-Caspr1, anti-NF140/186) and chronic immune sensory polyradiculopathy (CISP — normal motor and sensory NCS, absent/proximal delayed SSEPs) are both excluded from CIDP classification.
Acute-onset CIDP (A-CIDP): Up to 13% of CIDP patients present acutely within 4 weeks and may initially be diagnosed as GBS. 5% of patients diagnosed with GBS are later reclassified as A-CIDP. Distinguishing features favouring A-CIDP: able to walk independently, less facial weakness, less respiratory/ANS involvement, more sensory signs, continues to deteriorate >8 weeks OR relapses ≥3 times after initial improvement.
Step 2 · Clinical Diagnostic Criteria (Table 1)
Clinical criteria are formulated as consensus expert opinion. These are the mandatory clinical features required before electrodiagnostic criteria are applied.
Clinical criteria (Table 1, Van den Bergh et al. 2021) — requirements differ by variant.
Typical CIDP requires ALL of: (1) Progressive or relapsing, symmetric, proximal and distal muscle weakness of upper and lower limbs + sensory involvement in ≥2 limbs; (2) Developing over ≥8 weeks; (3) Absent or reduced tendon reflexes in ALL limbs
CIDP variants: One of the variant-specific patterns (see Step 1) — otherwise as in typical CIDP, but tendon reflexes may be normal in unaffected limbs
Time course REQUIRED
Symptom progression: developing over ≥8 weeks (or relapsing-remitting course)
The ≥8-week criterion distinguishes CIDP from GBS (which peaks by 4 weeks). A relapsing-remitting course also qualifies. Acute-onset CIDP (A-CIDP) presents within 4 weeks but either continues to worsen beyond 8 weeks OR relapses ≥3 times — the 8-week criterion is therefore ultimately met by these patients. There is no upper limit on disease duration at presentation.
Weakness pattern REQUIRED
Weakness pattern consistent with selected phenotype
Typical: Symmetric proximal + distal weakness in upper and lower limbs with sensory involvement ≥2 limbs
Distal: Weakness predominantly distal, lower limb > upper limb
Multifocal: Asymmetric, multifocal, upper limb predominant, >1 limb involved
Focal: Weakness restricted to one limb (brachial or lumbosacral plexus, or individual peripheral nerve)
Motor: Symmetric proximal/distal weakness without clinically detectable sensory involvement
Sensory: Sensory symptoms and signs (vibration, position sense, cutaneous sensation) without motor weakness — gait ataxia common
Tendon reflexes REQUIRED
Absent or reduced tendon reflexes in affected limbs
For typical CIDP, reflexes must be absent or reduced in ALL limbs. For CIDP variants, reflexes may be normal in unaffected limbs — reduced reflexes are required only in affected limbs. Normal reflexes throughout in a patient with suspected typical CIDP significantly weakens the diagnosis.
Step 3 · Electrodiagnostic Criteria (Tables 2 & 3, PICO 1)
The Task Force strongly recommends electrodiagnosis to support the clinical diagnosis. Both motor and sensory NCS are now required. The 2021 criteria define two tiers: CIDP-level and possible CIDP-level electrodiagnostic support.
Motor conduction criteria — strongly supportive of demyelination (Table 2): At least one of (a)–(g) in the required number of nerves:
(a) Distal motor latency prolongation ≥50% above ULN in ≥2 nerves
(b) Motor conduction velocity reduction ≥30% below LLN in ≥2 nerves
(c) F-wave latency prolongation ≥20% above ULN in ≥2 nerves (≥50% if distal CMAP <80% LLN)
(d) Absent F-waves in ≥2 nerves (if CMAP ≥20% LLN) + ≥1 other demyelinating parameter in ≥1 other nerve
(e) Motor conduction block: ≥30% proximal/distal CMAP amplitude reduction in ≥2 nerves (or 1 nerve + ≥1 other parameter)
(f) Abnormal temporal dispersion: >30% CMAP duration increase in ≥2 nerves (≥100% in tibial)
(g) Distal CMAP duration prolongation in ≥1 nerve + ≥1 other parameter

Weakly supportive: As above but in only 1 nerve
Key note: Skin temperature ≥33°C palm, ≥30°C lateral malleolus. Carpal tunnel syndrome must be excluded for distal median latency. Study median, ulnar (below elbow), peroneal (below fibular head), and tibial nerves on one side first; extend if criteria unmet.
Motor nerve conduction result REQUIRED
Motor NCS demyelination criteria
How many nerves meet motor demyelination criteria (any of a–g)?

Required for full CIDP-level EDx support (by variant):
— Typical, Multifocal, Focal, Motor CIDP: ≥2 nerves meeting criteria
— Distal CIDP: ≥2 upper limb nerves (distal CMAP ≥1 mV); criteria in lower limb only → possible distal CIDP only
— Sensory CIDP: motor conduction must be NORMAL in ≥4 nerves (median, ulnar, peroneal, tibial)

For "possible CIDP" level: criteria met in only 1 motor nerve (for most variants)

Recommendation 1 (Typical CIDP, PICO 1): Sensitivity/specificity of 2010 EFNS/PNS electrodiagnostic criteria: 95%/96% (Tackenberg et al.), 81%/96% (Rajabally et al.), 73%/91% (Breiner et al.) across different patient populations.
Proximal stimulation guidance: If criteria not met with standard 4-nerve protocol, extend to contralateral side and/or stimulate ulnar/median at axilla and Erb's point. Between Erb's point and wrist: ≥50% CMAP amplitude reduction required for conduction block. Exclude Martin-Gruber anastomosis for ulnar forearm conduction block.
Sensory nerve conduction result REQUIRED
Sensory NCS result (Table 3)
CIDP-level: Sensory conduction abnormalities (prolonged distal latency, reduced SNAP amplitude, or slowed conduction velocity outside normal limits) in ≥2 nerves.

Possible CIDP: Same criteria apply in ≥2 nerves.

Sensory CIDP with normal motor NCS additionally requires one of:
(a) Sensory NCV <80% LLN (for SNAP >80% LLN) or <70% LLN (for SNAP <80% LLN) in ≥2 nerves (median, ulnar, radial, sural)
(b) Sural sparing pattern — abnormal median or radial SNAP with normal sural SNAP (excluding CTS)

Note: For sural nerve SNAP amplitude, age-dependent reference values should be used after age 60.
Important limitation: Sensory CIDP cannot be more than a "possible" diagnosis based on clinical and electrophysiological criteria alone, because these criteria do not permit identification of normal reference values compatible with sensory nerve demyelination. Sensory CIDP requires supportive criteria to approach diagnostic certainty.
Step 4 · Supportive Criteria (PICOs 2–4, 6)
Supportive criteria upgrade a possible CIDP diagnosis to CIDP when combined with clinical criteria. Two supportive criteria are required to upgrade. They may also support the diagnosis in uncertain cases.
When are supportive criteria required?
CIDP (motor criteria met in ≥2 nerves): No supportive criteria required
Possible CIDP (criteria met in 1 nerve): ≥2 supportive criteria required to upgrade to CIDP diagnosis
Special case: Motor conduction abnormalities not meeting demyelination criteria + sensory abnormalities in ≥2 nerves → possible CIDP only if also objective response to treatment + 1 other supportive criterion

Supportive criteria include: (a) response to treatment, (b) ultrasound, (c) MRI, (d) CSF protein, (e) nerve biopsy
Treatment response (PICO 2)
Objective response to immunomodulatory therapy (IVIg, plasma exchange, or corticosteroids)
Objective response requires improvement on ≥1 disability AND ≥1 impairment scale. A positive response is not specific for CIDP. Up to 32% non-responders to first-line therapy may still have CIDP. Uncontrolled studies report positive response in 68–99% of patients (Viala et al., Cocito et al., Chan et al.).

Validated improvement thresholds (from clinical trials):
— I-RODS: +≥4 centile points | INCAT disability scale: −≥1 point
— mISS: −≥2 points | MRC sum score (0–60): +≥2 to 4 points
— Grip strength (Martin Vigorimeter): +≥8 to 14 kPa | Jamar dynamometer: +≥10% (averaged over 3 consecutive days)
Caution: Lack of improvement does not exclude CIDP. Current immunomodulatory treatments are not CIDP-specific — other autoimmune conditions may also respond. Treatment response must be interpreted in the clinical and electrophysiological context to avoid over-diagnosis.
Nerve ultrasound (PICO 3)
Nerve ultrasound — enlargement at ≥2 proximal sites (adult patients only)
The Task Force suggested ultrasound in adult patients fulfilling possible CIDP but not CIDP criteria. Validated cut-offs for nerve enlargement in CIDP:
— Median nerve: >10 mm² at forearm; >13 mm² upper arm; >9 mm² interscalene (trunks)
— Nerve roots: >12 mm² cross-sectional area

Enlargement mainly of proximal nerve segments in arm nerves and spinal nerve roots is the most characteristic CIDP finding. A prospective multicentre validation study (Herraets et al. 2020, Neurology) confirmed the yield of a brachial plexus + proximal median nerve bilateral protocol.

Before concluding ultrasound abnormalities support CIDP, exclude: MMN, CMT1, IgM paraproteinaemic neuropathy (anti-MAG), POEMS syndrome, diabetic radiculoplexus neuropathy, amyloid neuropathy, neuralgic amyotrophy, leprosy, neurofibromatosis, neurolymphomatosis.
Paediatric caution: No evidence supports ultrasound in paediatric CIDP. Inherited demyelinating neuropathies are more prevalent in children, rater experience is limited, and validated cut-offs based on disease controls are lacking. Not recommended in children.
MRI nerve roots / plexus (PICO 3)
MRI — enlargement and/or T2 signal increase of spinal nerve roots (adult patients only)
MRI preferred for nerve root assessment when ultrasound is unavailable or non-contributory. T2-weighted sequences (DIXON/STIR, coronal + sagittal planes). Preferred quantitative cut-off: nerve root diameter (measured next to ganglion, coronal plane) >5 mm. Two studies found low inter-rater reliability in chronic inflammatory neuropathies even among experienced raters — low reproducibility and absence of validated cut-offs limit routine use. High cost also limits utility. MRI is NOT recommended as routine first-line investigation.
CSF analysis (PICO 4)
CSF protein elevation (without cellular pleocytosis)
CSF protein is often elevated in CIDP (sensitivity 42–77%, specificity uncertain). The Task Force suggests NOT performing CSF if diagnostic criteria for CIDP are already met. Consider CSF when: (1) possible CIDP but not CIDP criteria, (2) acute/subacute onset, (3) infectious/malignant aetiology suspected.

Age-adjusted cut-offs: ≥0.5 g/L under age 50; >0.6 g/L over age 50 (sensitivity 68% in one study). Interpret cautiously with diabetes or prior spinal surgery. Risk of misdiagnosis if electrodiagnosis is non-confirmatory and only CSF protein is elevated.
Nerve biopsy (PICO 6)
Nerve biopsy — supportive pathological features (only when all other investigations non-diagnostic)
NOT recommended as routine. Reserve for: (1) suspected CIDP not confirmable by other means; (2) little/no response to treatment and alternative diagnosis (CMT, amyloidosis, sarcoidosis, nerve sheath tumour) under consideration.

Supporting pathological features for CIDP: thinly myelinated axons and small onion bulbs; thinly myelinated or demyelinated internodes on teased fibre analysis; perivascular macrophage clusters; supportive demyelination features on electron microscopy.

Sensitivity and specificity are poor. Most often sural or superficial peroneal nerve biopsied; biopsy of a clinically affected nerve is preferred. Sural nerve biopsy is associated with numbness, acute pain, allodynia, chronic pain, dysaesthesia, neuroma formation, infection, and wound dehiscence.
Step 5 · Serological Testing (PICOs 5 & 7)
Monoclonal protein testing is mandatory in all adults with suspected CIDP. Nodal-paranodal antibody testing should be considered when specific clinical features are present or when there is poor treatment response.
Monoclonal gammopathy testing (PICO 7) — strongly advised in all adult patients:
Testing should include serum protein electrophoresis + immunofixation + spot urine immunofixation for Bence Jones protein ± serum free light chains (SFLC).
If IgM paraprotein found: Test anti-MAG antibodies (Bühlmann ELISA or locally validated assay). Anti-MAG titre >7000 BTU strongly implies a different diagnosis than CIDP — anti-MAG neuropathy is outside CIDP scope.
If POEMS syndrome suspected (distal + painful CIDP + lambda IgA/IgG paraprotein): Test serum VEGF levels.
If distal CIDP and no IgM paraprotein found: Consider repeat testing.
Monoclonal protein screen REQUIRED in adults
Serum/urine immunofixation result
Monoclonal gammopathies may mimic CIDP (anti-MAG IgM neuropathy, POEMS syndrome, multiple myeloma, AL-amyloidosis). CIDP associated with IgG/IgA MGUS may behave like typical CIDP. The independent diagnostic significance of a monoclonal gammopathy on CIDP diagnosis is limited — the gammopathy itself requires haematology-oncology consultation if found.
Anti-MAG antibodies (if IgM paraprotein present)
Anti-MAG antibody titre (Bühlmann ELISA)
Only relevant if IgM paraprotein is present. Anti-MAG neuropathy has specific electrodiagnostic and pathologic findings, predominantly does not respond to IVIg or corticosteroids, and is outside the scope of CIDP. High titre (>7000 BTU) strongly implies a different diagnosis. Low titre requires careful clinical interpretation. ~2/3 of patients with distal CIDP phenotype have IgM paraproteinaemic neuropathy.
Nodal-paranodal antibody testing (PICO 5)
Anti-NF155, anti-CNTN1, anti-Caspr1, anti-NF140/186 (cell-based assay + confirmatory ELISA)
Strongly consider testing when ANY of the following are present:
— Resistance to standard IVIg + corticosteroids
— Acute/subacute aggressive onset; previous GBS or A-CIDP diagnosis
— Low-frequency tremor, ataxia disproportionate to sensory involvement, other cerebellar features
— Predominantly distal weakness
— Respiratory failure + cranial nerve involvement
— Associated nephrotic syndrome
— Very high CSF protein

Antibody-specific features: Anti-CNTN1 → acute/subacute onset, motor/ataxic, poor IVIg response. Anti-NF155 → younger onset, distal weakness, ataxia, tremor, poor IVIg response. Anti-Caspr1 → ataxia, neuropathic pain, cranial nerve involvement, poor IVIg response. Anti-NF140/186 → severe phenotype, especially IgG3 isotype.

If positive → Autoimmune Nodopathy diagnosis (NOT CIDP). Rituximab may be effective (anecdotal evidence from independent cohorts), particularly for anti-NF155 and anti-CNTN1 IgG4 antibodies which target memory B cells/CD20+ short-lived plasma cells.
Testing method: Cell-based assay using mammalian expression vectors encoding human NF155, CNTN1, NF186/NF140, and Caspr1. Avoid N-terminal tags; avoid GFP-tagged vectors for CNTN1. Confirm positive results with ELISA (human recombinant proteins) or teased-nerve immunohistochemistry. Apply confirmatory testing for low titre sera or dubious staining to avoid false positives.

Red flags should prompt reconsideration and investigation of alternative diagnoses before confirming a CIDP diagnosis. Also document treatment plan — new FDA-approved therapies are available since the 2021 guideline.
Red flags that suggest a diagnosis other than CIDP (Flowchart 2, Van den Bergh et al. 2021): Any of the following should trigger consideration of alternative diagnoses from Tables 4 and 5. Multiple red flags make CIDP diagnosis substantially less likely.

Treatment plan (first-line) CLINICAL MANAGEMENT
Intended first-line induction treatment
Strong recommendations (Van den Bergh et al. 2021):
— IVIg or corticosteroids: strongly recommended (IVIg induction: 2 g/kg over 2–5 days; ICE trial showed 94% response to 2 g/kg induction + two 1 g/kg treatments at 3-week intervals, n=117 treated)
— Plasma exchange: strongly recommended if IVIg and corticosteroids ineffective (moderate certainty evidence, 2 trials, 59 participants: twice-weekly PE vs. sham)
— SCIg: strongly recommended for maintenance (PATH trial: high certainty evidence, n=172)
— IVIg weakly preferred over PE for ease of administration
— Motor CIDP: IVIg should be considered as initial treatment (corticosteroids may worsen motor CIDP)

FDA-approved novel therapy (June 2024): Efgartigimod alfa + hyaluronidase-qvfc (Vyvgart Hytrulo; Argenx) — first FcRn blocker approved for CIDP. Mechanism: reduces circulating IgG including pathogenic autoantibodies by blocking neonatal Fc receptor recycling. Dose: 1000 mg SC weekly. ADHERE trial (n=322): 66.5% of patients responded in open-label Stage A; in randomised Stage B, efgartigimod reduced relapse risk by 61% vs. placebo (HR 0.39; 95% CI 0.25–0.61; p=0.000039). 99% of eligible patients enrolled in open-label extension. First novel mechanism in CIDP in 30 years. Also EMA-approved.
Treatments to avoid: Interferon beta-1a (2 RCTs, n=87: no benefit, possible serious adverse events — strongly recommended against). Fingolimod (1 RCT, n=106: no benefit, moderate certainty — weakly recommended against). MS disease-modifying therapies are generally ineffective in CIDP. Methotrexate (1 RCT, n=60: primary endpoint not met — weakly recommended against).

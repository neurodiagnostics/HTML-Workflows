Step 1 · Basic Features — Mandatory Inclusion (B1)
All three B1 criteria must be present before the MDS-PSP criteria can be applied to any phenotype at any stage.
B1 Mandatory Inclusion (Table 1): Sporadic occurrence · Age ≥40 at onset · Gradual progression
Any new onset neurological, cognitive, or behavioral deficit that subsequently progresses — in the absence of another identifiable cause — counts as a PSP-related symptom. MAPT rare variants may lead to inherited phenocopies; MAPT carriers may have earlier onset.
B1-1 · Sporadic occurrence
PSP is classified as a sporadic disease. MAPT rare variants (mutations) may lead to inherited phenocopies with a Mendelian trait pattern — in that case, the case is classified as inherited, not sporadic. MAPT sequencing may be considered in cases where higher certainty is warranted or where a Mendelian inheritance pattern is suspected. LRRK2 and Parkin rare variants have been observed in autopsy-confirmed PSP, but their causal relationship is unclear.
A patient with a first- or second-degree relative with a PSP-like syndrome and Mendelian inheritance should undergo genetic counseling. Absence of a known family history does not guarantee sporadic occurrence in small families.
B1-2 · Age ≥40 years at onset of first PSP-related symptom
No autopsy-confirmed PSP case has been demonstrated to manifest before age 40. PSP look-alikes — particularly Niemann-Pick disease type C — may present earlier. If age at onset is <40, the B3 context-dependent exclusion for young-onset disease applies (Wilson's, NPC, hypoparathyroidism, neuroacanthocytosis, neurosyphilis). MAPT rare variant carriers may have earlier onset — this is an exception to the sporadic age rule.
B1-3 · Gradual progression of PSP-related symptoms
The disease course must be one of gradual progression, not sudden onset, stepwise deterioration, or rapid decline (which would suggest vascular, autoimmune, metabolic, or prion etiology). PSP-related symptoms include any new neurological, cognitive, or behavioral deficit that progresses during the clinical course in the absence of another identifiable cause. This broad definition reflects the wide spectrum of PSP presentations.
Step 2 · Exclusion Criteria (B2 Mandatory + B3 Context-Dependent)
B2 exclusions apply to every patient. B3 exclusions only apply when suggestive clinical features are present — do not apply B3 if the features are absent.
B2 · Mandatory Exclusion Criteria — Clinical APPLY TO ALL
B2-C1 · Predominant unexplained episodic memory impairment (suggestive of AD)
Predominant amnestic presentation — inability to recall recent events, rapid forgetting, with preserved procedural and frontal functions — suggestive of hippocampal/medial temporal pathology as in Alzheimer's disease. Executive dysfunction alone in PSP does not qualify. Must be otherwise unexplained.
B2-C2 · Predominant unexplained autonomic failure (suggestive of MSA or Lewy body disease)
Orthostatic hypotension (≥30 mmHg systolic or ≥15 mmHg diastolic after 3 minutes standing), urinary incontinence/retention, or other severe autonomic dysfunction disproportionate to the parkinsonian syndrome and otherwise unexplained. Mild autonomic features can occur in PSP.
B2-C3 · Predominant unexplained visual hallucinations or fluctuating alertness (suggestive of DLB)
Recurrent well-formed visual hallucinations or pronounced fluctuations in cognition/alertness suggesting Lewy body pathology. Ocular motor dysfunction-related misperceptions in PSP do not qualify. Must be otherwise unexplained.
B2-C4 · Predominant unexplained multisegmental upper and lower motor neuron signs (suggestive of MND)
Combined UMN and LMN signs in multiple body regions suggesting motor neuron disease. Note: Pure upper motor neuron signs are NOT an exclusion criterion — they can occur in PSP (e.g., PSP-PLS variant). Multisegmental MND pattern involving both UMN and LMN is the exclusion.
B2-C5 · Sudden onset or stepwise / rapid progression with corresponding imaging or laboratory findings (suggestive of vascular, autoimmune, metabolic, or prion etiology)
Sudden onset, clear stepwise worsening with corresponding MRI lesions (infarcts, signal changes), or very rapid progression with corresponding biomarker findings. Must be in conjunction with supporting imaging or laboratory evidence — not clinical features alone.
B2-C6 · History of encephalitis
A clear documented history of encephalitis — viral, bacterial, autoimmune — that could have produced the current neurological syndrome. Post-encephalitic parkinsonism can mimic PSP.
B2-C7 · Prominent appendicular ataxia
Prominent limb ataxia (not gait imbalance from postural instability) suggesting cerebellar pathology. PSP-C (cerebellar variant) is not included in MDS-PSP criteria due to insufficient clinicopathological evidence. Mild cerebellar signs are not an exclusion.
B2-C8 · Identifiable cause of postural instability (primary sensory deficit, vestibular, severe spasticity, or LMN syndrome)
If postural instability is fully explained by an identifiable cause (e.g., severe peripheral neuropathy causing sensory ataxia, Menière's disease, severe cervical myelopathy with spasticity), the postural instability criterion cannot be attributed to PSP.
B2 · Mandatory Exclusion Criteria — Imaging APPLY TO ALL
B2-I1 · Severe leukoencephalopathy on cerebral imaging
B2-I2 · Relevant structural abnormality (NPH, obstructive hydrocephalus, basal ganglia / brainstem infarctions, hemorrhages, tumors, malformations)
B3 · Context-Dependent Exclusions APPLY ONLY IF CLINICALLY INDICATED
Only assess these if the corresponding clinical features are present. If not applicable, mark N/A.
B3 overall assessment — Any context-dependent exclusion criteria triggered?
B3 exclusions to apply when clinically indicated:

Imaging: In sudden/stepwise onset — exclude stroke, CADASIL, severe CAA (DWI/FLAIR/T2*). In very rapid progression — exclude prion disease (DWI cortical/subcortical hyperintensities).

Laboratory: In PSP-CBS — exclude primary AD pathology (CSF tau/phospho-tau/Aβ42 or amyloid PET). In age <45 — exclude Wilson's, NPC, hypoparathyroidism, neuroacanthocytosis, neurosyphilis. In rapid progression — exclude prion disease (14-3-3, NSE, total tau >1,200 pg/mL, RT-QuIC) and paraneoplastic encephalitis (anti-Ma1/Ma2). In Whipple's features — PCR in CSF.

Genetics: Known rare variants excluding PSP include: C9orf72, GRN, FUS, TARDBP, VCP, CHMP2B (non-MAPT FTD); SYNJ1, GBA (PD); APP, PSEN1, PSEN2 (AD); NPC1/2; ATP13A2; DCTN1; POLG; ATN1; PRNP; HTT; ATXN1/2/3/7/17.
Step 3 · Domain O — Ocular Motor Dysfunction
Mark the highest certainty level present. Levels with lower numbers contribute higher diagnostic certainty. Only the highest level present in each domain is used in the diagnostic combination logic.
Ocular Motor Domain levels:
O1 (Level 1 — Highest): Vertical supranuclear gaze palsy
O2 (Level 2): Slow velocity of vertical saccades
O3 (Level 3 — Lowest): Frequent macro square wave jerks OR eyelid opening apraxia
O1 · Vertical supranuclear gaze palsy
Level 1
Clear limitation of the range of voluntary gaze in the vertical more than horizontal plane, affecting both up- and downgaze (more than expected for age), which is overcome by activation with the vestibulo-ocular reflex (doll's-eye maneuver). At later stages, VOR may be lost or the maneuver prevented by nuchal rigidity.
Downgaze palsy is more specific for PSP than upgaze palsy, which is common with aging. The VOR preservation distinguishes supranuclear from nuclear or infranuclear gaze palsies. Incomplete downgaze restriction with VOR override still qualifies if clearly beyond age-expected limitation.
O2 · Slow velocity of vertical saccades
Level 2
Decreased velocity (and amplitude) of vertical greater than horizontal saccadic eye movements. May be established by quantitative infrared oculography or bedside testing. Gaze assessed by command ("Look at the flicking finger"), not pursuit. Target must be >20° from primary gaze. Saccades must be slow enough for the examiner to see the eye movement (rotation), not just initial and final positions. Delay in saccade initiation is NOT slowing. Supported by slowed or absent fast components of vertical optokinetic nystagmus.
Bedside assessment: Ask patient to make rapid refixations between two targets (examiner's finger and thumb) held >20° apart vertically. Normal saccades are too fast to see movement. In PSP, the examiner can track the eye rotating between targets. Compare vertical vs. horizontal — vertical should be disproportionately affected.
O3 · Frequent macro square wave jerks OR eyelid opening apraxia
Level 3
Macro square wave jerks: Rapid involuntary saccadic intrusions during fixation, displacing the eye horizontally from primary position and returning it after 200–300 ms. Most SWJ are <1° and rare in health, but up to 3–4° and frequent (>10/min) in PSP.

"Eyelid opening apraxia": Inability to voluntarily initiate eyelid opening after a period of lid closure, in the absence of involuntary forced lid closure (blepharospasm). Often attributed to pretarsal orbicularis activation (pretarsal blepharospasm) rather than true apraxia.
No ocular motor features from this domain
Step 4 · Domain P — Postural Instability
All postural instability features require onset within 3 years of first PSP-related symptom. Mark the highest certainty level present.
Postural Instability Domain levels:
P1 (Level 1): Repeated unprovoked falls within 3 years
P2 (Level 2): Tendency to fall on pull-test within 3 years
P3 (Level 3): More than two steps backward on pull-test (but unaided recovery) within 3 years
P1 · Repeated unprovoked falls within 3 years of PSP-related symptom onset
Level 1
Spontaneous loss of balance while standing, OR history of more than one unprovoked fall, within 3 years after onset of PSP-related features. Falls must be attributable to postural instability, not other causes (e.g., syncope, seizure, orthopedic). This is the most diagnostically powerful postural instability criterion — early spontaneous falls are the hallmark of PSP-Richardson's syndrome.
P2 · Tendency to fall on the pull-test within 3 years
Level 2
Tendency to fall backward on the pull-test if not caught by examiner, within 3 years. Test: examiner stands behind patient (standing erect, eyes open, feet comfortably apart and parallel) and delivers a quick, forceful backward pull on the shoulders (per MDS-UPDRS item 3.12). Falling if not caught = P2. More than two steps back but self-recovery = P3.
P3 · More than 2 steps backward but unaided recovery on pull-test within 3 years
Level 3
No postural instability features from this domain
Step 5 · Domain A — Akinesia
Mark the highest certainty level present. A1 and A2 require 3-year onset criterion. Mark only one level.
Akinesia Domain levels:
A1 (Level 1): Progressive gait freezing within 3 years (not levodopa responsive)
A2 (Level 2): Akinetic-rigid parkinsonism, predominantly axial, levodopa resistant
A3 (Level 3): Parkinsonism with tremor and/or asymmetric and/or levodopa responsive
A1 · Progressive gait freezing within 3 years (not levodopa responsive)
Level 1
Sudden and transient motor blocks or start hesitation that are the predominant feature within 3 years of symptom onset, progressive, and not responsive to levodopa. In early disease, akinesia may be present but limb rigidity, tremor, and dementia are absent or mild. This pattern defines PSP-PGF (progressive gait freezing) phenotype.
A2 · Parkinsonism: akinetic-rigid, predominantly axial, levodopa resistant
Level 2
Bradykinesia and rigidity with axial predominance (neck, trunk, proximal limbs more than distal), and levodopa resistance. Levodopa resistance defined as <30% improvement on MDS-UPDRS motor scale — assessed with ≥1,000 mg/day (if tolerated) for ≥1 month, OR after a formal challenge dose of ≥200 mg.
Clinical Clue CC1 (Levodopa resistance): <30% improvement on MDS-UPDRS-III. Must receive ≥1,000 mg/day for ≥1 month, or assessed after ≥200 mg acute challenge. Patients never trialed on adequate levodopa cannot be classified as levodopa resistant.
A3 · Parkinsonism with tremor and/or asymmetric predominance and/or levodopa responsive
Level 3
Bradykinesia with rigidity and/or tremor, and/or limb-predominant asymmetry, and/or levodopa responsiveness (≥30% improvement on MDS-UPDRS-III). This more PD-like presentation carries lowest certainty for PSP but still contributes when combined with other domain features. Represents the PSP-P (parkinsonism) phenotype.
No akinesia features from this domain
Step 6 · Domain C — Cognitive Dysfunction
Mark the highest certainty level present. Features must be persistent (not transient). C2 requires ≥3 of the listed sub-features.
Cognitive Domain levels:
C1 (Level 1): Speech/language disorder — nfaPPA or progressive apraxia of speech
C2 (Level 2): Frontal cognitive/behavioral presentation (≥3 of: apathy, bradyphrenia, dysexecutive syndrome, reduced phonemic verbal fluency, impulsivity/disinhibition/perseveration)
C3 (Level 3): Corticobasal syndrome (CBS) — ≥1 cortical sign + ≥1 movement disorder sign
C1 · Speech/language disorder — nonfluent/agrammatic PPA or progressive apraxia of speech
Level 1
Must be persistent (not transient). At least one of:
nfaPPA: Loss of grammar and/or telegraphic speech or writing.
Progressive AOS: Effortful, halting speech with inconsistent speech sound errors and distortions, or slow syllabically segmented prosodic speech patterns — with spared single-word comprehension, object knowledge, and word retrieval during sentence repetition.
PSP-SL (speech/language) phenotype — one of the more common variant presentations. AOS alone can predate other PSP features by years. Hypophonia and dysarthria without AOS features do not qualify for C1; they may contribute as Clinical Clue CC2 (hypokinetic, spastic dysarthria).
C2 · Frontal cognitive/behavioral presentation (≥3 persistent features)
Level 2
At least 3 of the following (persistent, not transient):
1. Apathy — reduced interest, initiative, and spontaneous activity (apparent to informant/patient)
2. Bradyphrenia — slowed thinking (apparent to informant/patient)
3. Dysexecutive syndrome — e.g., reverse digit span, Trails B, Stroop test, Luria sequence (≥1.5 SD below age/education-adjusted norms)
4. Reduced phonemic verbal fluency — e.g., D, F, A, or S words per minute (≥1.5 SD below norms)
5. Impulsivity, disinhibition, or perseveration — e.g., socially inappropriate behaviors, overstuffing mouth, motor recklessness, applause sign, palilalia, echolalia
C3 · Corticobasal syndrome — ≥1 cortical sign + ≥1 movement disorder sign
Level 3
Requires at least one from each group (may be asymmetric or symmetric):
Cortical signs: (a) Orobuccal or limb apraxia; (b) Cortical sensory deficit (astereognosis, graphesthesia); (c) Alien limb phenomena (more than simple levitation)
Movement disorder signs: (a) Limb rigidity; (b) Limb akinesia; (c) Limb myoclonus
PSP-CBS is one of the variant phenotypes. The CBS clinical syndrome has multiple possible underlying pathologies (PSP, CBD, AD, TDP-43, etc.). PSP-CBS and PSP-SL together define the category of "probable 4R-tauopathy" when the B3 context-dependent exclusion for AD is applied.
No cognitive features from this domain
Step 7 · Supportive Features
Supportive features have positive predictive value insufficient to qualify as diagnostic criteria, but sufficient to increase diagnostic confidence. Imaging findings can upgrade to "imaging-supported" diagnosis.
Clinical Clues (CC1–CC4)
CC1
Levodopa resistance (<30% UPDRS-III improvement on ≥1,000 mg/day ≥1 month or ≥200 mg challenge)
CC2
Hypokinetic, spastic dysarthria — slow, low volume and pitch, harsh voice
CC3
Dysphagia — unexplained, severe enough to require dietary adaptations
CC4
Photophobia — intolerance to visual perception of light (adaptive dysfunction)
Imaging Findings (IF1–IF2) Qualifies for "imaging-supported" label
IF1
Predominant midbrain atrophy or hypometabolism (relative to pons) — MRI or [18F]FDG-PET. Hummingbird sign / morning glory sign on sagittal MRI.
IF2
Postsynaptic striatal dopaminergic degeneration — [123I]IBZM-SPECT or [18F]-DMFP-PET

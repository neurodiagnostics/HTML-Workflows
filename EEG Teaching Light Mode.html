Lesson 01 · 40 minutes

EEG Fundamentals

Before a resident can read a single epoch, they need to understand where the electrodes are, how the montage determines what they're seeing, and what artifacts look like. These foundations prevent the most common misreadings.

The 10–20 System — 10 min
Where the Electrodes Are and Why It Matters

The International 10–20 System places electrodes at standardised positions defined by percentages of skull distance — 10% and 20% intervals from nasion to inion and between the preauricular points. The system ensures that electrode positions correspond to reproducible underlying cortical regions across patients.

Residents must know the letter-number convention: Fp = frontopolar, F = frontal, C = central, P = parietal, T = temporal, O = occipital. Odd numbers = left hemisphere, even numbers = right hemisphere, z = midline. This is the dermatome map of EEG — without it, you cannot localise anything.

Memory Anchor
Odd = Left, Even = Right. The temporal chain runs F7–T3–T5 on the left and F8–T4–T6 on the right. Cz sits dead centre over the vertex — it is the reference point for the entire system and is always in the middle of your longitudinal bipolar montage.
Montages — 10 min
How the Montage Determines What You See

A montage is a scheme for pairing electrodes to display voltage differences. The same underlying brain activity looks different depending on which montage you're using — this is the most disorienting thing for junior residents. They must understand two montages cold before reading any EEG.

Each channel = voltage difference between two adjacent electrodes. The standard "double banana" runs Fp1–F3–C3–P3–O1 on the left parasagittal chain and Fp2–F4–C4–P4–O2 on the right, with the temporal chain Fp1–F7–T3–T5–O1 and Fp2–F8–T4–T6–O2.

Phase reversal is the key localisation tool. When a discharge is maximal at electrode X, channels above and below X will show deflections pointing toward each other (negative phase reversal = spike). This is how you localise a focus on bipolar montage — find where the waveforms point toward each other.

Phase reversal: the two channels sharing electrode C3 deflect toward each other, identifying C3 as the site of maximal electronegativity. This is the fundamental localisation tool on bipolar montage.

Each channel = voltage at a single electrode referenced to a common point (common average, linked ears, Cz, or Fz). All channels are independent. A large discharge appears in all channels simultaneously but is largest in amplitude at the electrode closest to the generator.

Localise by amplitude: the highest-amplitude channel is closest to the focus. Unlike bipolar montage, there is no phase reversal — instead you look for which channel has the most prominent waveform. The reference electrode choice matters: a "contaminated" reference (e.g., Cz used as reference when there's a vertex discharge) will pollute all channels simultaneously, creating a spurious generalised pattern.

Teaching Point
Teach residents to always view the same epoch in two montages before concluding localisation. A focus that looks bilateral or diffuse on referential may show clear unilateral phase reversal on bipolar, and vice versa. Most EEG readers use longitudinal bipolar as their primary working montage and referential to confirm.
Artifacts — 10 min
What to Exclude Before Reading Anything

More EEG misreadings are caused by artifact misidentification than by failure to recognise a real pattern. Establish the rule: before calling anything abnormal, identify a plausible biological or technical source.

Muscle Artifact (EMG)
High-frequency (>30 Hz), irregular, brief bursts. Worse in frontal and temporal electrodes. Increases with patient agitation, chewing, or blinking. Does not follow physiological waveform morphology.
Eye Blink Artifact
Large slow, broad deflection maximal at Fp1/Fp2 with an anterior-to-posterior voltage gradient. Disappears in posterior channels. Caused by the corneoretinal potential moving with eyelid motion.
Electrode Pop / Artifact
Appears in a single channel as a near-instantaneous vertical deflection. Does NOT appear in the adjacent electrode pair. This is the most common mimic of an epileptiform spike — confirm any apparent spike in at least 2 channels before calling it real.
60 Hz (Power Line) Artifact
Perfectly regular 60 Hz sinusoidal oscillation. Caused by high electrode impedance picking up environmental electrical interference. Affects one or multiple channels. Fix: check and reduce impedance at the offending electrode.
The Cardinal Rule
Never call a spike that appears in only one channel. Electrode pops are the single most common cause of false-positive epileptiform readings by trainees. A real interictal epileptiform discharge has a field — it appears across multiple electrodes with an anatomically plausible distribution and an associated slow wave.
The 5-Step Framework — 5 min
How to Read Every EEG in Order
  1. 1
    Confirm technical adequacyElectrodes present and labelled, impedances acceptable, paper speed 30 mm/s, sensitivity 7 µV/mm, filters standard (HF 70 Hz, LF 1 Hz). Note any missing electrodes.
  2. 2
    Characterise the backgroundWhat is the dominant posterior rhythm? What is its frequency, amplitude, symmetry, and reactivity to eye opening? Is it age-appropriate? This single step screens for diffuse encephalopathy.
  3. 3
    Assess state and state changesIs the patient awake, drowsy, or asleep? Are normal state-change features present (vertex waves, sleep spindles, K-complexes)? Absence of state change in a comatose patient is itself a significant finding.
  4. 4
    Identify focal abnormalitiesFocal slowing, focal attenuation, focal sharp waves. Localise to a region using phase reversal on bipolar or maximum amplitude on referential. Always confirm in at least two channels.
  5. 5
    Identify epileptiform activitySpikes, sharp waves, spike-wave complexes, periodic discharges. Apply ACNS terminology. Always correlate with clinical state — an electrographic seizure without clinical correlate is still a seizure.

Lesson 02 · 40 minutes

Normal EEG Across States

Residents cannot identify abnormal patterns until they know normal cold. The most important skill is recognising the posterior dominant rhythm and understanding how EEG changes with drowsiness and sleep — because failing to account for state causes most false-positive reads.

The Posterior Dominant Rhythm — 12 min
The Single Most Important Background Feature

The posterior dominant rhythm (PDR) — also called the alpha rhythm — is the most clinically informative single feature of the resting EEG. It is assessed in the occipital channels (O1, O2) in a relaxed, eyes-closed awake patient.

Normal PDR — Full Montage Showing Posterior Predominance (9 Hz, Eyes Closed)
Full 8-channel longitudinal bipolar montage showing the defining feature of the PDR: posterior predominance. Frontal channels (Fp1–F3, Fp2–F4, red) show low-voltage irregular mixed activity — this is the normal anterior background in a wakeful adult, not silence. Central channels (F3–C3, F4–C4, amber) show small-amplitude alpha as a transitional zone. Parietal-occipital channels (C3–P3 → P3–O1 in blue/purple) show progressively larger amplitude alpha, maximal at the P3–O1 and P4–O2 channels. The gradient is not flat-to-large but irregular-to-organised: the key feature is that a regular rhythmic sinusoidal rhythm becomes visible and dominant as you move posteriorly, while anteriorly activity is present but disorganised.
Normal PDR — Alpha Blocking with Eye Opening
Alpha blocking: the PDR attenuates within 1–2 seconds of eye opening. This reactivity confirms the rhythm is a true PDR and not artifact. Absence of alpha blocking (PDR persists with eyes open) raises suspicion for occipital pathology or a non-physiological rhythm.
Abnormal PDR — Slow (6 Hz) — Still Posterior-Predominant
An abnormal PDR at 6 Hz still shows the same posterior gradient — the slow rhythm remains largest in the parietal-occipital channels. The finding is abnormal because the frequency is below 8 Hz (Grade I slowing), not because the posterior predominance is lost.

Sleep Features — 10 min
Normal EEG Changes in Drowsiness and Sleep

One of the most common sources of false-positive reads by junior residents is misidentifying normal sleep features as pathology. Vertex waves get called focal central sharp waves. Sleep spindles get called generalised epileptiform activity. Teach these patterns with the same rigor as pathological ones.

Vertex Sharp Wave (Stage N1)
Sharp biphasic waveform, maximal at Cz, occurring in early sleep/drowsiness. Bilaterally synchronous. Not epileptiform — it is a normal sleep feature. Distinguished from a pathological central spike by its symmetric Cz maximum and sleep state context.
Sleep Spindles (Stage N2)
Waxing-and-waning 12–14 Hz bursts lasting 0.5–2 seconds. Maximal centrally (C3, Cz, C4). Hallmark of Stage N2. Not epileptiform. Absent spindles in a comatose patient suggests thalamic or brainstem dysfunction.
K-Complex (Stage N2)
High-amplitude biphasic waveform: sharp negative deflection followed by a slower positive wave. Frontocentral maximum. Hallmark of Stage N2, often followed by a sleep spindle. Duration >0.5 s. Normal sleep — not epileptiform.
The Breach Rhythm Trap
A breach rhythm occurs at the site of a skull defect (craniotomy, burr hole). The absent bone acts as a low-impedance window, allowing higher-amplitude, sharper-appearing activity through. This makes entirely normal underlying cortex look like focal spike activity. Always check the clinical history for prior neurosurgery before interpreting focal sharp activity in a single region.

  • B. Normal PDR (alpha rhythm) and normal vertex sharp waves of drowsiness

Lesson 03 · 40 minutes

Abnormal Background Patterns

Abnormal backgrounds encode the severity of cerebral dysfunction. Teach residents to grade slowing systematically, recognise the critical care patterns, and understand why reactivity is the single most prognostically weighted feature on a coma EEG.

This lesson is best taught with a laptop open to an actual cEEG viewer. Pull an anonymised encephalopathy study and walk through it in real time. Grading diffuse slowing is a skill that requires repetition — no description replaces seeing 30 seconds of background and asking "what Hz is this?"

Critical Patterns — 15 min
Suppression, Burst-Suppression, and Alpha Coma
Burst-Suppression
Alternating periods of high-amplitude mixed-frequency bursts and near-flat suppression. Caused by profound cortical depression: deep anaesthesia (intentional), severe anoxic injury, or barbiturate coma. Clinical context determines prognosis — induced burst-suppression for refractory SE has a different implication than spontaneous burst-suppression after cardiac arrest.
Alpha Coma
Alpha-frequency (8–13 Hz) activity that is diffuse rather than posteriorly dominant and does not attenuate with eye opening or stimulation. Associated with brainstem lesions (pontine stroke) or severe post-anoxic injury. Carries poor prognosis when persistent and unreactive.
Triphasic Waves
Three-phase waveforms (negative–positive–negative) at 1–2 Hz, anteriorly predominant with anterior-to-posterior lag. Classic association: hepatic or uraemic encephalopathy. Critical caveat: triphasic morphology GPDs can represent NCSE — if clinical state does not match metabolic encephalopathy, a benzodiazepine trial may be warranted (Salzburg criteria).
Reactivity — The Single Most Important Coma EEG Feature
Reactivity is defined as any change in background activity (frequency, amplitude, or morphology) in response to a standardised stimulus — typically sternal rub, nail bed pressure, or loud auditory stimulus. Preserved reactivity in a comatose patient, even with a severely abnormal background, is associated with better outcome. Absent reactivity with burst-suppression or diffuse delta post-cardiac arrest is a major adverse prognostic indicator. Document reactivity systematically in every coma EEG. [Rossetti et al., Neurology 2010]

Lesson 04 · 45 minutes

Epileptiform Discharges

This is the lesson residents are most eager to reach — but they are least prepared for it without the foundations of the prior three sessions. Teach morphology rigorously before teaching clinical significance. A well-described discharge is useful. An incorrectly identified one is dangerous.

Generalised 3 Hz Spike-Wave — Absence
Generalised 3 Hz spike-wave complexes: simultaneous in all channels, no phase reversal on bipolar (because there is no single generator — the discharge is bilateral and synchronous). Hallmark of typical absence epilepsy (childhood absence, juvenile absence). Each complex lasts <10 seconds during typical absence; >30 minutes = absence status epilepticus.
ACNS Critical Care Terminology — 15 min
LPDs, GPDs, LRDA, GRDA — The Patterns You Must Know

The ACNS 2021 standardised terminology replaced older, inconsistent terms (PLEDs, BIPLEDs, FIRDA) with a descriptive, hierarchical system. Residents rotating through the NeuroICU must know these terms because they appear in every cEEG report and directly influence management decisions.

LPDs — Lateralised Periodic Discharges (formerly PLEDs)▼

What they are: Periodic sharp waves or spike-wave complexes occurring at regular intervals (typically 0.5–3 Hz) over one hemisphere or a focal region. The interval between complexes is their defining feature.

Clinical significance: LPDs nearly always indicate an acute or subacute structural lesion — ischaemic stroke, herpes encephalitis, tumour, cortical injury. They sit firmly within the ictal-interictal continuum. The presence of LPDs should prompt urgent evaluation for an underlying structural lesion if none is known.

LPDs+F (plus fast): LPDs with superimposed high-frequency activity carry higher ictal risk and are more likely to represent ongoing seizure activity. In this setting, treat as seizure until proven otherwise.

GPDs — Generalised Periodic Discharges (formerly triphasic waves)▼

What they are: Periodic discharges occurring bisynchronously across both hemispheres, typically at 1–2.5 Hz. Triphasic morphology (negative–positive–negative) is a subtype of GPDs.

Clinical significance: GPDs are associated with severe diffuse cerebral dysfunction. Classic causes: hepatic encephalopathy, uraemic encephalopathy, post-anoxic injury, prion disease (CJD produces characteristic 1 Hz GPDs). However, GPDs can also represent NCSE — the distinction requires clinical correlation and often a benzodiazepine trial.

The key teaching point: Do not automatically assume GPDs = metabolic encephalopathy. If the clinical state is unexplained, perform a trial of IV benzodiazepine and watch the EEG for electrographic improvement and clinical correlate.

LRDA — Lateralised Rhythmic Delta Activity▼

What they are: Rhythmic (not periodic — no inter-discharge interval) delta activity (<4 Hz) over one hemisphere, typically 1–3 Hz, lasting at least 10 seconds without evolution.

Clinical significance: LRDA is highly associated with ipsilateral structural lesions — particularly acute ischaemic stroke, subdural haematoma, or post-ictal slowing after a focal seizure. It is less epileptiform than LPDs but carries a significant risk for seizure development. LRDA over the temporal region after a seizure is the classic post-ictal focal delta pattern.

GRDA — Generalised Rhythmic Delta Activity (formerly FIRDA)▼

What they are: Rhythmic bilateral delta activity, often frontally predominant (previously called FIRDA: Frontal Intermittent Rhythmic Delta Activity). Occurs in runs rather than continuously.

Clinical significance: GRDA is a non-specific pattern associated with diffuse encephalopathy, raised intracranial pressure, or deep midline/thalamic dysfunction. It is not an epileptiform pattern by itself but its presence in a comatose patient warrants investigation of ICP and metabolic state. GRDA in a patient with metabolic encephalopathy is expected; GRDA in a patient with a normal metabolic panel suggests structural deep pathology.

Pattern Illustrations
What Each Pattern Looks Like on EEG
LPDs — Lateralised Periodic Discharges
LPDs: Periodic sharp-wave complexes confined to the right temporal chain (F8–T4, T4–T6). The left hemisphere (F7–T3, T3–T5) shows quiet background — this lateralisation is the defining feature. Inter-discharge interval is regular (~0.67 s = ~1.5 Hz). Classic cause: right hemispheric structural lesion (stroke, tumour, HSV encephalitis).
GPDs — Generalised Periodic Discharges
GPDs: Triphasic neg–pos–neg morphology occurring synchronously in all channels at ~1.5 Hz. Critically, both hemispheres fire simultaneously — there is no lateralisation, no phase reversal, no amplitude asymmetry. Classic associations: hepatic encephalopathy, uraemic encephalopathy, CJD (1 Hz GPDs). Red dashed lines highlight perfect bilateral synchrony.
LRDA — Lateralised Rhythmic Delta Activity
LRDA: Smooth, continuous 2 Hz rhythmic delta confined to the left temporal chain (F7–T3, T3–T5). The key distinction from LPDs: there are no gaps between waves — this is a continuous rhythm, not periodic discharges. The right hemisphere is quiet. Associated with ipsilateral structural lesion or post-ictal state. Less epileptiform than LPDs but still warrants investigation.
GRDA — Generalised Rhythmic Delta Activity
GRDA: Continuous 2 Hz bilateral rhythmic delta with frontal predominance (Fp1–F3, Fp2–F4 show maximum amplitude) that attenuates posteriorly (C3–P3, C4–P4 show minimal activity). Both hemispheres are symmetric — distinguishing it from LRDA. Not intrinsically epileptiform. Associated with raised ICP, deep midline dysfunction, or diffuse encephalopathy.
The Critical Distinction: LPDs vs LRDA
Both are lateralised. The single differentiating feature is the inter-discharge interval. LPDs have regular gaps between complexes — you can tap the rhythm like a metronome and there are silent periods between beats. LRDA has no gaps — it is a continuous rhythm with no return to baseline between waves. This distinction matters because LPDs carry higher epileptogenic risk than LRDA.

Lesson 05 · 35 minutes

Status Epilepticus on EEG

Status epilepticus is the highest-stakes EEG diagnosis in the NeuroICU. Residents must know the electrographic criteria, recognise NCSE patterns, understand the Salzburg criteria, and know how to monitor treatment response on cEEG.

Teach this lesson with a cEEG case if one is running in the unit. The most powerful teaching moment is showing a resident the EEG of an unresponsive patient, asking "is this seizure?", and watching them work through the Salzburg criteria in real time.
Electrographic SE Criteria — 10 min
When Does an EEG Pattern Become Status Epilepticus?

The operational clinical definition of status epilepticus is seizure activity lasting ≥5 minutes, but on EEG the criteria are more nuanced. An electrographic seizure (ESz) is defined as rhythmic epileptiform activity lasting ≥10 seconds with one of the following features: evolution in frequency, amplitude, or morphology; or post-ictal slowing/attenuation following the discharge.

Electrographic status epilepticus (ESE) = electrographic seizure activity for ≥30 minutes out of any 60-minute period of cEEG monitoring. This is the definition used in cEEG practice and drives treatment escalation decisions.

The Salzburg Criteria for NCSE (2013, revised 2019)
NCSE is diagnosed when any one of the following is met in a patient with impaired consciousness:

1. Epileptiform discharges >2.5 Hz for ≥10 seconds, OR
2. Epileptiform discharges ≤2.5 Hz or rhythmic delta/theta activity AND at least one: (a) clinical correlate, (b) EEG evolution (frequency/amplitude/morphology), or (c) IV benzodiazepine produces EEG and clinical improvement

Exception: In patients with known epilepsy and typical ictal patterns, the threshold may differ. Salzburg requires correlation with clinical state throughout.

Source: Leitinger M et al., Epilepsia 2016;57(10):1503–1516
NCSE Patterns — 15 min
Four Patterns You Must Recognise

Electrographic pattern: Continuous or near-continuous generalised 3 Hz (or slightly slower) spike-wave complexes. May be less regular than typical absence, with frequency ranging 2.5–4 Hz. Background is otherwise preserved.

Clinical presentation: Impaired but not absent consciousness — the patient may appear dazed, confused, or automatonically responsive but cannot engage normally. Often seen in patients with known generalised epilepsy who miss medication, or in elderly patients with no prior epilepsy diagnosis (de novo absence SE in the elderly).

Treatment: IV benzodiazepine is first-line and typically highly effective. Response on EEG — cessation of spike-wave and restoration of background — confirms the diagnosis.

Typical Absence SE — Continuous Generalised 3 Hz Spike-Wave
Continuous generalised 3 Hz spike-wave filling the entire epoch — all 6 channels fire simultaneously with no localising feature. The background between complexes is preserved (distinguishing this from a diffuse encephalopathy). This pattern persisting in a confused patient = absence SE. IV benzodiazepine should produce abrupt cessation and return of normal background.

Electrographic pattern: Sustained focal rhythmic epileptiform activity over one region — typically temporal (most common), frontal, or parietal. May evolve in frequency. Can appear as LPDs+F (LPDs with superimposed fast activity) or as sustained rhythmic theta/alpha over the affected region.

Clinical presentation: Variable — ranges from subtle behavioural change to prominent automatisms (lip smacking, hand picking) to aphasia (dominant temporal NCSE) to gaze deviation. The key: the patient's behaviour cannot be explained by a structural lesion or metabolic state alone.

Dominant temporal NCSE: A critically underdiagnosed pattern — patients present with isolated aphasia or confusion and are admitted under stroke teams. cEEG reveals continuous left temporal seizure activity. Always consider cEEG in aphasia with fluctuating course and normal or prior MRI.

Focal NCSE — Left Temporal Sustained Rhythmic Activity with Frequency Evolution
Left temporal focal NCSE: F7–T3 and T3–T5 show sustained ictal activity while right temporal channels (F8–T4, T4–T6) and midline (Fz–Cz) remain quiet. The hallmark of an evolving seizure is frequency evolution — this epoch shows the discharge accelerating from ~2 Hz theta at onset through 4 Hz to a fast 6 Hz phase. This evolution is what distinguishes a focal seizure from static LRDA. Clinically: left temporal NCSE = aphasia without motor signs.

Background: Subtle generalised convulsive SE (subtle GCSE) occurs when a patient has been in prolonged convulsive SE and the motor manifestations have exhausted — the clinical seizure appears to have ended but the EEG shows ongoing electrographic seizure activity. This is one of the most dangerous patterns in neurocritical care.

Electrographic pattern: GPDs or LPDs with superimposed fast activity, or continuous generalised spike-wave, in a patient who appears post-ictal or unresponsive. Critically — the EEG pattern does not match the clinical state (patient appears post-ictal but EEG shows ongoing ictal activity).

Why it matters: Every minute of unrecognised electrographic status epilepticus causes neuronal injury. The DeLorenzo study (1998) showed that 14% of patients with apparent clinical SE cessation had ongoing electrographic seizures on EEG. This is the primary justification for mandatory cEEG monitoring after convulsive SE.

Subtle GCSE — GPDs+F: Generalised Periodic Discharges with Superimposed Fast Activity
GPDs+F in subtle GCSE: large synchronous complexes across all channels at ~1.5 Hz, but each complex carries superimposed rapid beta oscillations riding on its peak — the "+F" (plus fast) modifier. This is the most ominous variant of GPDs because the superimposed fast activity indicates ongoing ictal recruitment. The patient appears unresponsive post-ictally but this EEG is actively seizing. Treat immediately as SE.
Clinical Rule
Any patient who does not return to their baseline mental status within 30–60 minutes of apparent convulsive seizure cessation requires emergent EEG to rule out ongoing non-convulsive seizures. This is non-negotiable.

The ictal-interictal continuum (IIC) describes EEG patterns that cannot be definitively classified as ictal or interictal based on morphology alone. These patterns include LPDs, GPDs, LRDA, GRDA, and their modifiers (+F, +S, +R).

The IIC is clinically important because it demands a management decision: treat as seizure (with attendant drug side effects and anaesthetic risk) or monitor conservatively. The Salzburg criteria provide a structured framework for this decision — if clinical correlate is present or EEG evolves, treat. If neither, monitor with repeated clinical assessment.

A practical approach: in a patient who is more encephalopathic than expected for their structural lesion or metabolic state, assume IIC patterns are contributing to encephalopathy until proven otherwise. A brief IV benzodiazepine trial — 1 mg lorazepam — with observation of both EEG and clinical response is a low-risk diagnostic and therapeutic test.

The Ictal-Interictal Continuum — Spectrum Diagram
The IIC spectrum runs from clearly interictal (isolated IEDs) through the ambiguous zone (LPDs, GPDs, LRDA, GRDA) to clearly ictal (ESz, ESE). The critical distinction within the IIC zone is the "+F" modifier: LPDs without fast activity are ambiguous and require Salzburg criteria to guide treatment, while LPDs+F carry sufficient ictal risk to justify treating as seizure empirically. When uncertain, the benzodiazepine trial under cEEG is both diagnostic and therapeutic.

Lesson 06 · 45 minutes

Case-Based Consolidation

Four cases integrating all prior lessons. Present history and EEG description before showing pattern details. Ask residents to work through the 5-step framework before any interpretation is offered.

For each case: read the history aloud, pause, and ask "what do you want to assess first?" If they jump to "is there a spike?" redirect them: "Start with the background. What does the PDR look like?" The framework must become automatic before they are reliable EEG readers.
Case 01
64F · Altered Mental Status in the ICU

History: 64-year-old woman, septic shock from urosepsis, on vasopressors and broad-spectrum antibiotics. GCS 9 at baseline for 48 hours — not improving despite source control. Neurology consulted. cEEG ordered.

EEG description (before numbers): Background is diffusely slow. No posterior dominant rhythm is identifiable. Waveforms are generalised, irregular, large-amplitude, and occur at approximately 1.5–2 Hz. Waveform morphology appears triphasic in some channels — negative–positive–negative — with an anterior predominance. No state changes over 30 minutes. Pause — ask residents: what is the differential? What single next step on EEG would help narrow it?

EEG data: GPDs with triphasic morphology at 1.5 Hz, frontal predominance, no evolution. No response to auditory stimulation (absent reactivity). LFT: ammonia 210 µmol/L, creatinine 3.8.

→ Reveal Interpretation and Report Language

Step 1 (Technical): All electrodes present. No major artifacts documented.

Step 2 (Background): No identifiable PDR. Diffusely slow — Grade III (severe diffuse dysfunction).

Step 3 (State): No state changes. Absent reactivity — poor prognostic sign, though clinical context (sedation, vasopressors) must be considered.

Step 4 (Focal): No focal slowing or attenuation beyond global pattern.

Step 5 (Epileptiform): GPDs with triphasic morphology — ACNS terminology GPDs (triphasic morphology), 1.5 Hz, frontal predominance.

Salzburg criteria assessment: Rate ≤2.5 Hz — does not independently meet NCSE criteria. Need clinical correlate or EEG evolution. Given ammonia of 210, metabolic encephalopathy is a strong competing explanation. However, absent reactivity in a comatose patient with GPDs warrants IV benzodiazepine trial under cEEG monitoring — if EEG and clinical state improve, NCSE was contributing.

Report language: "EEG demonstrates a severely abnormal background with no identifiable posterior dominant rhythm and diffuse continuous delta activity (Grade III diffuse dysfunction). GPDs with triphasic morphology are present at 1.5 Hz with frontal predominance, consistent with severe metabolic encephalopathy. Reactivity is absent. Salzburg criteria for NCSE are not definitively met at this rate; however, given the severity of encephalopathy, a brief IV benzodiazepine trial under continuous EEG monitoring is recommended to exclude NCSE contribution. Correlation with ammonia, renal function, and medication list is essential."

Case 02
32M · Recurrent Seizures Since Age 14

History: 32-year-old man with juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (JME), presenting for routine outpatient EEG after a breakthrough tonic-clonic seizure. Currently on valproate. No recent illness or medication change reported. The EEG is performed with hyperventilation and photic stimulation.

EEG description: Background PDR is 9 Hz, symmetric, reactive. During hyperventilation at 2 minutes, a 2-second burst of generalised 4–5 Hz polyspike-wave complexes occurs, maximal frontally, associated with subtle eye flutter noted by technologist. During photic stimulation at 16 Hz, a brief generalised photoparoxysmal response occurs. Pause — ask: what syndrome does this pattern suggest? What frequency spike-wave is classically associated with JME vs absence epilepsy?

→ Reveal Interpretation

Background: Normal 9 Hz PDR — age-appropriate, reactive. Normal interictal background.

Activation procedures: Hyperventilation activated generalised polyspike-wave at 4–5 Hz (faster than typical absence epilepsy's 3 Hz). This is the characteristic discharge of JME — generalised polyspike-wave at 4–6 Hz, frontally predominant, with associated clinical correlate (eye flutter = myoclonus). Photoparoxysmal response confirms photosensitivity, another feature of JME.

JME vs Absence Epilepsy: Typical absence = 3 Hz regular spike-wave, occurring spontaneously, often without clinical correlate. JME = 4–6 Hz polyspike-wave, typically activated by morning, sleep deprivation, and photic stimulation, associated with myoclonus and occasionally tonic-clonic seizures. Valproate is first-line for both, but the distinction matters for prognosis — JME is typically lifelong whereas childhood absence often remits.

Teaching point on activation: Hyperventilation and photic stimulation are mandatory activation procedures in outpatient EEG and substantially increase diagnostic yield. Always ensure these are performed and documented.

Case 03
58M · Post-Cardiac Arrest · Day 3

History: 58-year-old man, VF cardiac arrest, ROSC after 22 minutes of CPR. Currently on targeted temperature management (36°C). GCS M3 off sedation. cEEG ordered for prognostication and seizure monitoring.

EEG description: Background is diffusely suppressed with occasional bursts of mixed-frequency activity (burst-suppression). Burst rate approximately 6 bursts per minute. Sternal rub produces no change in background. During one 30-minute epoch, 3 brief runs of evolving rhythmic activity over the right hemisphere lasting 15–25 seconds each are noted, associated with subtle right facial twitching documented by bedside nurse. Pause — ask: does this represent seizure? Does reactivity matter here? What does burst-suppression tell us at day 3 post-arrest?

EEG data: Burst-suppression with absent reactivity. Right hemisphere ESz (electrographic seizures) ×3, each 15–25 sec, with clinical correlate. Background does not return to baseline between seizures.

→ Reveal Interpretation

Background: Burst-suppression with absent reactivity at day 3 post-cardiac arrest is a major adverse prognostic indicator. Per the Rossetti prognostic framework, absent reactivity with burst-suppression at 72 hours post-arrest has a false-positive rate for poor outcome of <5% (i.e., nearly all patients with this pattern die or remain in a vegetative state). [Rossetti AO et al., Neurology 2010]

Seizures: Three right hemisphere electrographic seizures with clinical correlate (focal facial twitching) = electroclinical seizures. Duration 15–25 seconds each — below the 30-minute ESE threshold but representing significant ongoing seizure burden in a devastated brain. Treat aggressively — post-anoxic seizures worsen neuronal injury regardless of background prognosis.

Management: IV levetiracetam or valproate as first-line ASM. If seizures persist, escalate to midazolam infusion. Importantly: treat the seizures and reassess — ongoing seizure activity is a potentially modifiable contributor to outcome even in a poor-prognosis background. Do not withhold treatment based on background alone.

Report language: "cEEG demonstrates severe diffuse background abnormality with burst-suppression pattern and absent reactivity — findings carrying significant adverse prognostic implications in the post-cardiac arrest context at day 3 (Rossetti criteria). Three right hemisphere electrographic seizures with clinical correlate (right facial twitching) are identified, duration 15–25 seconds. These meet criteria for recurrent electroclinical seizures. Recommend escalation of antiseizure medication with ongoing cEEG monitoring. Prognostic multimodal assessment at 72–96 hours is recommended per institutional post-arrest protocol."

Case 04
45F · Acute Aphasia with Fluctuating Course

History: 45-year-old woman with no prior neurological history. Brought in by family for acute word-finding difficulty and confusion beginning 6 hours ago. MRI DWI is negative for acute stroke. CTA shows no occlusion. The stroke team is planning discharge with TIA workup. Neurology is called because the aphasia fluctuates — better for 10–15 minutes then worsens again. No witnessed motor activity.

EEG description: Technologist notes patient becomes notably less responsive during the recording. In temporal channels, sustained rhythmic 2 Hz theta activity develops over the left temporal region (T3, T5 maximum) lasting 3.5 minutes with gradual frequency evolution from 2 Hz to 3 Hz, then abrupt cessation followed by focal delta slowing over the same region. Pause — ask: what is happening during those 3.5 minutes? What would confirm NCSE? What does the post-discharge delta tell you?

→ Reveal Interpretation

Diagnosis: Focal non-convulsive seizure / NCSE from left temporal focus. The 3.5-minute rhythmic temporal theta with frequency evolution (2 → 3 Hz) and abrupt offset = electrographic seizure. Duration >30 seconds with clinical correlate (worsening aphasia during the run) = focal electroclinical seizure. The post-ictal focal left temporal delta (LRDA) confirms the seizure origin.

Salzburg criteria: Rhythmic delta/theta with EEG evolution and clinical correlate — meets NCSE criteria.

Why aphasia fluctuates: Each seizure transiently suppresses dominant hemisphere language function. The 10–15 minute improvement windows represent inter-ictal periods. This is a classic presentation of left temporal NCSE presenting as isolated aphasia — frequently misdiagnosed as TIA, psychiatric, or metabolic.

Workup: MRI with FLAIR and gadolinium (cortical swelling, enhancement, or a structural lesion missed on initial DWI), CSF for encephalitis panel (anti-LGI1, anti-CASPR2, HSV PCR — autoimmune and infectious encephalitis classically present with focal NCSE and aphasia in this age group), and long-term EEG. Start levetiracetam IV urgently.

Clinical pearl: Fluctuating aphasia + normal initial MRI + no vascular risk factors in a 45-year-old = autoimmune encephalitis until proven otherwise. Anti-LGI1 encephalitis classically presents with faciobrachial dystonic seizures and temporal lobe NCSE. Order the panel before starting steroids.

The Rule They Must Never Forget
Read the background before you look for spikes. A single sharp wave in a normal background and the same sharp wave in Grade III diffuse slowing represent entirely different clinical situations. The background is the context that gives every other finding its meaning. Residents who skip to epileptiform hunting will misread EEGs their entire careers.

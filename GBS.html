Step 1 · Clinical Diagnostic Criteria
GBS is an acute immune-mediated polyradiculoneuropathy. It is a clinical diagnosis supported by CSF and electrodiagnostic findings. The Brighton Collaboration criteria (2011, validated by Fokke et al. Brain 2014) provide four levels of diagnostic certainty. All levels share a common clinical prerequisite: bilateral flaccid limb weakness + decreased or absent deep tendon reflexes in weak limbs + monophasic course + absence of alternative diagnosis.
Epidemiology and context: Annual incidence 0.81–1.89/100,000. Peak onset after respiratory tract infection (~60% of cases) or gastroenteritis — Campylobacter jejuni is the most common identified antecedent (~30% of GBS), particularly associated with axonal subtypes (AMAN/AMSAN). Incubation 1–3 weeks. Other triggers: CMV, EBV, Influenza, SARS-CoV-2, Zika, influenza vaccination (1–2 excess cases/million doses). Bimodal age distribution (young adults and >50 years). Male:Female ~1.5:1.

Nadir timing: 85–90% of patients reach nadir within 4 weeks; median ~2 weeks. Progression beyond 8 weeks suggests CIDP rather than GBS.
Core clinical criteria — all four required for Brighton Level 1 ALL FOUR REQUIRED FOR LEVEL 1
Bilateral AND flaccid weakness of the limbs (upper, lower, or all four — at least bilateral lower limb weakness)
The Brighton criteria require bilateral and flaccid weakness — not spastic, not unilateral. Weakness is classically ascending (beginning distally in the legs then progressing upward), but this pattern is variable. Purely upper extremity onset is less common but occurs. Bifacial and descending variants also exist (Miller Fisher overlap). Flaccidity distinguishes GBS from upper motor neuron lesions. Unilateral weakness essentially excludes GBS — consider mononeuropathy, stroke, or structural lesion.
Clinical exam tip: Assess strength in bilateral hip flexors, knee extensors, ankle dorsiflexors, shoulder abductors, elbow flexors, and wrist extensors. Document MRC scale for each (0–5). The MRC sum score (6 muscle groups × 2 sides × 5 = max 60) is required for the EGRIS respiratory risk calculation.
Decreased or absent deep tendon reflexes in the weak limbs (hyporeflexia or areflexia)
Areflexia or hyporeflexia of weak limbs is a core feature, reflecting involvement of both large-caliber afferent fibers (Ia spindle afferents) and efferent motor axons or demyelination of the reflex arc. Preserved reflexes in weak limbs raise doubt about GBS — consider Lambert-Eaton (intact or enhanced reflexes that improve post-exercise), hyperkalemia, toxic neuropathy, or West Nile virus poliomyelitis. Note: reflexes may occasionally be preserved early in AMAN if only distal axons are involved and proximal testing reveals weakness before reflex loss.
Proximal reflex preservation: In early or purely distal GBS, knee and ankle jerks are typically the first to disappear. Biceps and brachioradialis reflexes usually follow. The loss of ankle jerks in the setting of ascending weakness is a strong clinical sign of GBS, distinguishing it from most central causes of acute weakness.
Monophasic course with time from onset to nadir between 12 hours and 28 days, followed by a plateau phase
The Brighton criteria require the progressive phase to last between 12 hours and 28 days, with a subsequent plateau. This monophasic course distinguishes GBS from: CIDP (progression >8 weeks, or relapsing-remitting), treatment-related fluctuations (TRF) in up to 10% of GBS patients within 2 months, and relapsing GBS (rare, <3%).

Treatment-related fluctuations (TRF): Worsening of 1+ GBS disability scale point within 2 months of reaching nadir in a patient who initially improved — occurs in ~10% of GBS. Typically responds to repeat IVIG/PLEX. If fluctuations occur beyond 8–12 weeks, reclassify as CIDP.
Brighton Level 4: Patients who do NOT meet the 12h–28 day nadir criterion (either because the progressive phase is longer or because of late fluctuations that deviate from a monophasic course) fall into Level 4 — "GBS not classifiable according to levels 1–3 but diagnosis not excluded." Level 4 captures 6% of confirmed GBS patients in the Fokke 2014 validation study (n=335).
Absence of an alternative diagnosis — clinical exclusion of CIDP, toxic neuropathy, critical illness polyneuropathy, vasculitic neuropathy, transverse myelitis, poliomyelitis
Brighton Level 1–3 all require absence of an alternative diagnosis. Exclusion criteria include: CIDP (progressive >8 weeks or relapsing); Botulism (descending paralysis, cranial neuropathies first, autonomic prominent, preserved sensation, toxin or food source); Transverse myelitis (sensory level, sphincter dysfunction, back pain, normal CSF protein usually); West Nile/poliovirus (asymmetric flaccid paralysis, fever, CSF pleocytosis); Vasculitic neuropathy (asymmetric, mononeuritis multiplex pattern, systemic features); Critical illness polyneuropathy/myopathy (ICU setting, sepsis); Tick paralysis (ascending, tick in hair); Hypokalemia, hypermagnesemia (check electrolytes); Lambert-Eaton (facilitation, autonomic, VGCC antibody).
Supportive clinical features SUPPORT DIAGNOSIS — NOT REQUIRED FOR BRIGHTON CRITERIA
Cranial nerve involvement — bilateral facial palsy (most common, ~50%), ophthalmoplegia, bulbar weakness, dysarthria, dysphagia
Cranial nerve involvement occurs in ~50% of GBS patients. Bilateral facial palsy is most common — asymmetric at onset but becoming bilateral. CN VI palsy (diplopia) less common. Bulbar weakness (dysphagia, dysphonia) increases aspiration risk and is a component of the EGRIS score. Miller Fisher syndrome (MFS): triad of ophthalmoplegia + ataxia + areflexia — a pure cranial variant often GQ1b-antibody positive. MFS-GBS overlap: cranial nerve involvement with limb weakness.
Autonomic dysfunction — tachycardia, hypertension/hypotension, urinary retention, ileus, diaphoresis, labile BP
Autonomic dysfunction occurs in ~65% of GBS patients requiring ICU admission. Cardiovascular instability (tachycardia, bradycardia, labile BP) is most common and can be life-threatening. Transient BP spikes from neck flexion or suctioning may cause catastrophic hypertension. Bradyarrhythmias (including asystole) require cardiac monitoring. Urinary retention (30%), ileus (15%) — NG tube and catheter if needed. Autonomic instability is an independent predictor of prolonged ICU stay and mortality.
Cardiac monitoring: All patients with GBS admitted to ICU should have continuous cardiac monitoring. Avoid hypotension from excessive narcotics or rapid changes in position. Pacing may be required for high-degree AV block. Avoid medications that worsen autonomic instability (e.g., beta-blockers can worsen bradycardia).
Pain — neuropathic pain (often radicular back pain or limb burning); pain at onset in majority of patients
Preceding infection or trigger — URTI or GI illness in 1–3 weeks prior; Campylobacter exposure; vaccination within 6 weeks
~65% of GBS cases have an identified antecedent illness. Campylobacter jejuni: ~30% of GBS cases — associated with axonal (AMAN/AMSAN) subtypes and anti-GM1/GD1a antibodies; may indicate worse prognosis. CMV: ~10% — tends to have more severe sensory involvement. EBV, influenza A, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Zika virus also implicated. Influenza vaccination: estimated 1–2 excess cases per million doses (extremely low absolute risk vs. benefit of vaccination). COVID-19 vaccines and natural SARS-CoV-2 infection: increased GBS risk reported, particularly with adenoviral vector vaccines (AstraZeneca).
Step 2 · Cerebrospinal Fluid Analysis
Lumbar puncture is strongly recommended in suspected GBS — CSF results differentiate Brighton Levels 1 and 2. Albumino-cytological dissociation (elevated protein with <10 WBC/µL) is the classic finding, but this may be absent in the first week. CSF is not required to make the diagnosis if clinical criteria are met, but absence raises the level of diagnostic certainty by one step.
CSF findings in GBS — timing matters:
Cytoalbuminological dissociation: Elevated protein (>45 mg/dL) with normal or near-normal cell count (<10 cells/µL) — classic GBS finding, present in ~80% at 2 weeks after symptom onset but only ~50% in week 1. Protein rises progressively, peaking at 4–6 weeks.
Cell count: Brighton requires <50 cells/µL. Higher pleocytosis (>50) should raise concern for infectious GBS mimics (HIV-associated polyradiculoneuropathy, CMV polyradiculitis in immunocompromised patients, Lyme radiculopathy, WNV). Always send cell count differential — lymphocyte predominance is typical even when mildly elevated.
Normal CSF: Does not exclude GBS — normal protein is seen in ~30% of patients at initial presentation (Brighton Level 2 still applies if cell count <50).
CSF cell count REQUIRED FOR LEVELS 1 AND 2
CSF white cell count — <10/µL is typical; Brighton allows up to 50/µL for Levels 1 and 2
Brighton criteria use a threshold of <50 cells/µL (not the classic <10 for "normal"). Elevated cells up to 50/µL are compatible with Levels 1 and 2 and may reflect mild inflammatory reaction. Cell count >50/µL is exclusionary for Levels 1–3 (→ Level 4 territory or consider infectious/inflammatory mimic).

HIV-associated GBS: One important exception — HIV-related acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy may present with higher cell counts (50–200/µL lymphocytes), reflecting HIV-related pleocytosis superimposed on the GBS picture. In any young adult with GBS, HIV serology should be sent.
Timing of LP: If LP is performed in the first week, protein may be normal even in true GBS. Repeat LP at 2 weeks if first LP shows normal protein and clinical picture is compelling — protein rises progressively over the first month.
CSF protein DISTINGUISHES LEVELS 1 AND 2
CSF total protein — elevated (>45 mg/dL) = Level 1; normal = Level 2 (if cell count <50/µL)
Elevated CSF protein reflects protein extravasation from disrupted blood-nerve barrier in the inflamed spinal nerve roots. In the Fokke 2014 validation study (n=335), normal protein concentration was the sole reason 33% of patients fell to Level 2 rather than Level 1. Both groups had equivalent clinical features, treatment response, and outcomes — Level 2 is not a "worse" diagnosis, just less certain.

Very high protein (>200 mg/dL): Suggests more extensive root involvement. Can cause papilledema in rare cases. Protein-induced viscosity may rarely cause CSF block (Froin's syndrome). Does not change diagnosis or management but may indicate greater severity.
Step 3 · Nerve Conduction Studies (NCS)
Electrodiagnosis is required for Brighton Level 1 and separates Levels 1/2 from Level 3. NCS findings support the diagnosis and subtype classification (AIDP vs. AMAN/AMSAN). Studies may be normal in the first week — repeat at 2 weeks improves yield. Early F-wave changes and prolonged distal latencies are often the first abnormalities.
NCS timing and yield: Normal NCS within the first week does not exclude GBS. F-wave absence and H-reflex abnormality are often the earliest electrodiagnostic findings, reflecting proximal root involvement. Distal motor latency prolongation and slowing of motor conduction velocity develop within 1–2 weeks in AIDP. CMAP amplitude changes (reduced in AMAN/AMSAN or severe AIDP with secondary axon loss) may not be evident until 2–4 weeks.

Brighton NCS requirement: Findings must be "consistent with one of the subtypes of GBS" — AIDP, AMAN, AMSAN, or inexcitable nerves (all four are acceptable). "Equivocal but consistent with GBS" counts for Level 1 (if CSF also supports). Normal or equivocal NCS insufficient for Level 1 → falls to Level 2 or 3.
NCS result — overall assessment REQUIRED FOR LEVEL 1; HELPS SUBTYPE
NCS findings — consistent with GBS subtype, normal, or not yet performed
Consistent with AIDP: Prolonged distal motor latencies, reduced motor conduction velocities (<70–75% of LLN), prolonged F-wave latencies, conduction block or temporal dispersion, normal or mildly reduced CMAP amplitudes, abnormal sensory NCS (reduced SNAP amplitudes and/or slowed velocities).

Consistent with AMAN: Normal distal motor latencies, normal or near-normal motor conduction velocity, normal F-waves, markedly reduced CMAP amplitudes (reflecting axonal loss), normal sensory NCS (motor-selective axonopathy). Anti-GM1/GD1a antibodies support this diagnosis.

Consistent with AMSAN: Same as AMAN plus abnormal sensory NCS (reduced SNAPs) — combined axonal loss of both motor and sensory fibers. More severe presentation.

Inexcitable nerves: Complete loss of all evoked responses — reflects severe early axonal injury (AMAN) or very severe demyelination. May predict prolonged recovery.
Reversible conduction failure (RCF) in AMAN: Anti-GM1/GD1a IgG antibodies bind to nodes of Ranvier, causing conduction block that can resolve with treatment or over time. On initial NCS this may appear as conduction block (suggesting AIDP) but serial studies show rapid recovery of CMAP amplitudes without demyelinating features — this is RCF, not true demyelination. Distinguishing AIDP from AMAN/RCF requires serial NCS.
Step 4 · GBS Subtype Classification — AIDP vs. AMAN vs. AMSAN
Subtype classification informs pathophysiology, prognosis, and antiganglioside antibody testing. AIDP dominates Western countries (~90%); AMAN and AMSAN are more prevalent in East Asia and after Campylobacter infection. All subtypes are treated with IVIG or plasmapheresis — subtyping does not currently change immunotherapy choice but impacts prognosis.

Based on NCS pattern and clinical features — which subtype best fits?
Antiganglioside antibody testing: Anti-GM1/GD1a IgG: positive in ~50–80% of AMAN cases (Asian cohorts); much lower in AIDP. Anti-GQ1b IgG: positive in ~90% of MFS and MFS-GBS overlap. Anti-GD1b: associated with AMSAN and sensory ataxic GBS. These tests are supportive but not required for diagnosis. Available from commercial labs (Mayo Clinic, hospital reference labs). Positive anti-GM1 in a patient with motor-predominant axonal pattern strongly supports AMAN.

Bickerstaff brainstem encephalitis (BBE): Anti-GQ1b positive, CNS involvement (altered consciousness, hyperreflexia) + external ophthalmoplegia + ataxia. GBS-BBE-MFS overlap spectrum recognized.
Step 5 · EGRIS — Erasmus GBS Respiratory Insufficiency Score
The EGRIS (Walgaard C et al. Ann Neurol 2010;67:781–787) predicts the probability of requiring mechanical ventilation within the first week of hospital admission. AUC 0.81 in international validation (IGOS cohort, Doets et al. Ann Neurol 2022). Score range: 0–7. Three components: days from onset to admission, facial/bulbar weakness, and MRC sum score at admission.
EGRIS validation data (Doets et al. Ann Neurol 2022, n=541 IGOS cohort): AUC 0.81 for discrimination between patients requiring vs. not requiring MV within week 1. External validation confirmed accuracy across multiple countries (Netherlands, UK, Brazil, Bangladesh, Japan, Australia). EGRIS overestimated MV probability in the international cohort (41% vs. 20% observed) — recalibration improved fit but rank order preserved. Clinical use: EGRIS should guide triage to appropriate care level (ward vs. high-dependency vs. ICU) and frequency of respiratory monitoring, not as a binary cutoff for intubation.
Component 1 — Days from onset of weakness to hospital admission SHORTER = HIGHER RISK
Number of days between first symptom of limb weakness and hospital admission
Shorter interval = faster progression = higher risk. A patient admitted within 1 day of weakness onset has a rapidly progressive course — high risk. EGRIS points: ≥14 days = 0 pts; 8–13 days = 1 pt; ≤7 days = 2 pts. This reflects the observation that fast progressors are more likely to develop respiratory failure early.
Component 2 — Facial and/or bulbar weakness at admission BULBAR = HIGHER RISK
Facial palsy (unilateral or bilateral) and/or bulbar dysfunction (dysphagia, dysphonia) at time of hospital admission
Facial and/or bulbar weakness signals involvement of cranial nerve nuclei or cranial nerves — ascending weakness has reached the brainstem level, suggesting rapid proximal spread. Bulbar dysfunction also directly impairs airway protection (impaired cough, aspiration risk). EGRIS points: absent = 0 pts; present = 1 pt. Even mild facial palsy (asymmetric) or mild dysphagia should be scored as present.
Component 3 — MRC sum score at admission LOWER MRC = HIGHER RISK
MRC sum score (6 muscle groups × both sides × MRC 0–5): shoulder abductors, elbow flexors, wrist extensors, hip flexors, knee extensors, foot dorsiflexors
MRC sum score ranges from 0 (complete paralysis) to 60 (full strength). Score each of 12 muscle groups (6 bilateral) on 0–5 scale. EGRIS points: MRC 51–60 = 0 pts; MRC 41–50 = 2 pts; MRC 31–40 = 4 pts; MRC ≤30 = 6 pts (note: some sources use a 0–4 scale with different cutoffs — the standard is 0/2/4/6 as above from Walgaard 2010).

Neck flexion: Not part of the standard EGRIS MRC sum, but neck flexor weakness correlates strongly with respiratory muscle weakness in GBS. Always test neck flexion at admission as a supplementary warning sign.
20/30 rule for respiratory monitoring: FVC <20 mL/kg or NIF worse than −30 cmH₂O warrants ICU admission and serial respiratory monitoring (FVC, NIF q4–6h). FVC <15 mL/kg + distress or NIF worse than −20 cmH₂O = elective intubation threshold. SpO₂ and ABG are late and unreliable — clinical observation and serial bedside PFTs are paramount.

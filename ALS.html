Step 1 · Criterion 1 — Progressive Motor Impairment
The Gold Coast Criteria collapse Definite, Probable, and Possible ALS into a single diagnostic entity. All three criteria must be met simultaneously.
Gold Coast Criteria (Table 1, Shefner et al. 2020) — all 3 required:
1. Progressive motor impairment documented by history or repeated clinical assessment, preceded by normal motor function
2. UMN + LMN dysfunction in ≥1 body region (both in same region if only one region involved), OR LMN dysfunction alone in ≥2 body regions
3. Investigations excluding other disease processes
Criterion 1 · Progressive Motor Impairment REQUIRED
Progressive motor impairment documented by history or repeated clinical assessment, preceded by normal motor function
Progression must be clearly documented — either by history (patient/caregiver report of new or worsening motor deficits over time) or by repeated clinical assessments demonstrating objective decline. The motor impairment must be preceded by a period of normal motor function, distinguishing ALS from static or congenital motor disorders.
Key points from Gold Coast consensus: ALS is a clinically focal onset disease in most cases, though generalized onset is recognized. Progression may be asymmetric and multifocal. The criteria do not specify a minimum duration of progression — clinical judgment determines whether the course is clearly progressive. Cognitive and behavioural changes (up to 50% of ALS patients; ALS-FTD in ~15%) are now recognized as part of the disease spectrum but are not required for diagnosis.
Step 2 · Define UMN and LMN Signs
Review the Gold Coast definitions of UMN and LMN dysfunction. You will apply these in the body region assessment (Step 3).
Upper Motor Neuron Dysfunction ≥1 of the following
Increased deep tendon reflexes — including reflex in a clinically weak/wasted muscle, or spread to adjacent muscles
Hyperreflexia in a limb with clinical weakness and wasting is a key UMN sign in ALS — the combination of LMN (weakness, wasting) and UMN (hyperreflexia) in the same muscle is pathognomonic. Reflex spread (e.g., jaw jerk from a facial tap, crossed adductor response) also qualifies. A preserved or brisk reflex in a clinically denervated muscle is highly suspicious for concurrent UMN involvement.
A reflex that is simply present (grade 2+) in a weak muscle qualifies — it need not be pathologically brisk. The key is that in the context of significant LMN dysfunction (wasting, weakness), a preserved or increased reflex indicates co-existing UMN involvement.
Pathological reflexes — Hoffman sign, Babinski sign, crossed adductor reflex, or snout reflex
Any of: Hoffman sign (finger flexion response to middle finger PIP flick — bilateral is more specific), Babinski sign (extensor plantar response), crossed adductor reflex (contralateral adductor activation with ipsilateral knee jerk), snout/pout reflex (orbicularis oris contraction with lip tap — indicates bilateral corticobulbar involvement). These are unambiguous UMN signs.
Isolated extensor plantar response can be non-specific (age, cervical myelopathy) — its significance should be interpreted in clinical context. Bilateral Hoffman sign is more specific for UMN dysfunction than unilateral. A brisk jaw jerk in isolation may reflect normal variants in the elderly.
Velocity-dependent increase in tone (spasticity)
Clasp-knife quality resistance to passive limb movement that increases with velocity — distinct from rigidity (velocity-independent, Parkinson's) and paratonia (gegenhalten). In ALS, spasticity may be subtle early, particularly in a limb with coexisting significant LMN dysfunction (hypotonia from denervation may mask spasticity).
Slowed, poorly coordinated voluntary movement not attributable to LMN weakness or Parkinsonian features
UMN-pattern slowing: slowness of fine motor tasks (finger tapping, rapid alternating movements) that is disproportionate to the degree of weakness or wasting, suggesting corticospinal dysfunction. Must be distinguished from bradykinesia (Parkinson's — pill-rolling, rigidity, hypomimia) and from pure LMN weakness (which can also slow movements but without the dissociation). Pseudobulbar features (emotional lability, slowed tongue movements) also reflect corticobulbar UMN involvement.
Lower Motor Neuron Dysfunction Clinical OR EMG (see below)
LMN dysfunction requires EITHER:
Clinical: Muscle weakness AND muscle wasting in the same muscle/region
EMG: Chronic neurogenic change (large MUPs — increased duration and/or amplitude; polyphasia and MUP instability are supportive but not obligatory) AND ongoing denervation (fibrillation potentials, positive sharp waves, OR fasciculation potentials)

Region-level LMN threshold (for body region counting): Abnormalities in ≥2 limb muscles innervated by different roots AND different nerves, OR 1 bulbar muscle, OR 1 thoracic muscle — by clinical exam or EMG.
Clinical LMN evidence — muscle weakness AND wasting
Both weakness (reduced MRC grade, <5/5) AND visible or palpable muscle wasting must be present in the same muscle or muscle group. Weakness alone without atrophy, or atrophy alone (e.g., disuse), does not qualify for the clinical LMN criterion. Fasciculations visible on inspection provide additional supportive evidence but are not sufficient alone for clinical LMN diagnosis under Gold Coast.
Fasciculations are not required, but their diffuse presence substantially increases diagnostic confidence. Tongue fasciculations are particularly specific for bulbar LMN involvement. Absence of fasciculations should prompt consideration of alternative diagnoses.
EMG LMN evidence — chronic neurogenic change AND ongoing denervation
Chronic neurogenic change (mandatory): Large motor unit potentials — increased duration and/or increased amplitude. Polyphasia and MUP instability (satellite potentials, nascent MUPs) are supportive but not obligatory.

Ongoing denervation (mandatory — at least one of): Fibrillation potentials, positive sharp waves, OR fasciculation potentials.

EMG LMN signs can substitute for clinical weakness/wasting — this is the primary advantage of EMG, allowing identification of sub-clinical LMN involvement in clinically unaffected muscles, thereby facilitating earlier diagnosis.
Per Gold Coast consensus: NCS/EMG remains a critically important (though not obligatory) investigation. It should be used to exclude other nerve diseases (multifocal motor neuropathy, Kennedy's disease, radiculopathy, myopathy) and to identify LMN involvement in clinically silent muscles. A thorough EMG screen of multiple body regions is standard of care in ALS evaluation.

Step 3 · Criterion 2 — Body Region Assessment
Four body regions: Bulbar, Cervical, Thoracic, Lumbosacral. Assign UMN/LMN status to each region based on your Step 2 findings.
Criterion 2 is met by EITHER:
Option A: UMN + LMN dysfunction in the same body region (≥1 region with both)
Option B: LMN dysfunction alone in ≥2 body regions (no UMN required)

LMN involvement in a region requires: ≥2 limb muscles from different roots AND different nerves (by exam or EMG), OR 1 bulbar muscle, OR 1 thoracic muscle.

Step 4 · Criterion 3 — Exclusion of Other Disease Processes
Investigations should be guided by the clinical presentation. The appropriate workup is not fixed — it depends on what alternative diagnoses are plausible in a given patient.
Gold Coast Criteria (Footnote 4): "The appropriate investigations depend on the clinical presentation, and may include nerve conduction studies and needle EMG, MRI or other imaging, fluid studies of blood or CSF, or other modalities as clinically necessary." The criteria do not mandate a specific test panel — clinical judgment determines which investigations are needed to exclude relevant mimics.
Core investigations to consider CONTEXT-DEPENDENT
NCS/EMG — excluded multifocal motor neuropathy, Kennedy disease, radiculopathy, myopathy
NCS is critically important to exclude: multifocal motor neuropathy (MMN — conduction block on motor NCS, responds to IVIg), Kennedy disease (X-linked spinobulbar muscular atrophy — sensory NCS abnormal, AR repeat expansion on genetics), hereditary spastic paraplegia mimics, radiculopathy/plexopathy (structural MRI, sensory NCS). Myopathy (elevated CK, short-duration MUPs on EMG, biopsy if needed).
EMG is not obligatory per Gold Coast, but the consensus group stated it is "critically important." It is the single most useful investigation for confirming LMN involvement in sub-clinical muscles and excluding mimics. A thorough screen of ≥2–3 body regions is standard.
Neuroimaging — excluded structural causes (cervical myelopathy, brainstem lesion, cortical lesion)
MRI brain and spine is indicated in most ALS evaluations to exclude: cervical spondylotic myelopathy (the most important structural mimic — cord compression causing UMN signs in lower limbs), brainstem lesion (glioma, MS — mimicking bulbar involvement), cortical lesion (stroke, tumor — mimicking UMN signs). ALS-specific MRI findings (corticospinal tract T2 signal, motor cortex atrophy, iron deposition on SWI) are supportive but not diagnostic.
Blood / CSF studies — excluded metabolic, inflammatory, and genetic mimics
Context-dependent panel. Core blood tests: CK (elevated in MMN, myopathy, Kennedy disease; mildly elevated in ALS), B12, copper, thyroid function, paraneoplastic panel (if relevant), GM1 antibodies (MMN), anti-HMGCR/anti-SRP if myopathy suspected. Genetic testing: SOD1, C9orf72, FUS, TARDBP — relevant for counseling and emerging gene therapies; Kennedy disease (AR repeat in SBMA gene). CSF: useful if inflammatory/infectious CNS disease is in the differential. Neurofilament light chain (NfL) and NfH are supportive biomarkers — elevated in ALS but not yet in formal diagnostic criteria.
Neurofilament levels (NfL/NfH — blood or CSF) are the most promising emerging biomarker in ALS. They correlate with disease progression rate and survival, and can help distinguish ALS from mimics. However, they are not currently incorporated into the Gold Coast Criteria as a diagnostic criterion.
Overall assessment — alternative diagnosis identified?
Based on the clinical presentation, EMG, imaging, and lab findings, has an alternative diagnosis been identified that better explains the findings? Common mimics in practice: multifocal motor neuropathy, Kennedy disease (SBMA), cervical myeloradiculopathy, primary lateral sclerosis (UMN only for ≥4 years), hereditary spastic paraplegia, inclusion body myositis, myasthenia gravis (bulbar mimics), lymphoma, paraneoplastic motor neuron disease, hirayama disease (young onset), post-polio syndrome.
PLS (Primary Lateral Sclerosis) is specifically relevant: patients with UMN signs in ≥2 body regions but no LMN signs for ≥4 years from symptom onset are classified as PLS rather than ALS. Gold Coast criteria do not separately diagnose PLS — these patients would not fulfill Criterion 2 for ALS until LMN signs appear.

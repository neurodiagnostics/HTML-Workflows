Step 1 · Clinical Presentation & Triggers
RCVS is characterised by recurrent thunderclap headaches (TCH) over 1–4 weeks, caused by reversible multifocal constriction of cerebral arteries. It predominantly affects women (F:M 2–10:1) aged 20–50 years. Up to 80% have an identifiable trigger. It is the most common cause of TCH after SAH has been excluded, and one of the most frequently misdiagnosed conditions in neurovascular medicine.
Epidemiology & natural history:
Recurrent TCH is nearly pathognomonic for RCVS — a single TCH could be SAH, but recurrent TCHs over days to weeks in a haemodynamically stable alert patient are RCVS until proven otherwise.
Timeline: Headaches peak in first 1–2 weeks; vasoconstriction peaks at ~2 weeks and resolves within 12 weeks (by definition). Over 70% of hospitalised patients develop complications within 2 weeks (stroke, SAH, PRES).
Prognosis: >90% have benign course; morbidity and mortality are driven by stroke complications. mRS 0–1 at discharge in >85% without stroke.
Headache characterisation RECURRENT TCH IS THE HALLMARK
Thunderclap headache — severe headache peaking within 60 seconds; “worst headache of my life” or “like a thunderclap”
The TCH of RCVS characteristically: (1) peaks within seconds; (2) lasts minutes to hours (self-limiting, unlike SAH which persists); (3) recurs multiple times over 1–4 weeks — this recurrent pattern distinguishes RCVS from SAH; (4) is triggered by Valsalva, exertion, emotion, bathing, orgasm, or occurs spontaneously. The pain usually resolves within 1–3 hours, contrasting with the persistent headache of SAH. During the recurrence phase, headaches may occur multiple times per day.
RCVS headache vs. SAH headache: The single most helpful clinical distinguisher is recurrence — SAH causes one catastrophic headache with persistent meningeal irritation; RCVS produces repeated stereotyped TCH attacks with normal intervals. Neck stiffness is much more common in SAH (from blood) and rare in RCVS (unless convexal SAH with meningeal blood).
Triggers UP TO 80% HAVE AN IDENTIFIABLE TRIGGER
Vasoconstrictive drug/substance exposure — SSRIs, SNRIs, triptans, ergotamines, nasal decongestants (pseudoephedrine, oxymetazoline), cocaine, amphetamines, cannabis, MDMA, bromocriptine, tacrolimus, cyclophosphamide
Vasoconstrictive drug exposure is the strongest single trigger and the most important for management — all implicated agents must be stopped immediately. SSRIs/SNRIs are among the most common triggers (serotonergic pathway activation increases vascular tone). Triptans are both a trigger and a contraindication for treatment (worsen vasoconstriction). Cannabis is increasingly recognised as a trigger, particularly in younger patients. Nasal decongestants (OTC sympathomimetics) are frequently overlooked. Immunosuppressants (tacrolimus, cyclophosphamide) cause RCVS as a delayed drug effect, typically 1–6 months after initiation.
Crucial treatment implication: If RCVS is suspected, avoid vasoconstrictive medications including triptans (frequently prescribed for “migraine” prior to RCVS diagnosis) and ergotamines. These can perpetuate or worsen vasoconstriction. Glucocorticoids are also contraindicated in RCVS and may worsen outcomes (Ducros A et al. retrospective series); this is why RCVS/PACNS differentiation is critical.
Postpartum state — within 4 weeks of delivery; most common single trigger; associated with pre-eclampsia, eclampsia, and PRES
The postpartum period is the most common single trigger for RCVS in women. RCVS typically occurs within the first week after delivery. The pathogenesis involves oestrogen withdrawal, fluid shifts, and sympathetic hyperactivation after delivery. Postpartum RCVS has a higher rate of complications than non-postpartum RCVS, including intracerebral haemorrhage. Postpartum RCVS exists on a spectrum with postpartum PRES (posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome). Eclampsia shares a similar pathophysiology (endothelial dysfunction and blood-brain barrier breakdown).
Exertional / Valsalva triggers — TCH during or immediately after sexual activity (orgasm), exercise, coughing, straining, bathing, or emotional stress
Female sex — F:M ratio 2:1 to 10:1; RCVS2 score component (+1 point)
Associated neurological features PRESENT IN ~25% AT ONSET
Focal neurological deficits — hemiparesis, visual disturbance, speech disturbance, hemisensory loss; develops in ~25% due to ischaemic or haemorrhagic complications
Focal deficits in RCVS usually develop 1–3 weeks after headache onset, not at the very first TCH (distinguishing from aneurysmal SAH). They reflect the ischaemic or haemorrhagic complications of vasoconstriction: watershed/border zone infarcts (from proximal vasoconstriction), convexal SAH (from distal cortical artery rupture), intracerebral haemorrhage, or PRES (vasogenic oedema from posterior circulation autoregulatory failure). Visual symptoms are particularly common and reflect posterior circulation involvement.
Neck stiffness — less common in RCVS than SAH; if present, suggests convexal SAH or meningeal irritation; neck stiffness strongly favours SAH over RCVS when presenting with first TCH
Step 2 · Calabrese Diagnostic Criteria (2007, Modified 2012)
The original criteria were proposed by Calabrese LH et al. (Ann Intern Med 2007;146:34) and subsequently refined based on large case series by Ducros A (Lancet Neurol 2012;11:906). All criteria must be met for a definitive diagnosis. RCVS is confirmed retrospectively when vasoconstriction resolves within 3 months — at presentation, a “probable RCVS” designation is used pending follow-up imaging.
Calabrese criteria (all required for definitive RCVS):
1. Acute severe headache (thunderclap or reaching maximum in <1 minute) with or without focal neurological deficits or seizures
2. Monophasic course with no new symptoms after 1 month
3. Segmental cerebral artery vasoconstriction on angiography (direct or indirect: CTA, MRA, or TCD)
4. No evidence of aneurysmal subarachnoid haemorrhage (aneurysmal SAH excluded by imaging)
5. Near-normal CSF (protein <100 mg/dL, WBC <10 cells/μL, normal glucose)
6. Complete or near-complete reversal of vasoconstriction within 12 weeks on follow-up imaging
Criterion 1 — Acute severe headache REQUIRED
Acute severe headache (thunderclap or maximal within 1 minute) — may be accompanied by focal deficits or seizures
Criterion 2 — Monophasic course REQUIRED
Monophasic course — no new symptoms after 1 month; headaches recur but do not worsen or add new neurological features after 4 weeks
This criterion distinguishes RCVS from PACNS, which typically has a progressive or relapsing course over months. In RCVS, the headache phase lasts 1–4 weeks. New neurological symptoms emerging after 4–6 weeks, or a relapsing course, should prompt reconsideration of PACNS or other diagnoses. Relapses of RCVS can occur (months to years later, especially with continued trigger exposure) but are uncommon.
Criterion 3 — Segmental vasoconstriction on imaging REQUIRED
Segmental vasoconstriction of cerebral arteries on CTA, MRA, or catheter angiography — “string of beads” pattern; may be absent early (<7 days from symptom onset)
Vasoconstriction in RCVS proceeds centripetally: begins in the distal small pial and cortical arteries (not visible on CTA/MRA), then progresses to mid-sized and proximal arteries over 2 weeks. This explains why early imaging (first 1–3 days) may be falsely negative on non-invasive angiography. Maximum vasoconstriction is typically reached 2–3 weeks after symptom onset.

Angiographic patterns: Multifocal “string of beads” (alternating dilatation and constriction) of mid-to-distal cerebral arteries. Typically bilateral and affects multiple vascular territories simultaneously. Proximal vessels (carotid siphon, M1, P1) are usually spared — involvement of proximal/carotid arteries (intracranial carotid) is a negative predictor for RCVS (RCVS2 score: −2 points).

TCD: Elevated MCA mean flow velocity (>120–140 cm/s) may detect proximal vasoconstriction earlier than MRA, though TCD is operator-dependent and may miss distal vasoconstriction.
Negative early CTA/MRA does not exclude RCVS. If clinical suspicion is high (recurrent TCH with triggers), repeat MRA at 7–14 days. Catheter angiography remains the gold standard and has the highest sensitivity, especially for distal vasoconstriction. The intra-arterial nimodipine test (vasodilation with nimodipine during catheter angiography) is positive in RCVS and negative in PACNS — a useful bedside differentiator.
Criterion 4 — No aneurysmal SAH REQUIRED
Aneurysmal SAH excluded — CT head negative for subarachnoid blood, OR blood pattern is convexal (cortical surface, not basal cisterns); no intracranial aneurysm on CTA/MRA
RCVS can cause convexal (cortical) SAH — thin blood on the cortical surface in the sulci — which is distinct from the basal cistern SAH of aneurysmal rupture. Convexal SAH in a young patient with recurrent TCH and triggers is highly suggestive of RCVS. Basal cistern SAH (interpeduncular, suprasellar) strongly suggests aneurysmal SAH and should prompt full aneurysm workup. CT within 6 hours is ~100% sensitive for SAH; LP for xanthochromia is indicated if CT is negative and >6 hours from symptom onset.
Criterion 5 — Near-normal CSF REQUIRED IF LP PERFORMED
CSF near-normal — protein <100 mg/dL, WBC <10 cells/μL, normal glucose; pleocytosis or very elevated protein favours PACNS or infection
CSF in RCVS may be: (1) entirely normal; (2) mildly elevated protein (<100 mg/dL) without pleocytosis; (3) show RBCs if convexal SAH has occurred. RCVS red flag: WBC >10 cells/μL or protein >100 mg/dL without bloody tap should prompt workup for PACNS, meningitis/encephalitis, or other vasculitic causes. In PACNS, CSF is abnormal in ~80–90% (pleocytosis, elevated protein, oligoclonal bands). Importantly, both RCVS and PACNS have negative inflammatory serology (normal CRP, ESR, CBC, metabolic panel, ANA, ANCA).
Criterion 6 — Vasoconstriction reversal within 12 weeks CONFIRMS DIAGNOSIS RETROSPECTIVELY
Complete or near-complete reversal of vasoconstriction within 12 weeks of symptom onset on follow-up angiography (CTA, MRA, or DSA)
This criterion is the defining feature of RCVS but is only confirmable retrospectively. Follow-up MRA or CTA should be performed at 6–12 weeks from symptom onset. Persistent vasoconstriction at 12 weeks suggests: (1) PACNS — the most important alternative; (2) Intracranial atherosclerosis; (3) Fibromuscular dysplasia; (4) Cocaine or other drug-induced vasculopathy (may be slower to resolve); (5) Rare RCVS variants with prolonged course. Vessel wall MRI (high-resolution 3T MRI) is emerging as a key tool: RCVS shows no vessel wall enhancement; PACNS shows circumferential wall thickening and enhancement.
Step 3 · RCVS2 Score (Rocha EA et al. Neurology 2019)
The RCVS2 score (Rocha EA, Topcuoglu MA, Singhal AB. Neurology 2019;92:e639) was developed to distinguish RCVS from other intracranial arteriopathies — especially PACNS — at the time of admission, using clinical and imaging features available on presentation. Score range: −2 to +10. Validated in patients aged 18–55 years with new-onset abnormal intracranial vascular imaging.
RCVS2 diagnostic thresholds (Rocha 2019, n=30 RCVS vs. 80 non-RCVS):
Score ≥5: Sensitivity 90%, Specificity 99% for RCVS vs. other arteriopathies
Score ≤2: Sensitivity 85%, Specificity 100% for excluding RCVS
Score 3–4: Equivocal — use bedside clinical approach (recurrent TCH + trigger + normal parenchymal MRI, or convexal SAH → RCVS likely; subcortical infarcts + abnormal CSF → PACNS more likely)

Limitation: Validated in a stroke-centre population, not in pure TCH populations. The RCVS2 score is most useful for differentiating RCVS from other angiopathies on vascular imaging — not for diagnosing RCVS from primary TCH alone (thunderclap headache alone carries 5 points, strongly influencing the score).

Step 4 · Imaging Findings & CSF
Multimodal imaging is essential for RCVS diagnosis, complication detection, and PACNS differentiation. Brain parenchymal imaging may be normal initially (>70% of cases at presentation) but develops complications in the 1–3 week window. Vascular imaging may also be initially negative if obtained within the first week.
Expected imaging timeline in RCVS (Ducros A. Lancet Neurol 2012):
Days 0–7: Brain MRI often normal; CTA/MRA may miss distal vasoconstriction; convexal SAH (if present) visible on CT
Days 7–14: Vasoconstriction becomes visible on CTA/MRA; complications begin (ischaemia, cortical haemorrhage, PRES)
Days 14–21: Maximum vasoconstriction; highest stroke/haemorrhage risk
Weeks 4–12: Gradual resolution of vasoconstriction on serial imaging
Vascular imaging findings MULTIFOCAL CONSTRICTION IN ≥2 VESSELS REQUIRED
Intracranial vascular imaging (CTA / MRA / DSA) — multifocal segmental constriction in ≥2 vascular territories
CTA: Rapid, widely available; sensitivity ~50–70% for distal vasoconstriction (misses very distal and small pial vessels); best for proximal/mid-sized vessel involvement and for ruling out aneurysm.
MRA (TOF): No contrast required; sensitivity for distal vasoconstriction is limited; most useful for follow-up at 6–12 weeks to document resolution.
DSA (catheter angiography): Gold standard; highest sensitivity for both proximal and distal vasoconstriction; allows intra-arterial nimodipine test (positive response = vasodilation confirms RCVS, no response suggests PACNS).
Vessel wall MRI (3T high-resolution): No vessel wall enhancement in RCVS; circumferential concentric enhancement in PACNS. This is the most powerful non-invasive differentiator and is increasingly available.
Intracranial carotid / proximal vessel involvement — constriction of ICA siphon, M1, or proximal basilar; RCVS2 negative predictor (−2 points)
RCVS characteristically involves mid-sized and distal arteries (M2–M4, A2–A4, P2–P4). Involvement of the intracranial internal carotid artery, M1 segment, or proximal basilar is atypical for RCVS and suggests an alternative diagnosis such as PACNS, intracranial atherosclerosis, or dissection. The RCVS2 score assigns −2 points for carotid (intracranial) involvement — a negative predictor because it is uncommon in RCVS and common in other arteriopathies.
Brain parenchymal complications DEVELOP 1–3 WEEKS AFTER HEADACHE ONSET
Convexal (cortical) SAH — thin haemorrhage on cortical surface, sulci; NOT in basal cisterns; RCVS2 +1 point
Convexal SAH is the most characteristic haemorrhagic complication of RCVS, occurring in ~20–35% of cases. It results from rupture of distal cortical arteries under vasoconstriction-related pressure. Key distinction: convexal SAH in RCVS is typically thin, sulcal, and over the cerebral convexity (NOT in the basal cisterns which are the hallmark of aneurysmal SAH). On FLAIR MRI, convexal SAH appears as bright sulcal signal. This pattern is also seen in cerebral amyloid angiopathy (in older patients) — an important mimic in patients >60 years.
PRES (posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome) — T2/FLAIR hyperintensity in posterior cerebral regions (occipital, parietal); vasogenic oedema
PRES occurs in ~10–20% of RCVS cases and represents blood-brain barrier breakdown from vasoconstriction-induced posterior circulation dysautoregulation. Clinical features: headache, visual disturbance, seizures, confusion. MRI shows T2/FLAIR hyperintensity in the posterior parietal and occipital regions (sometimes frontal), representing vasogenic oedema. PRES and RCVS share pathophysiology and frequently coexist; postpartum RCVS-PRES overlap is particularly common. PRES is also associated with hypertension, pre-eclampsia, immunosuppressants, and renal failure.
Cerebral infarction — ischaemic stroke; watershed / border-zone distribution typical of RCVS (from proximal vasoconstriction); deep subcortical infarcts favour PACNS
Ischaemic complications occur in ~5–15% of RCVS cases, typically 1–3 weeks after symptom onset. The distribution is important: border-zone (watershed) infarcts suggest proximal vasoconstriction affecting perfusion pressure — favours RCVS. Deep subcortical (white matter, basal ganglia, thalamic) infarcts suggest small vessel disease in vessel walls — strongly favours PACNS or other small-vessel vasculitis. Cortical infarcts may occur in both.
Vessel wall MRI (3T high-resolution) — no enhancement in RCVS vs. circumferential concentric enhancement in PACNS
High-resolution vessel wall MRI (VWI) at 3T is the most powerful non-invasive tool to differentiate RCVS from PACNS: RCVS: vessel walls are thin, show no gadolinium enhancement (non-inflammatory vasoconstriction). PACNS: vessel walls show circumferential thickening and concentric enhancement (inflammatory destruction). VWI is increasingly available at tertiary centres and avoids the need for brain biopsy in many cases where PACNS was previously the only definitive confirmation. A negative VWI in a patient with vasoconstriction strongly supports RCVS.
Step 5 · RCVS vs. PACNS — Side-by-Side Differentiator
Distinguishing RCVS from primary angiitis of the CNS (PACNS) is the most critical diagnostic challenge in this field, because their treatments are diametrically opposed: RCVS is managed with calcium channel blockers and trigger avoidance; PACNS requires immunosuppression with steroids ± cyclophosphamide. Steroids may worsen RCVS outcomes (Ducros A et al. retrospective data). The RCVS2 score was specifically developed for this differentiation.

Vessel wall MRI (VWI) result MOST POWERFUL NON-INVASIVE DIFFERENTIATOR
3T high-resolution vessel wall MRI with gadolinium contrast — assess for arterial wall enhancement at sites of vasoconstriction
Vessel wall MRI (VWI) images the arterial wall itself rather than the lumen. At sites of angiographic vasoconstriction:

RCVS findings: Vessel walls appear thin and show no gadolinium enhancement. This reflects non-inflammatory vasoconstriction — the wall architecture is intact, only the tone is dysregulated. This is the expected finding in pure RCVS.

PACNS findings: Vessel walls show circumferential concentric thickening and gadolinium enhancement, reflecting active transmural inflammation and destruction of the vessel wall. Enhancement persists or worsens on follow-up if untreated.

Technical requirements: Must be performed at 3T (1.5T has insufficient resolution for small intracranial vessel walls). Black-blood sequences with fat suppression and gadolinium contrast. Images should be acquired at the sites of angiographic abnormality. Interpretation requires neuroradiologist experience with VWI.

Evidence (Mandell DM et al. Stroke 2012): In a prospective study of patients with intracranial vasculopathy, VWI correctly identified RCVS (no enhancement) vs. CNS vasculitis (concentric enhancement) in all cases. This has been replicated in multiple cohort studies.

Caveats: (1) Steroid treatment before VWI may suppress enhancement in PACNS, producing false-negative results — perform VWI before starting immunosuppression. (2) Very proximal large-vessel involvement in PACNS may not show wall enhancement on all studies. (3) A small number of atypical RCVS cases show mild enhancement; clinical context remains essential.
Practical note: VWI is the single most valuable investigation when RCVS and PACNS cannot be differentiated on clinical and standard imaging grounds. It should be performed urgently in equivocal cases before any treatment decision, particularly before starting steroids. It is increasingly available at tertiary stroke and neuroradiology centres.
Brain biopsy result GOLD STANDARD — WHEN NON-INVASIVE TOOLS INCONCLUSIVE
Stereotactic or open brain biopsy targeting leptomeninges and cortex at a site of angiographic or MRI abnormality
Brain biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosing PACNS when non-invasive tests are inconclusive. It is indicated when: (1) VWI is equivocal or technically limited; (2) the patient has a deteriorating clinical course and the diagnosis is uncertain; (3) treatment decisions cannot be safely made without histological confirmation.

RCVS biopsy findings: Normal vessel wall architecture. No inflammatory infiltrate. No granulomas. No fibrinoid necrosis. This finding, combined with the clinical picture, confirms RCVS and excludes PACNS.

PACNS biopsy findings: Transmural granulomatous or lymphocytic inflammation of vessel walls. Multi-nucleated giant cells (granulomatous PACNS, most common). Fibrinoid necrosis in necrotising PACNS. Amyloid deposition in amyloid-beta-related angiitis (ABRA), a subtype of PACNS.

Yield and limitations: Sensitivity of biopsy for PACNS is ~53–74% (false negatives occur due to sampling error — PACNS is segmental and may not be present at the biopsy site). Specificity is high when positive. Targeting a site of leptomeningeal enhancement on MRI or an area of parenchymal or vessel abnormality improves yield. A normal biopsy does not definitively exclude PACNS but significantly raises the probability of RCVS or another diagnosis.

Risk: Complication rate approximately 0.5–2% (haemorrhage, infection, neurological deficit depending on biopsy site). Should be performed at a specialised neurovascular/neurosurgical centre.
Timing: Brain biopsy should be performed before starting steroids or cyclophosphamide — treatment may suppress the histological findings and reduce diagnostic yield. If the patient is rapidly deteriorating and PACNS is clinically suspected, empirical treatment while awaiting or pursuing biopsy may occasionally be necessary, but this decision should involve neurology and rheumatology jointly.

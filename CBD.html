Step 1 · Prerequisites (Both cr-CBD and p-CBD)
These entry criteria apply to both the clinical research criteria for probable CBD (cr-CBD) and the possible CBD criteria (p-CBD). They differ only in age cutoff and family history.
Two sets of criteria (Table 5):
cr-CBD (Clinical Research Criteria for Probable Sporadic CBD): More specific — maximizes chance of diagnosing true CBD pathology without other tau contamination. Requires age ≥50, no family history, no tau mutations. Phenotype: Probable CBS or FBS/naPPA + ≥1 CBS feature.
p-CBD (Possible CBD Criteria): Broader — still emphasizes tau pathology but more inclusive. No age minimum, family history allowed, tau mutations allowed. Includes PSPS phenotype. Phenotype: Possible CBS or FBS/naPPA/PSPS + ≥1 CBS feature (b–f only).
Shared mandatory prerequisites BOTH SETS
Insidious onset and gradual progression
The onset must be insidious (not sudden) and the course must be one of gradual progression. Acute or subacute presentations, or step-wise deterioration, should prompt consideration of alternative diagnoses — particularly Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD), which may mimic CBS with rapid progression. Neither patient with CBD died in the first year in a rapidly progressive dementia series (vs. 8/22 CJD cases dying within 12 months of onset).
Symptom duration of at least 1 year
A minimum of 1 year of symptoms is required to exclude rapidly progressive conditions more likely to represent other pathologies (especially CJD). Mean disease duration in compiled CBD cases was 6.6 years (SD 2.4, range 2.0–12.5). Mean time to presentation in one series was 3.0 years after symptom onset.
cr-CBD specific prerequisites PROBABLE ONLY
Age at onset ≥50 years (cr-CBD) / No minimum (p-CBD)
Age ≥50 is required for cr-CBD — this will identify 98% of CBD cases and exclude pathologies with younger age at onset (e.g., FTLD). Mean age at CBD symptom onset was 63.7 years (SD 7.0, range 45–77.2). No age minimum is set for p-CBD, allowing for young-onset familial CBD cases related to tau mutations.
Family history (>1 relative with similar neurodegenerative disease)
A family history of >1 relative with a similar neurodegenerative disease excludes cr-CBD (sporadic) but is permitted in p-CBD. Familial CBD-like cases may be associated with MAPT tau mutations (e.g., N296N) producing CBD-like pathology. Note: familial CBS may also reflect GRN mutations with FTLD-TDP pathology — a non-tau etiology that would then be excluded by the mutation exclusion criteria.
Step 2 · Clinical Phenotype (Table 4)
Four phenotypes are associated with CBD pathology. Select which phenotype(s) are present. The phenotype determines which criteria set (cr-CBD vs. p-CBD) can be applied. CBS features (a–f) will be assessed in Step 3.
Phenotype requirements (Table 5):
cr-CBD: Probable CBS or FBS/naPPA + ≥1 CBS feature (a–f)
p-CBD: Possible CBS or FBS/naPPA/PSPS + ≥1 CBS feature (b–f, NOT feature a)

PSPS is included only in p-CBD — it is much more likely to represent PSP than CBD.

Corticobasal Syndrome
Probable CBS: Asymmetric — ≥2 of motor (a–c) + ≥2 of cortical (d–f)
Possible CBS: May be symmetric — ≥1 of motor (a–c) + ≥1 of cortical (d–f)
Motor (a–c): rigidity/akinesia, dystonia, myoclonus
Cortical (d–f): orobuccal/limb apraxia, cortical sensory deficit, alien limb
FBS
Frontal Behavioral-Spatial Syndrome
≥2 of: (a) executive dysfunction, (b) behavioral or personality changes, (c) visuospatial deficits
When present: needs ≥1 CBS feature (a–f for cr-CBD; b–f for p-CBD) to fulfill phenotype requirement

Step 3 · CBS Features (a–f)
These six features define the corticobasal syndrome and also serve as required adjunct features for FBS, naPPA, and PSPS phenotypes. For PSPS in p-CBD, only features b–f are eligible (not feature a).
CBS feature requirements for non-CBS phenotypes:
cr-CBD: FBS or naPPA requires ≥1 CBS feature from (a–f)
p-CBD: FBS, naPPA, or PSPS requires ≥1 CBS feature from (b–f) only — feature (a) limb rigidity/akinesia is excluded as an adjunct feature for p-CBD non-CBS phenotypes

CBS sub-feature thresholds (for CBS phenotype itself — Table 4):
Probable CBS: Asymmetric + ≥2 motor (a–c) + ≥2 cortical (d–f)
Possible CBS: ≥1 motor (a–c) + ≥1 cortical (d–f) [may be symmetric]
Motor features (a–c) — Corticobasal motor
(a) Limb rigidity or akinesia
Limb rigidity (85% of compiled CBD cases, 57% at presentation) and bradykinesia/akinesia (76% overall, 48% at presentation) are the most common motor findings. Asymmetry was documented in 73% of CBD cases with parkinsonism. The nature of limb rigidity may relate to parkinsonism, dystonia, or gegenhalten/paratonia. For CBS phenotype: must be asymmetric for Probable CBS; may be symmetric for Possible CBS. Note: (a) does NOT count as a CBS adjunct feature for p-CBD non-CBS phenotypes.
(b) Limb dystonia
Limb dystonia is a CBS-defining feature — 38% of compiled CBD cases had it (20% at presentation). Clinical series report higher rates (59–71%) in mixed CBS/CBD series, suggesting lower true frequency in pathology-confirmed cases. May be superimposed on myoclonus. For CBD, the nature of dystonia is poorly characterized — limb dystonia may coexist with parkinsonism or higher cortical features. The presence of limb dystonia, bradykinesia, and rigidity can make assessing ideomotor apraxia challenging.
(c) Limb myoclonus
Myoclonus in 27% of compiled CBD cases (15% at presentation) — notably lower than rates reported in CBS clinical series (55–93%). May be superimposed on dystonia. Most commonly in upper extremities, occasionally face. Types described: focal myoclonus, stimulus-sensitive myoclonus, action myoclonus. Low-amplitude action myoclonus may be mistaken for tremor. A very short latency cortical reflex myoclonus may be characteristic in CBS but not confirmed in CBD specifically.
Note: Low-amplitude action myoclonus may resemble tremor. Repetitive myoclonic bursts in CBD may be mistaken for tremor in some case reports.
Higher cortical features (d–f) — Corticobasal cortical
(d) Orobuccal or limb apraxia
Limb apraxia in 57% of compiled CBD cases (45% at presentation) — core to all previous diagnostic criteria. Ideomotor apraxia is most commonly described. Limb-kinetic apraxia also reported in some series. Orobuccal apraxia may co-occur. Importantly: the presence of limb dystonia, bradykinesia, and rigidity can make assessing ideomotor apraxia challenging, and diagnosing limb-kinetic apraxia is essentially impossible in this context. "Apraxia of eyelid opening" is often a misnomer representing pretarsal blepharospasm rather than true apraxia.
(e) Cortical sensory deficit
Cortical sensory loss (astereognosis, graphesthesia, tactile extinction) in approximately 27% of compiled CBD cases where reported (25% at presentation). Less than half of compiled cases had data on cortical sensory testing — the true frequency may differ. Visual neglect can also occur in CBD (but also in AD). Cortical sensory deficit reflects parietal cortex involvement and is an important lateralized higher cortical sign.
(f) Alien limb phenomena — more than simple levitation
Alien limb in 30% of compiled CBD cases (22% at presentation). Definition includes complex unintentional limb movements interfering with normal tasks, and the sensation that a limb was foreign or had a will of its own. Criteria specify "more than simple levitation" — isolated limb levitation without the above features does not qualify. What constitutes alien limb remains a matter of debate and varies across criteria sets; this operationalization attempts to distinguish pathological alien limb from simple motor overflow or levitation.
Overall asymmetry of presentation (motor features)
Asymmetry is required for Probable CBS (not required for Possible CBS). Asymmetry of parkinsonism was documented in 73% of compiled CBD cases with parkinsonism. Asymmetry is one of the distinguishing features of CBS vs. PSP — PSP-Richardson syndrome typically has more symmetric or axially predominant parkinsonism.

Step 4 · Exclusion Criteria (Both cr-CBD and p-CBD)
All seven exclusion criteria apply to both cr-CBD and p-CBD. Any single positive exclusion criterion prevents a CBD diagnosis. These are not ranked — any one disqualifies.
Exclusion criteria for both cr-CBD and p-CBD (Table 5 footnote a):
1. Evidence of Lewy body disease: classic 4-Hz PD resting tremor, excellent and sustained levodopa response, or hallucinations
2. Evidence of MSA: dysautonomia or prominent cerebellar signs
3. Evidence of ALS: presence of both upper AND lower motor neuron signs
4. Semantic- or logopenic-variant PPA
5. Structural lesion suggestive of focal cause
6. GRN mutation or reduced plasma progranulin; TDP-43 mutations; FUS mutations
7. Evidence of AD: low CSF Aβ42/tau ratio or positive amyloid PET (¹¹C-PiB); or genetic mutation suggesting AD (presenilin, APP)

Note on tau mutations (MAPT): These are an exclusion for cr-CBD but not p-CBD. GRN/TDP-43/FUS are exclusions for both.
Evidence of Lewy body disease — classic 4-Hz PD resting tremor, excellent and sustained levodopa response, or hallucinations
Classic 4–6 Hz resting tremor with excellent and sustained levodopa response is highly suggestive of idiopathic PD. Hallucinations strongly suggest PD or DLB — only a single published CBD patient had hallucinations (with levodopa treatment); hallucinations not associated with levodopa were also rarely described in brain bank subjects. Transient mild–moderate levodopa improvement can occur in CBD, but sustained response is rare and an exclusion. Levodopa-induced dyskinesias rarely develop in CBD.
Evidence of MSA — dysautonomia or prominent cerebellar signs
Prominent dysautonomia (orthostatic hypotension, urinary/bowel dysfunction, sexual dysfunction) or prominent cerebellar features (limb or gait ataxia, cerebellar dysarthria) suggest multiple system atrophy rather than CBD. CBD series do not routinely describe these features. "Prominent" is the operative word — mild autonomic features or a single subtle cerebellar sign may not warrant exclusion without clinical judgment.
Evidence of ALS — both upper AND lower motor neuron signs
The co-presence of UMN (hyperreflexia, spasticity, Babinski) AND LMN (fasciculations, wasting, reduced reflexes) signs in multiple body regions suggests ALS/MND spectrum. Upper motor neuron signs alone are not excluded (they occur in CBD) — it is the multisegmental combined UMN + LMN pattern that excludes.
Semantic- or logopenic-variant PPA
Semantic variant PPA (svPPA) — fluent speech, impaired single-word comprehension, semantic knowledge loss — is associated with FTLD-TDP (GRN or C9orf72) or FTLD-tau (rarely CBD). Logopenic variant PPA — word-finding pauses, sentence repetition impairment — is strongly associated with AD pathology. Neither is associated with CBD. Only the nonfluent/agrammatic variant PPA (naPPA) is a CBD phenotype.
Structural lesion suggestive of focal cause
GRN mutation or reduced plasma progranulin; TDP-43 mutations; FUS mutations
These mutations are associated with non-tau (TDP-43 or FUS) FTLD pathology rather than CBD tau pathology — they are exclusions for both cr-CBD and p-CBD. Familial CBS may be associated with GRN mutations (FTLD-TDP) rather than CBD. Note: MAPT tau mutations are separately an exclusion for cr-CBD only (not p-CBD). GRN/TDP-43/FUS remain exclusions for both.
Evidence of AD — low CSF Aβ42/tau ratio, positive ¹¹C-PiB amyloid PET, or AD genetic mutation (presenilin, APP)
Results strongly suggestive of AD pathology on CSF (low Aβ42/tau ratio) or amyloid PET (positive ¹¹C-PiB PET) are exclusion criteria. Caveat: excluding cases with evidence of amyloid may result in missing approximately 14% of CBD cases (Dickson, personal communication, 2012) — coexisting amyloid pathology is relatively common. Genetic mutations associated with AD (presenilin-1/2, APP) are also exclusion criteria.
MAPT tau mutation — exclusion for cr-CBD only; permitted in p-CBD
Tau mutations (MAPT) have resulted in CBD-like pathology (e.g., N296N mutation causing CBD-like pathology including astrocytic plaques and swollen achromatic neurons). Whether these familial CBD-like cases should be classified with typical sporadic CBD is unclear. cr-CBD requires sporadic occurrence and excludes MAPT mutations. p-CBD permits MAPT tau mutations, allowing inclusion of familial cases in broader research protocols targeting tau pathology as a class.

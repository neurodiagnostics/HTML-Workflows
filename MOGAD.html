Step 1 · Clinical Context and Patient Selection
MOGAD is a distinct CNS demyelinating disorder, separate from MS and AQP4-IgG–seropositive NMOSD. Correct patient selection before testing is essential — MOG-IgG is not a screening test for all demyelinating disease.
Epidemiology (Banwell et al. 2023, Lancet Neurol):
Incidence: 1.6–3.4 per million per year; Prevalence: ~20 per million (95% CI 11–34)
Age: All ages affected. Paediatric-onset frequent; no marked sex predominance (F=M), unlike MS (F>M) and NMOSD (F>>M)
Disease course: Monophasic OR relapsing — ~27–36% of adults relapse within ~1–2 years of first attack; 4-year relapse risk 31.7%, 8-year risk 36.3% (UK cohort, n=183)

Key principle: MOG-IgG testing should be targeted, not universal. The positive predictive value (PPV) depends heavily on pretest probability — low-prevalence populations yield high false-positive rates.
Patient presentation type REQUIRED
Which presentation has prompted consideration of MOGAD?
High pretest probability (testing strongly recommended):
• Children <11 years with optic neuritis or ADEM: MOG-IgG approaches 50% frequency
• Adults with optic neuritis + severe disc oedema: MOG-IgG ~39% (vs ~5% in all adult optic neuritis)
• AQP4-IgG-seronegative NMOSD features: bilateral optic neuritis, LETM, area postrema syndrome
• Recurrent optic neuritis or NMOSD-like phenotype that is AQP4-seronegative

Lower pretest probability (interpret with caution):
• Adult optic neuritis without features suggestive of MOGAD: MOG-IgG ~5%
• MS with clinical/radiological features typical of MS + positive CSF OCBs: MOG-IgG 0.3–2.5% — routine testing NOT recommended
PPV formula (Panel 2, Banwell et al.): PPV = (sensitivity × prevalence) / [(sensitivity × prevalence) + (1−specificity) × (1−prevalence)]. A low-positive titre in a low-prevalence population (e.g., confirmed MS) will produce predominantly false-positive results. Universal MOG-IgG screening in adults with confirmed MS is strongly discouraged.
Step 2 · Criterion B — MOG-IgG Test Result
A positive MOG-IgG on cell-based assay (CBA) is the core diagnostic criterion. Serum is the preferred specimen. The distinction between clear positive, low positive, and negative drives the diagnostic pathway and additional requirements.
Assay methodology (Panel 1, Banwell et al. 2023):
Preferred: Live cell-based assay (CBA) using full-length human MOG — highest specificity
Acceptable alternative: Fixed CBA — lower sensitivity/specificity than live CBA
Not recommended: ELISA — insufficient sensitivity and specificity
Secondary antibody: IgG Fc, IgG1-specific, or IgG heavy+light with external validation

Clear positive (live CBA): ≥2 doubling dilutions above assay cutoff, or above assay-specific titre/flow-cytometry cutoff
Clear positive (fixed CBA): Titre ≥1:100
Low positive (fixed CBA): Titre ≥1:10 and <1:100

An international blinded comparative study of 7 live CBAs from 4 centres showed excellent interlaboratory agreement for clear positives; low-positive samples were more frequently discordant.
Serum MOG-IgG result REQUIRED — core criterion
Serum MOG-IgG on cell-based assay
Testing timing: Highest yield at the time of an incident clinical attack, ideally before corticosteroids, IVIG, or apheresis (these can reduce detectable MOG-IgG). If tested after acute treatment and negative, repeat testing ≥3 months later is advised.

Titre kinetics: MOG-IgG titres usually decline over time. Persistent seropositivity is associated with a 2–10× increased relapse likelihood, particularly with persistently high titres. Seroconversion from negative to positive is extremely uncommon.

CSF testing: Only 42–62% of serum-positive patients are also CSF-positive. CSF MOG-IgG testing is reserved for seronegative patients with strong clinical/MRI features, especially if serum testing was confounded by acute therapies.
Clinical implication of titre: Positive predictive value for MOGAD-consistent features increases with titre. Low-positive titres (<1:100 on fixed CBA) are less discriminatory and can be found in confirmed MS, other neurological diseases, and healthy individuals. Always confirm low-positive results with a second independent live CBA from a laboratory with MOG-IgG expertise.
Step 3 · Criterion A — Core Clinical Demyelinating Event
MOGAD diagnosis requires one of six core clinical event types. Select the primary presentation. Clinical features that help distinguish MOGAD from MS and AQP4-NMOSD are shown for each event type.
Diagnostic criterion A requires ONE of the following core events (Figure 3, Banwell et al. 2023). A single event may qualify (monophasic) or a patient may have had multiple events (relapsing). The most current or relevant event should be characterised below.
Core clinical event type REQUIRED
Select the primary core clinical event
Optic neuritis: Unilateral or bilateral reduced VA developing over hours–days, often with retro-orbital pain (exacerbated by eye movement), colour vision loss, visual field loss. Supported by T2-hyperintense signal or gadolinium enhancement of optic nerve/chiasm. In MOGAD: bilateral simultaneous ON is 31–58% (vs <5% in MS); optic disc oedema is moderate-severe in 45–95%; visual acuity often <6/60 at nadir. Recovery is typically rapid with corticosteroids, but relapses can occur during taper. Relapsing optic neuritis occurs in 30–50% of MOG-IgG patients.

Myelitis: Acute motor, sensory, sphincter, or erectile disturbance developing over hours–days. T2 hyperintensity on spinal MRI. LETM (≥3 vertebral segments) in >60% of cases. Central cord/H-sign in 66–75%; conus lesion characteristic. Moderate-severe EDSS (>4) at nadir in ≥50%. Motor recovery generally good; bladder/bowel/sexual dysfunction can be permanent in up to 30% despite good motor recovery.

ADEM: Acute polyfocal neurological deficits with encephalopathy (not postictal) + multifocal T2 bright lesions. Most common presentation in children <10 years. MOG-IgG found in ~50% of paediatric ADEM. Infectious prodrome (mainly respiratory) preceding in 40–75%. Complete/near-complete clinical and radiological resolution in >70% of children.

Cerebral monofocal/polyfocal deficits: Deficits developing over hours–days referable to T2-hyperintense lesions in middle cerebellar peduncle, periventricular white matter, or cortical/subcortical areas. Juxtacortical/cortical lesions more common than in MS.

Brainstem/cerebellar: Clinical deficits developing over hours–days with T2 lesions in brainstem/cerebellum. Large middle cerebellar peduncle lesions suggest MOGAD (rare in MS and AQP4-NMOSD). Area postrema syndrome less common in MOGAD than AQP4-NMOSD.

Cerebral cortical encephalitis with seizures: Fever, headache, reduced consciousness, seizures or status epilepticus. Cortical FLAIR lesions (FLAMES: FLAIR hyperintense lesions in anti-MOG encephalitis with seizures) with overlying leptomeningeal enhancement. Occurs in ~6.7% (19/285) of MOG-IgG patients. Consider dual antibody testing (NMDA-R in CSF/serum) — 4–7.5% overlap with NMDA-R encephalitis.
Cumulative onset data (Banwell et al. 2023, N=1361 combined cohorts): Optic neuritis ~50%; Transverse myelitis ~20%; ADEM or brain lesions ~15%; ON+TM overlap ~5%; Other/unknown ~10%. In adults (N=377), optic neuritis predominates (~55%). In children (N=176), ADEM/brain lesions are more frequent (~40%).
Step 4 · Supporting Clinical and MRI Features
Supporting features are required when MOG-IgG is low positive, reported positive without titre, or CSF-only positive. They are not required for clear positive results. Select all that apply for the patient's presentation.
When are supporting features required? (Figure 3, Banwell et al. 2023)
Clear positive serum MOG-IgG: No additional supporting features required (proceed directly)
Low positive / no titre reported / CSF-only positive: Must be AQP4-IgG seronegative AND have ≥1 supporting clinical or MRI feature from the list below

Supporting features are organised by presentation type below. Even for clear-positive results, document supporting features — they strengthen diagnostic confidence.
Optic neuritis supporting features
Bilateral simultaneous optic neuritis
Longitudinal optic nerve involvement (>50% nerve length on MRI)
Perineural optic sheath enhancement on MRI
Optic disc oedema on fundoscopy or MRI
Myelitis supporting features
Longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis (LETM, ≥3 vertebral segments)
LETM is present in >60% of MOG-IgG myelitis cases. First-attack LETM rarely occurs in MS. In a population-based study, LETM occurred in only 1.3% (11/863) of MS patients vs. 26% (7/27) of MOG-IgG patients (p<0.001). MRI is normal in up to 10% of spinal attacks.
Central cord lesion (H-sign) — axial grey matter involvement
T2-hyperintense lesions centrally located in 66–75% of MOG-IgG myelitis. Grey matter restricted (H-sign) in 30–50%. Bright spotty lesion appearance more typical of AQP4-NMOSD. 20–25% of MOG-IgG spinal lesions do not involve grey matter.
Conus lesion
Brain / brainstem / cerebral supporting features
Multiple ill-defined T2-hyperintense lesions (supratentorial ± infratentorial white matter)
MOGAD brain lesions tend to be bilateral, ill-defined ("fluffy"), and large — with deep grey matter involvement. In contrast to MS (small, ovoid, well-demarcated juxtacortical/periventricular). Brain T2 lesions are absent in 47–68% of MOGAD patients (especially ON/myelitis presentations).
Deep grey matter involvement (thalami, basal ganglia)
Ill-defined T2 involving pons, middle cerebellar peduncle (MCP), or medulla
Large MCP lesions are a characteristic MOGAD pattern — rare in MS and AQP4-NMOSD. In a study of brainstem/cerebellar involvement, large confluent MCP lesions were disproportionately seen in MOGAD. Area postrema and midbrain lesions can cause episodic nausea/vomiting (>48h) in a small proportion of patients.
Cortical lesion ± leptomeningeal enhancement (FLAMES pattern)
FLAMES (FLAIR hyperintense lesions in anti-MOG encephalitis with seizures) are cortical lesions most apparent on FLAIR. Unilateral or bilateral cortical leptomeningeal enhancement (linear pattern) is seen with cerebral cortical encephalitis. This pattern helps distinguish cortical MOGAD from viral encephalitis and MS.
Step 5 · Criterion C — Exclusion of Better Diagnoses
MOGAD must be diagnosed only after other conditions that better explain the clinical picture have been excluded. This is a clinician-judgment step. Key alternative diagnoses are evaluated below.
Key exclusions required (Criterion C, Figure 3, Banwell et al. 2023):
MS: MOG-IgG frequency in confirmed MS: 0.3–2.5%. A patient with optic neuritis meeting 2024 McDonald criteria for MS + positive CSF OCBs + low-titre MOG-IgG → more appropriately diagnosed as MS
AQP4-IgG–seropositive NMOSD: Dual positivity (AQP4-IgG + MOG-IgG) is very rare; AQP4-IgG nearly always has high titre while MOG-IgG is low. When dual positive, AQP4-IgG is the clinically dominant diagnosis
Other diagnoses: Infectious/para-infectious (viral encephalitis, PML), neoplastic, metabolic, vascular, or genetic demyelinating disorders
Multiple sclerosis exclusion REQUIRED
Does the clinical/radiological picture better fit MS (2024 McDonald criteria)?
MS features that argue against MOGAD: small ovoid juxtacortical/periventricular T2 lesions (Dawson's fingers, S-shaped U-fibre lesions), frequent CSF oligoclonal bands (>80% in MS vs. infrequent in MOGAD), silent lesion accrual between attacks ("0% of 110 follow-up spinal MRIs in 81 MOGAD patients had new silent lesions" — Banwell et al.), persistent T1-hypointense lesions (frequent in MS, extremely rare in MOGAD), central venule sign, progressive disease course in absence of relapses.
Practical guidance: Although most patients with MOGAD will not meet McDonald criteria for MS, and vice versa, some patients will satisfy both sets of criteria. In this case, clinical judgment — including titre level, imaging character, and longitudinal course — should guide the final diagnosis. Ill-defined ("fluffy") brain lesions with deep grey matter involvement and bilateral optic neuritis with disc oedema strongly favour MOGAD over MS despite meeting McDonald DIS/DIT criteria.
AQP4-IgG NMOSD exclusion REQUIRED
AQP4-IgG serostatus
Many labs run AQP4-IgG and MOG-IgG concurrently. A clearly positive AQP4-IgG in a patient with NMOSD-like features (optic neuritis, LETM, area postrema syndrome) strongly supports AQP4-IgG–seropositive NMOSD, and MOG-IgG testing is not indicated. Patients presenting with NMOSD features who are AQP4-IgG seronegative should be tested for MOG-IgG. When dual positivity occurs, AQP4-IgG titre is nearly always high and MOG-IgG titre is low; the clinical course follows AQP4-IgG–seropositive NMOSD.
Other alternative diagnoses CONSIDER
Other diagnoses reasonably excluded (infectious, neoplastic, genetic, metabolic, vascular)?
Differential diagnoses to consider in MOGAD (appendix pp 12–15, Banwell et al.):
• Infectious: Viral encephalitis (HSV, VZV, EBV), PML, HTLV-1 myelopathy
• Autoimmune: NMDA-R encephalitis (4–7.5% overlap with MOG-IgG — test CSF/serum in seizure presentations), CNS vasculitis, sarcoidosis
• Neoplastic: CNS lymphoma, paraneoplastic encephalomyelitis
• Genetic: Leukodystrophies (leukodystrophy-like MRI pattern is a rare MOGAD variant in children — occurs but is atypical)
• Vascular: Compressive or vascular myelopathy, Susac syndrome
Note: MOGAD and COVID-19 association remains uncertain — high community prevalence makes causal attribution tenuous.

Red flags (Panel 3, Banwell et al.) should prompt reconsideration of the MOGAD diagnosis. Also characterise the current disease course — important for relapse risk stratification and treatment decisions.

Disease course characterisation CLINICAL MANAGEMENT
Current disease course classification
A relapse is defined as a new clinical attack occurring >30 days following onset of a prior attack. In incident cohort studies: 27–36% of adults relapsed within 10–16 months; 4-year relapse risk 31.7%; 8-year risk 36.3% (UK cohort, n=183, Satukijchai et al.). In paediatric incident cohorts: 17–20% of 200 children relapsed over 1–7 years (median time to first relapse 11 months). Persistent MOG-IgG seropositivity increases relapse likelihood 2–10×.

Relapse timing: Most common in first 6 months; can occur during or after corticosteroid taper. Some patients have early cluster then stability; others have ongoing relapses beyond 12 months. Relapse-free progression (as in MS) does not appear to occur in MOGAD.
Treatment evidence: No completed RCTs. Network meta-analysis (Xian et al. PMC2022): IVIG, oral corticosteroids, MMF, azathioprine, and rituximab all significantly reduced relapse incidence vs. no treatment (ORs 0.075–0.34). DMT (MS-targeted therapies) increased relapse risk (OR 1.3) and should be avoided. Meta-analysis of rituximab (n=238, 13 studies): 55% remained relapse-free (95% CI 0.49–0.61); ARR reduced by 1.36 (95% CI 1.02–1.71, p<0.001). Oral prednisone ≥12.5 mg/day for ≥3 months after first attack was associated with 88% reduced relapse risk (multicentre cohort, n=109). First azathioprine RCT in MOGAD (NCT05349006) is ongoing.
MOG-IgG persistence (if serial testing available) PROGNOSTIC
Longitudinal MOG-IgG serostatus (serial testing)
Persistent MOG-IgG seropositivity is associated with 2–10× increased relapse likelihood, particularly when titres remain high. Titres usually decline with time but may remain positive for years. Seroconversion from negative to positive at relapse is extremely uncommon. Onset titre does not have strong prognostic value for recovery or relapse — it is the persistence pattern that matters. Standardised definition of persistence and its prospective prognostic significance require further study.

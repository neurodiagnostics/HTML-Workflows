Step 1 · Hunt-Hess Grade
The Hunt-Hess scale (Hunt WE, Hess RM. J Neurosurg 1968;28:14–20) classifies severity of aneurysmal SAH based on clinical condition at presentation. Originally designed as a guide to neurosurgical operative risk, it remains the most widely used clinical grading system. Grades 4–5 define poor-grade SAH — associated with 40–80% mortality but not zero. Aggressive treatment is indicated unless brainstem reflexes are absent.
Key principle: Assess after initial resuscitation and, where applicable, after EVD placement (which may improve grade by lowering ICP). Increase grade by 1 in the presence of serious systemic disease or severe angiographic vasospasm — though this modifier is inconsistently applied in practice.

Poor-grade (HH 4–5): Rebleeding risk is highest in first 24h (~5.7%/day). All grades warrant early aneurysm securing (ideally ≤24h). EVD for obstructive hydrocephalus or IVH. Nimodipine 60mg PO q4h × 21 days for all grades. SBP goal <160 mmHg pre-securing.

Serious systemic disease OR severe angiographic vasospasm present? — either criterion increases HH grade by 1
The original Hunt-Hess criteria specify that two conditions increase the grade by one level: (1) serious systemic disease, or (2) severe vasospasm on angiography.

Systemic disease: Uncontrolled hypertension with end-organ damage, active cardiac failure or arrhythmia, significant pulmonary disease, hepatic insufficiency (Child-Pugh B/C), or renal failure requiring dialysis.

Severe angiographic vasospasm: Vessel narrowing ≥67% on DSA, CTA, or MRA at presentation — rarely present acutely but may be observed on diagnostic angiography performed at the time of aneurysm securing. In practice this modifier is inconsistently applied — document explicitly and justify if increasing grade.
Why this matters: Systemic disease compounds poor-grade SAH prognosis by limiting tolerance for vasospasm treatment (induced hypertension), anesthesia risk for aneurysm securing, and ICU interventions. Cardiac complications of SAH (neurogenic stunned myocardium, Takotsubo-pattern, troponin elevation) are common in HH 4–5 and may themselves require grade adjustment.
Step 2 · WFNS Grade
The World Federation of Neurological Surgeons (WFNS) scale (1988) uses GCS score and presence of focal motor deficit to provide a more objective grading system than Hunt-Hess, avoiding subjective terms like "stupor." WFNS Grades 4–5 define poor-grade SAH. Both Hunt-Hess and WFNS should be documented — they measure related but distinct clinical dimensions.
WFNS grade map:
Grade I: GCS 15, no motor deficit
Grade II: GCS 13–14, no motor deficit
Grade III: GCS 13–14, motor deficit present
Grade IV: GCS 7–12, with or without motor deficit
Grade V: GCS 3–6, with or without motor deficit

Note on GCS timing: Assess GCS before sedation/intubation whenever possible. If already intubated on arrival, use best known pre-intubation GCS. The motor component of GCS (M1–M6) is the single strongest predictor of neurological outcome in aSAH.
GCS total score at presentation PRE-SEDATION / PRE-INTUBATION PREFERRED
Glasgow Coma Scale total score (E+V+M) — select best pre-intubation value
GCS components: Eye (E): 1=none, 2=pain, 3=voice, 4=spontaneous. Verbal (V): 1=none, 2=sounds, 3=words, 4=confused, 5=oriented. Motor (M): 1=none, 2=extension/decerebrate, 3=flexion/decorticate, 4=withdrawal, 5=localizing, 6=obeys. For intubated patients, use E+M only (V=1T) and note limitation.

Prognostic weight: Motor score M1–M2 (decerebrate/flaccid) correlates most strongly with poor outcome. GCS 3 carries ~70–80% mortality but isolated treatment nihilism is not justified — no single admission finding is a sufficient predictor to deny treatment.
Focal motor deficit? DISTINGUISHES WFNS II vs III AT GCS 13–14
Focal neurological deficit — hemiparesis, aphasia, or other focal motor finding at presentation
Focal deficit in WFNS refers primarily to motor deficit (hemiparesis, hemiplegia). The focal deficit modifier only changes grade for GCS 13–14 patients (distinguishes Grade II from Grade III) — for GCS ≤12 or GCS 15, presence/absence of focal deficit does not change WFNS grade.

CN III palsy in SAH: Posterior communicating artery (PComm) aneurysms classically cause CN III palsy — ptosis, mydriasis, exodeviation. This is a surgical emergency (external compression by the aneurysm wall). Pupil involvement distinguishes from diabetic CN III palsy where the pupil is typically spared.
Early ischemia at presentation: Focal deficits at SAH onset may reflect direct intracerebral hematoma from the ruptured aneurysm, acute vasospasm (rare), or ischemia from acute ICP elevation reducing CPP. These are distinct from DCI which develops days later.
Step 3 · CT Findings — Modified Fisher Grade
The Modified Fisher Scale (Frontera JA et al. Neurosurgery 2006;59:21–27, n=1355 patients from 4 RCTs) predicts risk of symptomatic vasospasm based on the initial non-contrast head CT. It improves on the original Fisher scale (1980) by providing monotonically increasing vasospasm risk across all four grades and accounting for intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH). Modified Fisher is preferred for vasospasm prediction.
CT assessment definitions:
Thin SAH: <1 mm in depth in any cistern or fissure
Thick SAH: ≥1 mm in depth — completely filling ≥1 cistern or fissure
IVH: Blood present in any part of the ventricular system

Vasospasm risk by modified Fisher grade (Frontera 2006): Grade 1: ~24%; Grade 2: ~33%; Grade 3: ~33%; Grade 4: ~40%. Note: original Fisher Grade 4 had counterintuitively lower vasospasm risk than Grade 3 — the modified scale corrects this by incorporating IVH as an additive risk factor.

Intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) — present on initial CT? Predicts hydrocephalus and independently increases DCI risk
IVH occurs in ~20–40% of aneurysmal SAH, most commonly with anterior communicating artery (AComm) and posterior fossa aneurysms. Severe IVH with cast formation causes obstructive hydrocephalus requiring emergency EVD.

Blood in the ventricular system introduces oxyhemoglobin and inflammatory mediators into the CSF circulation, likely contributing to vasospasm and cortical spreading depression — mechanisms of DCI beyond simple arterial narrowing.

Modified Graeb score: Used in some centers to quantify IVH burden; Graeb ≥5 predicts need for intraventricular tPA and prolonged EVD. Routine IVH thrombolysis (alteplase 1–2 mg intraventricular q8h) reduces clot burden and may reduce vasospasm in selected centers.
EVD indications in SAH: Acute obstructive hydrocephalus on CT, GCS ≤13 with ventriculomegaly, or progressive neurological deterioration from hydrocephalus. ICP monitoring via EVD allows targeted CPP management (CPP goal ≥60 mmHg in poor-grade SAH).
Acute hydrocephalus — ventricular dilatation on CT; Evans ratio >0.3; temporal horn enlargement >2mm
Acute hydrocephalus occurs in ~20–30% of SAH at presentation, most commonly from IVH causing obstruction at the aqueduct or 4th ventricle, or from subarachnoid blood impairing CSF reabsorption. Evans ratio >0.3 (frontal horn width / maximum inner skull width) is the standard threshold. Temporal horn enlargement (>2mm) is an early sign.

Subacute/chronic hydrocephalus: Communicating hydrocephalus may develop 2–4 weeks post-SAH from impaired CSF reabsorption. Presents as progressive cognitive decline, gait instability, incontinence (normal pressure hydrocephalus phenotype). May require VP shunt. Serial head CT every 2–3 days in the acute period is standard.
Step 4 · Vasospasm Surveillance — TCD and Clinical Triggers
Cerebral vasospasm peaks between days 5–14 post-SAH. TCD is the standard non-invasive surveillance tool. Clinical deterioration is the most important trigger for escalation to CTA/CTP or DSA. TCD is best validated for the MCA (AUC ~0.65 for DCI prediction). All aSAH patients should receive daily or every-other-day TCD from day 3–14.

TCD thresholds — MCA (best validated vessel):
MFV <120 cm/s: No significant vasospasm — routine daily monitoring
MFV 120–149 cm/s: Mild vasospasm — increase monitoring frequency, optimise volume status
MFV 150–199 cm/s: Moderate vasospasm — consider CTA/CTP; escalate with any clinical change
MFV ≥200 cm/s: Severe vasospasm — correlates with ≥50% vessel diameter reduction; CTA+CTP or DSA indicated
Lindegaard Ratio (LR) = MCA MFV / ipsilateral extracranial ICA MFV: LR >3 supports vasospasm over hyperemia; LR >6 = severe vasospasm. Critical when MFV 120–199 cm/s.

Rate-of-rise trigger: MFV increase >50 cm/s/day or >30 cm/s/study = escalation threshold independent of absolute value.
Current TCD MCA MFV (peak bilateral) DOCUMENT DAILY DAY 3–14
TCD MCA mean flow velocity — peak bilateral; also document ACA, ICA siphon, basilar if available
TCD technique: Insonate MCA at 45–65mm depth via transtemporal window. Record peak systolic, end-diastolic, and mean flow velocity. Calculate pulsatility index (PI = [PSV−EDV]/MFV; normal 0.6–1.1). Elevated PI with normal MFV may reflect elevated ICP rather than vasospasm.

Limitations: Cannot assess cortical/perforator vasospasm (only large vessels). Poor temporal window in ~10–15% (elderly women, obesity). Highly operator-dependent. Normal TCD does not exclude DCI.
Hyperemia vs. vasospasm: Post-SAH hyperemia causes elevated MFV but LOW Lindegaard ratio (<3). True vasospasm causes elevated MFV with HIGH LR (>3). Distinguishing these is critical — induced hypertension in a hyperemic state can worsen outcome.

Clinical trigger for escalation ANY CHANGE = ACT
New neurological change from post-ictus baseline — new focal deficit, GCS decline ≥2 points, new or escalating headache, worsening confusion not explained by other cause
Clinical deterioration is the primary trigger for urgent DCI workup, independent of TCD values. Causes in the day 3–14 window: DCI from vasospasm, rebleeding (if aneurysm not secured), hydrocephalus, seizure, hyponatremia (SIADH vs. cerebral salt wasting), fever, sepsis, medication effects, or PE.

Hyponatremia (~30–40% of SAH): SIADH (euvolemic) vs. cerebral salt wasting (hypovolemic, high urine Na) — treatment is opposite. CSW: treat with IV saline and fludrocortisone. SIADH: fluid restriction is contraindicated in acute SAH where hypovolemia worsens DCI — use 3% NaCl instead.

Fever: Target normothermia aggressively. Fever >38.5°C increases cerebral metabolic demand and worsens ischemic injury in the DCI window.
Step 5 · Delayed Cerebral Ischemia (DCI) — Vergouwen 2010 Consensus
DCI is defined by the Vergouwen consensus (Stroke 2010;41:2391–2395) as the occurrence of focal neurological impairment or global neurological deterioration not attributable to other causes, lasting >1 hour, or new ischemic infarction on CT/MRI. DCI complicates ~20–30% of aSAH and carries OR 2.65 for unfavorable neurological outcome (GOS 1–3) at discharge.
DCI consensus definition (Vergouwen et al. Stroke 2010):
Clinical criterion: New focal neurological impairment (hemiparesis, aphasia, apraxia, hemianopia, neglect) OR GCS decline ≥2 points from best post-ictus baseline, lasting >1 hour — not attributable to other causes
Imaging criterion: New CT or MRI infarction within 6 weeks of SAH, not present on the 24–48h post-aneurysm-securing scan, not attributable to procedural causes
Timing: Onset typically day 3–14 (peaks day 7–8); may occur up to day 21
DCI ≠ vasospasm: Angiographic vasospasm (vessel narrowing) and DCI are distinct entities. DCI can occur without large-vessel vasospasm (microvascular spasm, cortical spreading depression, microthrombosis), and vasospasm can occur without DCI. Clinical DCI criteria do not require imaging vasospasm.
Clinical DCI criterion EITHER CRITERION = DCI DIAGNOSIS
New focal neurological deficit lasting >1 hour OR GCS decline ≥2 points lasting >1 hour, not attributable to other causes (rebleeding, hydrocephalus, seizure, metabolic, medication, infection, systemic)
Mandatory exclusion before diagnosing DCI: Rebleeding (CT: new high-density, sudden onset); Acute hydrocephalus (CT: ventriculomegaly); Seizure (EEG — post-ictal Todd's paralysis mimics DCI); Metabolic (hyponatremia, hyperglycemia, uremia, hepatic); Medication effect (oversedation); Systemic (sepsis, hypotension, hypoxia, PE); Procedure-related infarct (within 24h of clipping/coiling).

Assess every 2–4 hours with standardized neurological exam. Must be new from post-ictus baseline. Hemispatial neglect, ataxia, and dysarthria also qualify as focal deficits.
Continuous EEG: Increasingly used in poor-grade SAH (HH 4–5) where clinical exam is unreliable. Non-convulsive seizures occur in ~10–20% of SAH. Cortical spreading depression (CSD) is a strong predictor of DCI and detectable by subdural or depth electrodes in NCC centers. Not yet standard of care but growing.
Imaging DCI criterion EITHER CRITERION = DCI DIAGNOSIS
New CT or MRI infarction within 6 weeks of SAH — not present on 24–48h post-securing scan, not attributable to procedural causes
Imaging confirmation requires: (1) new hypodensity on CT or DWI restriction on MRI; (2) absent on the 24–48h post-aneurysm-securing baseline scan; (3) no alternative explanation (procedural, embolic from cardiac source, etc.). MRI DWI is more sensitive than CT for early ischemia, especially posterior fossa and small cortical infarcts.

CT perfusion (CTP): CTP detects reduced CBF/CBV and prolonged MTT before infarction is visible on non-contrast CT. Triggered by clinical deterioration or TCD alarm thresholds. CBF <25 mL/100g/min or CBV <2 mL/100g suggests ischemic penumbra. CTP guides decision to perform DSA ± intervention.
DCI treatment protocol: Step 1: Induced hypertension (SBP 160–200 mmHg via norepinephrine/phenylephrine; maintain euvolemia). Step 2: Optimize nimodipine (do NOT stop — manage BP separately). Step 3: CTA + CTP or DSA if not already done. Step 4: Intra-arterial nimodipine ± balloon angioplasty for proximal severe vasospasm. Supportive: Normothermia, euglycemia, Hgb ≥8–9 g/dL, avoid hypovolemia.
Angiographic vasospasm — imaging confirmation SEPARATE FROM DCI DEFINITION
Vasospasm on CTA, MRA, or DSA — vessel narrowing: <33% = mild; 34–66% = moderate; ≥67% = severe (correlates with ≥50% diameter reduction on DSA)
Angiographic vasospasm confirms large-vessel narrowing but does NOT equal DCI — they are defined separately. DSA is the gold standard and allows simultaneous treatment (angioplasty, IA nimodipine). CTA is used for initial imaging confirmation. CTP adds functional perfusion data.

DSA indications: MFV ≥200 cm/s, clinical DCI meeting criteria, or CTA/CTP showing severe vasospasm with perfusion deficit — especially when balloon angioplasty or IA nimodipine is being considered. DSA complication rate <1% in experienced centers.

Step 1 · Confirm Tremor and Assess Frequency
Tremor is defined as an involuntary, rhythmic, oscillatory movement of a body part. Confirm tremor is present and differentiate from non-tremor hyperkinesias before proceeding. Document frequency when possible.
A · Tremor Confirmation MANDATORY
Rhythmic, oscillatory, involuntary movement confirmed on examination
Must be rhythmic and oscillatory — distinguish from: myoclonus (arrhythmic, shock-like jerks), chorea (irregular, flowing, non-rhythmic), asterixis (lapses of sustained posture, not true tremor), clonus (requires sustained stretch), and dystonic movements (sustained, twisting contractions). Physiological tremor is present in all individuals but is generally not visible or symptomatic unless enhanced.
IPMDS 2017: Tremor is "an involuntary, rhythmic, oscillatory movement of a body part." Physiological tremor is not pathological — do not record as tremor disorder unless enhanced by fatigue, anxiety, drugs, or metabolic disturbance. Differentiating tremor from dystonic movements may require careful examination and sometimes neurophysiology.
B · Tremor Frequency DOCUMENT
Frequency guide (most accurately measured with accelerometry or polymyography):
<4 Hz — Myorhythmia, some palatal tremors, Holmes tremor
4–8 Hz — Most pathological tremors (PD rest tremor 4–7 Hz; ET postural/kinetic 4–8 Hz)
8–12 Hz — Central neurogenic component of physiological tremor; rhythmic cortical myoclonus typically >8 Hz
>13 Hz — Primary orthostatic tremor (13–18 Hz, must confirm with EMG)
Frequency category (select best estimate)
If tremor frequency is in the 13–18 Hz range, primary orthostatic tremor (POT) must be considered and confirmed with EMG. POT may be palpable but not visible in lower limb muscles during standing; the audible "helicopter sign" can be detected by auscultation. The 13–18 Hz high-coherence pattern across affected muscles is pathognomonic.
Bhatia 2017: "frequency is not very helpful in diagnosis because the frequency of most pathological tremors is 4 to 8 Hz." Exception: myorhythmia/palatal tremors <4 Hz; primary orthostatic tremor 13–18 Hz.
Duration of tremor symptoms
Duration is critical for ET classification. The IPMDS 2017 criteria require at least 3 years for an essential tremor diagnosis — to reduce the odds of subsequent development of other neurological signs (dystonia, parkinsonism, or ataxia). Tremors meeting ET criteria but lasting <3 years should be labeled indeterminate tremor pending further observation.
TITAN study (post-2017 criteria): only ~3% of ET/ET-plus patients required the indeterminate label for the 3-year criterion (15/680 in year 1), suggesting this is more theoretical than practical concern in typical practice.
Step 2 · Activation Conditions
Identify all activation conditions that apply — tremors can coexist across categories. This is the single most important axis for differential diagnosis.
A · Rest vs. Action KEY DISTINCTION
Rest Tremor — present in a fully supported, voluntarily relaxed body part
Assess with adequate support of the body part against gravity (hands resting on lap, head fully supported). In PD, rest tremor amplitude almost always diminishes or is abolished at least transiently during voluntary movement initiation, and increases during mental stress (counting backward, Stroop test). Rest tremor may also appear or worsen during walking or movement of another body part. Re-emergent tremor of PD (tremor reappearing after brief pause during postural hold) should be classified as an action tremor, not rest tremor, despite similar waveform to rest tremor.
Rest tremor that does NOT subside during voluntary movements is more consistent with ET plus or dystonic tremor than PD. This distinction has clinical value in separating PD rest tremor from other syndromes (Papengut et al., Parkinsonism Relat Disord 2013;19:693–697).
Rest tremor — does it subside/pause at movement initiation?
This feature separates parkinsonian rest tremor from the rest tremor seen in ET plus. In PD, suppression is expected at least transiently. In ET plus, rest tremor does not reliably subside with movement. In dystonic tremor, suppression may also be absent.
B · Action Tremor Types SELECT ALL THAT APPLY
Postural Tremor — maintaining position against gravity
Occurs while voluntarily maintaining a position against gravity (e.g., arms outstretched). Most common in ET and enhanced physiological tremor. Can be position-independent (present in any posture against gravity) or position-specific (only in a particular posture). Orthostatic tremor is a special form — tremor of lower limbs and trunk occurring only during standing.
Simple Kinetic Tremor — during voluntary movement (non-target-directed)
Tremor occurring during voluntary movement that is roughly the same amplitude throughout the movement (e.g., waving hands side-to-side at slow speed). This is the most characteristic tremor type in ET. Distinguish from intention tremor, which has a crescendo increase in amplitude as the limb approaches a target.
Intention Tremor — crescendo increase approaching a visual target (<5 Hz)
Tremor amplitude increases as the affected limb approaches its target (finger-nose-finger, heel-shin). Usually caused by a lesion in the cerebellothalamic pathway. Frequency typically <5 Hz. Can occur in ET (intention tremor is a specific but not sensitive finding for ET: specificity 85%, sensitivity only 45% in one cohort of 210 tremor patients — van der Stouwe et al., Parkinsonism Relat Disord 2016;30:23–28).
Intention tremor is rarely an isolated tremor syndrome. When focal or unilateral, evaluate for cerebellothalamic pathway pathology. Holmes tremor (rest + postural + intention, <5 Hz) implies brainstem pathology near the red nucleus.
Task-Specific Tremor — only during a specific motor task (e.g., writing)
Tremor occurring only during a specific repetitive task. Primary writing tremor is the most common form. Task-specific tremors of the hand or mouth also occur in musicians and sportspeople (yips in golfers). There is ongoing debate as to whether these are focal dystonia variants, overuse syndromes, or distinct isolated tremor syndromes. Must be distinguished from writing tremor in writer's cramp (dystonia) or young-onset PD.
Isolated task-specific tremors are an exclusion criterion for ET and ET plus per IPMDS 2017. If writing tremor only — classify separately, not as ET.
Isometric Tremor — during muscle contraction against a stationary object
Tremor occurring when making a fist, squeezing the examiner's fingers, or gripping a fixed object. Distinct from kinetic tremor. Can be present in ET, PD, and enhanced physiological tremor. Less commonly examined but part of the complete activation condition assessment.
Orthostatic Tremor — tremor during standing, lower limbs/trunk
Tremor in lower limbs or trunk occurring specifically during standing. Confirm with EMG — primary orthostatic tremor (POT) has a hallmark 13–18 Hz frequency with high coherence across affected muscles. The tremor may be palpable but not visible; the "helicopter sign" (audible hum auscultated over lower limb muscles or via EMG loudspeaker) may be present. Slower frequency tremor during standing (<13 Hz) = pseudo-orthostatic tremor — usually associated with other neurological signs.
POT diagnosis requires EMG confirmation per IPMDS 2017. POT in combination with PD, dementia, or spinocerebellar ataxia = "primary orthostatic tremor plus." 184-patient series: mean age of onset ~61, 63% female (Hassan et al., Neurology 2016;86:458–464).
Step 3 · Anatomical Distribution
Document all affected body regions. Distribution guides syndromic classification and helps distinguish ET (bilateral upper limb action tremor) from focal, hemibody, or generalized syndromes.
Bilateral Upper Limbs ET ANCHOR
Bilateral upper limb action tremor (postural and/or kinetic)
This is the defining criterion for essential tremor: an isolated tremor syndrome of bilateral upper limb action tremor, ≥3 years' duration, absence of other neurological signs. Onset in the upper limbs was discussed as a possible criterion but was rejected due to insufficient data. Side asymmetry is common and acceptable. Onset of tremor in upper limbs is the most common presentation of ET.
Other Distribution Regions DOCUMENT ALL
Head tremor (yes-yes, no-no, or variable directions)
Head tremor is common in the context of ET (does not exclude ET when co-present with arm tremor). Isolated head tremor (without arm tremor) is an exclusion for ET/ET plus. Isolated head tremor overlaps significantly with tremulous cervical dystonia — this remains clinically controversial. The geste antagoniste (sensory trick) suggests dystonia. A "null point" where dystonic posture relieves tremor is a dystonic feature.
Quantified sensory tricks significantly reduced tremor amplitude and frequency in 60 patients with head tremor, supporting dystonic mechanism in some cases (Masuhr et al., Mov Disord 2000;15:960–964).
Voice tremor — visible/audible oscillation of vocal apparatus
Isolated voice tremor (no dystonia in vocal apparatus, no tremor/dystonia elsewhere) is an isolated focal tremor syndrome — excluded from ET/ET plus. Voice tremor in ET occurs by definition in combination with hand tremor. Vocal cord hyperabduction/adduction, or dystonia elsewhere, indicates dystonic voice tremor. Voice tremor may also be seen in dystonia 6 and ANO3 mutation carriers.
Lower limb tremor (rest or action, unilateral or bilateral)
Lower limb rest tremor should prompt evaluation for parkinsonism (DaTscan if isolated). Lower limb involvement may also be present in ET (does not exclude ET). Orthostatic tremor specifically affects lower limbs during standing. Holmes tremor can involve proximal and distal lower limb muscles.
Jaw / chin tremor
Jaw tremor associated with dystonia elsewhere is classified as jaw tremor and dystonia syndrome. Jaw tremor can also occur in PD. Isolated jaw tremor (without dystonia) is a rare focal tremor syndrome. Jaw tremor alone does not fulfill criteria for PD parkinsonism per MDS criteria.
Palatal tremor — rhythmic movement of soft palate (0.5–5 Hz)
Essential palatal tremor: Ear click (tensor veli palatini), normal inferior olive MRI, no other neurological signs — isolated focal tremor syndrome, often idiopathic or functional.

Symptomatic palatal tremor: No ear click, involves levator veli palatini + other brainstem muscles (eye movements, face), typically combined with ataxia; olivary pseudohypertrophy on MRI; caused by dentato-olivary pathway lesions. Combined tremor syndrome.
Symptomatic palatal tremor has been described with Guillain-Mollaret triangle lesions, GFAP mutations (Alexander disease), POLG1 mutations, neuroferritinopathy, and the PAPT syndrome.
Unilateral or hemibody predominance
Hemitremor (one side of the body affected) strongly suggests PD — especially with accompanying bradykinesia and rigidity. Persistent bilateral symmetry throughout the disease course is a red flag against PD. Asymmetry is compatible with ET (and expected in PD). Unilateral intention tremor raises concern for focal cerebellothalamic pathway pathology.
Step 4 · Associated Neurological Signs
The presence or absence of associated signs determines whether this is an isolated or combined tremor syndrome, and drives syndromic classification.
A · Parkinsonism Signs COMBINED SYNDROME
Bradykinesia + rigidity (classic parkinsonian tremor pattern)
Classic parkinsonian tremor is a 4–7 Hz rest tremor (pill-rolling) of the hand, lower limb, jaw, tongue, or foot — combined with bradykinesia and rigidity. Typically asymmetric and unilateral in onset. Re-emergent postural tremor with same waveform may coexist. Rest tremor is uncommon in MSA, CBD, and PSP. Higher frequencies than 4–7 Hz have been described.
Rest tremor is uncommon in MSA (<10% of MSA-P), corticobasal degeneration, and PSP (Wenning et al., Lancet Neurol 2013;12:264–274; Armstrong et al., Neurology 2013;80:496–503). Presence of classic rest tremor with parkinsonism therefore favors PD within the parkinsonian syndromes.
B · Dystonia Signs COMBINED SYNDROME
Dystonia — in the same body part as tremor (dystonic tremor) or elsewhere
Dystonic tremor: Tremor in a body part affected by dystonia (e.g., tremulous cervical dystonia = dystonic head tremor).
Tremor associated with dystonia: Tremor and dystonia in different body parts.

Clues to dystonia: geste antagoniste (sensory trick), null point relieving tremor, overflow dystonia with movement, action dystonia at movement initiation. The distinction between dystonic tremor and ET with soft signs remains clinically challenging and is an area of active research.
Sensory tricks have been convincingly demonstrated for cranial and other dystonic tremors (Albanese & Lalli, Mov Disord 2009). The sensitivity/specificity of geste antagoniste and null point for distinguishing dystonic from essential head tremor have not been formally validated.
C · Cerebellar / Holmes Signs COMBINED SYNDROME
Ataxia, cerebellar oculomotor signs, or other cerebellar features
Intention tremor combined with cerebellar ataxia, dysmetria, or oculomotor signs (gaze-evoked nystagmus, saccadic pursuit) indicates a combined tremor syndrome. Holmes tremor (rest + postural + intention tremor, <5 Hz, from proximal and distal muscles) usually reflects acquired brainstem pathology near the red nucleus. Look for lesions in the Guillain-Mollaret triangle on MRI.
Holmes tremor: 29-patient series — delay from lesion to tremor onset was 0–24 months; lesion localization varied but red nucleus/dentato-rubro-thalamic tract was most common (Raina et al., Neurology 2016;86:931–938).
D · Soft Signs / ET Plus Features UNCERTAIN SIGNIFICANCE
Soft neurological signs — impaired tandem gait, questionable dystonic posturing, mild memory impairment
These signs have uncertain significance and do not suffice for an additional syndromic diagnosis. If present alongside ET criteria, classify as ET plus. ET with rest tremor (that does not subside with movement) should also be classified as ET plus. The interpretation of soft signs is subjective and left to clinical judgment.
ET plus does NOT include clearly defined syndromes like dystonic tremor or task-specific tremor. The ET plus category was created to standardize what was previously an inconsistently defined gray zone of "ET with soft signs." Prospective longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the natural history of ET plus.
E · Functional Tremor Features POSITIVE SIGNS
Distractibility, frequency entrainment, or antagonist muscle coactivation
Distractibility: Tremor diminishes or ceases when attention is directed away from the affected body part (counting backward, complex motor task). Sensitivity 92%, specificity 94% for functional tremor (van der Stouwe et al., 2016, n=210).

Entrainment: Tremor frequency shifts to match an externally cued voluntary rhythm performed by the other hand. Sensitivity 91%, specificity 91% (van der Stouwe et al., 2016).

Coactivation sign: Tonic antagonist muscle coactivation detectable at tremor onset on EMG.

An electrophysiological test battery (EMG + accelerometry, ≥3/10 positive tests) achieves sensitivity 89.5%, specificity 95.9% for functional tremor (Schwingenschuh et al., Mov Disord 2016;31:555–562).
Additional supporting features: sudden onset; spontaneous remissions; variable amplitude and frequency; inconsistent topographic distribution; other functional neurological signs. Longstanding functional tremor may become resistant to distraction — complex or unexpected tasks may be needed. A positive diagnosis based on these signs is preferred over diagnosis by exclusion alone.
Step 5 · Ancillary Testing Reference
This step contains protocol and interpretation guides. Entries for syndromic classification belong in Steps 1–4.
Key principles: Most tremor syndromes are clinical diagnoses. Ancillary testing confirms syndromic classification in specific situations. · EMG / polymyography → required for primary orthostatic tremor (13–18 Hz confirmation) and functional tremor test battery · DaTscan → consider for isolated rest tremor with normal exam — excludes dopaminergic deficiency · MRI brain → indicated for sudden-onset tremor, Holmes tremor, symptomatic palatal tremor
Dopamine Transporter (DAT) Imaging in Tremor: Indications (per IPMDS 2017 principles): · Isolated rest tremor without other parkinsonian signs (monosymptomatic tremor at rest) · Differentiate tremor-dominant PD from ET with rest component (ET plus) · Evaluate indeterminate tremor when clinical diagnosis uncertain after ≥3 years Interpretation: · Normal DaTscan → essentially excludes nigrostriatal degeneration (PD, MSA-P, PSP, CBD) – Possible diagnoses: ET plus with rest tremor, dystonic tremor, functional tremor, benign tremulous parkinsonism (SWEDD syndrome) · Abnormal (reduced striatal uptake) → supports dopaminergic degeneration – Does NOT differentiate PD from MSA-P, PSP, or CBD Key caveats: · DaTscan sensitivity ~85–90% for early PD; may be normal in early or benign tremulous PD · SWEDD (scans without evidence of dopaminergic deficit): long-term follow-up suggests many represent dystonic tremor, ET plus, or functional tremor · Does NOT qualify as a supportive criterion for MDS-PD established criteria (only olfactory loss and cardiac MIBG meet the required specificity threshold) Medications to withhold: · Cocaine and stimulants — 12h · Methylphenidate — 12h · Modafinil — 12h · Bupropion — varies (caution) · Most dopaminergic drugs do NOT significantly affect DaTscan binding
Electrophysiological Tests in Tremor (Table 1, Bhatia 2017): 1. Surface EMG (polymyography): · Documents tremor presence and confirms rhythmicity · Measures tremor frequency and EMG burst morphology · Identifies myoclonus (arrhythmic, short burst) and asterixis (silent periods) · Required for PRIMARY ORTHOSTATIC TREMOR diagnosis: – EMG of bilateral lower limbs during standing – 13–18 Hz coherent tremor across affected muscles = diagnostic 2. Accelerometry + weight loading: · Enhanced physiological tremor: frequency DECREASES with loading (specificity 95%, sensitivity 42%) · Functional tremor: amplitude INCREASES with 500g loading (specificity 92%, sensitivity 22%) · Note: amplitude increase >130% on 500g loading = highly suggestive of functional tremor · Mechanical-reflex vs. central neurogenic tremor: loading separates these (resonance shift) 3. Functional tremor battery (Schwingenschuh et al., Mov Disord 2016;31:555–562): · Test components: tonic coactivation at tremor onset, tapping task performance at 3 frequencies, frequency shift/suppression/entrainment with tapping, response to ballistic movements, coherence analysis, weight loading amplitude change · Score ≥3 of 10 positive tests = functional tremor · Sensitivity 89.5%, Specificity 95.9% (n=40 functional vs. 72 other tremors) 4. Clinical signs for functional tremor (van der Stouwe et al., Parkinsonism Relat Disord 2016;30:23–28, n=210): · Distractibility: Sens 92%, Spec 94% · Entrainment: Sens 91%, Spec 91% · Loading amplitude increase: Sens 22%, Spec 92% (high specificity, low sensitivity) · Intention tremor for ET: Sens 45%, Spec 85% · Frequency decrease on loading (EPT): Sens 42%, Spec 95% 5. Coherence analysis: · Primary orthostatic tremor: uniquely high coherence bilaterally at 13–18 Hz · Functional tremor: coherence between bilateral upper limb tremors (when bilateral)
MRI Indications and Findings: WHEN TO OBTAIN MRI: · Sudden-onset tremor (consider vascular, neoplasm, demyelination) · Holmes tremor (rest + postural + intention, low frequency) · Symptomatic palatal tremor (olivary pseudohypertrophy) · Focal/unilateral intention tremor (cerebellothalamic pathway lesion) · Tremor with cerebellar, brainstem, or pyramidal signs · Young-onset tremor (Wilson's disease, SCA, mitochondrial) KEY MRI FINDINGS: Holmes Tremor / Rubral Tremor: · Lesions near red nucleus, subthalamic nucleus, or dentatorubrothalamic tract · Thalamic involvement → often with dystonia and abnormal proprioception · Guillain-Mollaret triangle: dentate nucleus → red nucleus → inferior olive (interruption at any level can produce palatal or Holmes tremor) Symptomatic Palatal Tremor: · Olivary pseudohypertrophy on T2 (pathognomonic when present) · Look for focal lesions in ipsilateral or contralateral brainstem Atypical Parkinsonism (Parkinsonian tremor differential): · MSA-P/C: putaminal rim sign (T2 hypointensity + hyperintense rim), hot cross bun sign · PSP: midbrain atrophy (hummingbird sign on sagittal T1), SCP atrophy · CBS: asymmetric posterior frontoparietal cortical atrophy Wilson's Disease (young-onset tremor with wing-beating tremor): · Bilateral T2 hyperintensity putamen/thalamus/brainstem ("face of the giant panda" sign) · Always consider in tremor patients <50 — check serum ceruloplasmin + 24h urine copper
When to Consider Genetic Testing: ESSENTIAL TREMOR GENETICS: · ET is a heterogeneous syndrome — no single gene accounts for most cases · Family history positive in 50–70% of ET cases, suggesting polygenic or autosomal dominant inheritance with variable penetrance · FUS mutations: exome sequencing identified FUS as a rare cause of ET (Merner et al., Am J Hum Genet 2012;91:313–319), but subsequent studies showed low frequency in European cohorts (Ortega-Cubero et al., Neurobiol Aging 2013) · FXTAS (Fragile X-associated tremor/ataxia syndrome): FMR1 premutation (55–200 CGG repeats) — consider in men >50 with ET-like tremor + ataxia, MCP sign on MRI, family history of fragile X intellectual disability or premature ovarian insufficiency DYSTONIC TREMOR GENETICS: · ANO3 (anoctamin 3, DYT24): causes focal/segmental dystonia AND isolated bibrachial tremor — can mimic ET. Also associated with voice tremor. · TOR1A (DYT1): early-onset generalized dystonia · THAP1 (DYT6): mixed dystonia; THAP1 mutations can present with voice/laryngeal dystonia PARKINSONIAN TREMOR GENETICS: · LRRK2 G2019S: most common dominant PD mutation; ~1–2% of all PD · GBA variants: biallelic = Gaucher; heterozygous = 5× increased PD risk · SNCA multiplication, PRKN, PINK1, DJ-1 for early-onset PD WILSON'S DISEASE: · ATP7B gene sequencing — always test in tremor patients age <50 · Wing-beating tremor (Holmes-type) + Kayser-Fleischer rings is pathognomonic FRAGILE X-ASSOCIATED TREMOR/ATAXIA (FXTAS): · FMR1 premutation carrier status (55–200 CGG repeats) in >50yo males · Revised diagnostic criteria emphasize MCP sign on MRI and clinical features (Apartis et al., Neurology 2012;79:1898–1907)

Step 1 · Thunderclap Headache — Definition & Eligibility
Thunderclap headache (TCH) is a severe headache with sudden onset reaching maximum intensity within 1 minute. It is a neurological emergency until proven otherwise. The Ottawa SAH Rule (Perry JJ et al.) applies to a specific, well-defined population — using it outside these inclusion criteria may produce false-negative results. First, confirm the patient meets the inclusion criteria and has no exclusion criteria.
Ottawa SAH Rule — inclusion criteria (apply the rule ONLY if ALL of these are true):
1. Age ≥15 years
2. New, acute, non-traumatic headache that reached maximal intensity within 1 hour of onset
3. Alert (GCS 15) with a normal neurological examination at time of assessment

Ottawa SAH Rule — exclusion criteria (do NOT apply the rule if any of these are present):
Referred from another hospital (already partially worked up)
Known history of 3 or more recurrent headaches over 6 months (chronic pattern changes the prior probability)
History of aneurysm, SAH, or brain tumour
New focal neurological deficit (these patients require immediate CT regardless of rule)
Papilloedema on fundoscopy
Confirm headache onset characteristics REQUIRED FOR ACCURATE RISK STRATIFICATION
Onset to peak intensity within 1 minute — “the worst headache of my life” or “like a thunderclap” — explosive, hyperacute onset
Key distinction: TCH is defined by the rate of onset, not the severity alone. A headache that builds over 15–30 minutes does not meet the strict definition, even if very severe. The 1-minute criterion captures the sentinel bleed and other vascular emergencies. Many patients will describe it as “the worst headache of my life” — while this phrase is highly associated with SAH, it lacks specificity. The suddenness is the pathognomonic feature.

The Ottawa SAH Rule uses a slightly broader criterion: maximal intensity within 1 hour (not 1 minute) — this was deliberate to increase sensitivity at the cost of specificity. ICHD-3 defines primary thunderclap headache as reaching maximum within 1 minute.
Misdiagnosis of SAH is common: Up to 50% of SAH cases are misdiagnosed on first presentation, most commonly as migraine, tension headache, or viral illness. The misdiagnosis rate is highest when: (1) the headache was the first symptom without LOC; (2) the patient had prior migraines; (3) the CT was interpreted without radiologist review; (4) LP was not performed after negative CT. A missed SAH diagnosis carries a case fatality rate of ~70% for the subsequent bleed.
Ottawa exclusion criteria EXCLUSIONS INVALIDATE THE RULE
New focal neurological deficit present — hemiparesis, aphasia, diplopia, facial droop, ataxia, or any new localising sign
Any new focal neurological deficit bypasses clinical decision rules entirely — these patients require immediate CT head without contrast (and neurosurgical/neurology consultation). Focal deficits with thunderclap headache suggest: SAH with vasospasm or focal clot effect; arterial dissection (cervical or intracranial); CVT; hypertensive intracerebral haemorrhage; ischaemic stroke presenting atypically; RCVS with infarction. Do NOT apply the Ottawa rule.
Papilloedema on fundoscopy — indicates raised intracranial pressure; may indicate CVT, IIH, malignant hypertension, or mass lesion
History of 3+ recurrent headaches over the past 6 months — a chronic headache pattern changes pre-test probability significantly
This exclusion was included because patients with a well-established, repetitive headache pattern over months have a very different prior probability of SAH than those presenting with a genuinely novel event. However, exercise caution: a patient with known migraines who presents with “the worst headache of my life, different from my usual migraines” should not be automatically excluded — the description of a qualitatively new headache in a known migraineur still warrants evaluation for SAH.
Step 2 · Ottawa SAH Rule
The Ottawa SAH Rule (Perry JJ et al. BMJ 2011;343:d4277 derivation; Ann Emerg Med 2017;70:18 validation, n=1,153, 67 SAH) applies to alert adults (≥15 years) with non-traumatic headache reaching maximum intensity within 1 hour and normal neurological examination. Rule: if any one of the six criteria is positive, CT head is indicated. Sensitivity 100% (95% CI 94.6–100%), specificity 13.6%.
Performance (Perry et al. Ann Emerg Med 2017, n=1,153 patients, 67 with SAH):
Sensitivity 100% (95% CI 94.6–100%)  •  Specificity 13.6% (95% CI 13.1–15.8%)
NPV for ruling out SAH: ~100% when all six criteria are negative
External validation in four countries, n=3,317 total: sensitivity 100% across all studies, specificity 7.6–15.3%

Critical limitation: High sensitivity, very low specificity — rule is designed to rule OUT SAH (high NPV), not to rule it in. A positive rule mandates CT; it does not diagnose SAH.
Do not apply if: patient has new neurological deficit, known aneurysm/SAH/brain tumour, referred from another hospital, or ≥3 recurrent headaches over 6 months.

Step 3 · Non-Contrast CT Head — Timing & Interpretation
Non-contrast CT head (NCCTH) is the first-line investigation for suspected SAH. Sensitivity is critically time-dependent. Modern multidetector CT within 6 hours of headache onset approaches 100% sensitivity for SAH. After 6 hours, sensitivity declines as subarachnoid blood is reabsorbed and becomes isodense to CSF.
CT sensitivity for SAH by time from onset (Backes D et al. Stroke 2012; Perry JJ et al. BMJ 2011):
<6 hours: ~98–100% sensitivity (Perry 2011 prospective study: 121/121 SAH detected within 6h; sensitivity 100%, 95% CI 97–100%)
6–12 hours: ~93–98% sensitivity — LP recommended after negative CT
12–24 hours: ~85–93% sensitivity — LP strongly recommended
>24 hours: Sensitivity drops further as blood reabsorbs. LP or MRI (FLAIR/DWI) required.

NICE NG228 (2022): If CT performed within 6 hours and negative — SAH ruled out (no LP required). If CT negative and performed >6 hours after onset — LP recommended to assess for xanthochromia.
North American approach (many centres): LP after any negative CT in the context of clinical suspicion for SAH, regardless of timing, to maximise sensitivity and to detect other treatable causes (meningitis, CVT).
CT result and timing TIME FROM HEADACHE ONSET TO CT IS CRITICAL
Time from headache onset to CT head — determines whether LP is needed after a negative CT
The 6-hour threshold is based on the Perry 2011 prospective cohort study (n=3,132, including 240 with SAH): CT within 6 hours had 100% sensitivity (95% CI 97.0–100%). The original study used modern multidetector CT with thin slices. Important caveats: (1) CT must be reported by a neuroradiologist (or experienced radiologist) — initial reads by residents miss ~5% of SAH; (2) The sensitivity data applies to overt SAH with hyperdense subarachnoid blood — very small “sentinel bleeds” may still be missed; (3) Some centres use MRI (FLAIR sequence) as an alternative to LP after negative CT when LP is contraindicated or declined.
MRI as LP alternative: MRI FLAIR sequence can detect subarachnoid blood up to 2–4 weeks after SAH but has limited sensitivity in the first 24 hours. DWI/SWI sequences help detect haemorrhage. MRI is a reasonable LP alternative in patients with coagulopathy, anticoagulation, or thrombocytopenia, or when LP is refused, but is not validated as equivalent to LP in the acute setting.
CT quality and reporting — modern multidetector CT (MDCT) ≥16 slices, thin sections, reported by radiologist (not resident preliminary read alone)
The 100% sensitivity figures for CT within 6 hours are based on studies using modern multidetector scanners. Single-slice or older CT technology has lower sensitivity. Radiologist interpretation is critical — studies show resident preliminary reads miss approximately 4–8% of SAH. All negative CT scans for suspected SAH should be confirmed by a radiologist or neuroradiologist before discharge decision.

Key CT findings in SAH: Hyperdense blood in the basal cisterns (interpeduncular, suprasellar, ambient), sylvian fissure, or cortical sulci. Blood may also be in the ventricular system (IVH). The modified Fisher scale and WFNS scale are assigned at this stage.
Step 4 · After Negative CT — LP vs. CTA Decision
After a negative CT, the next step depends on timing and the clinical picture. The two main options are lumbar puncture (LP) for xanthochromia and RBC count, or CT angiography (CTA) to directly image cerebral vessels. These strategies are complementary, not mutually exclusive — CTA detects aneurysms but not all SAH; LP detects haemoglobin breakdown products but misses structural causes.
LP — what it detects and when:
Xanthochromia (yellow discolouration): Develops 2–4 hours after SAH onset; present in nearly all patients by 12 hours; persists up to 2–4 weeks. Visual inspection is most practical; spectrophotometry more sensitive but rarely available. Best obtained ≥6–12 hours after onset for maximum sensitivity.
RBC count (tubes 1 and 4): Persistent high RBC count (>2,000/mm³) that does not decrease between tube 1 and tube 4 suggests true SAH vs. traumatic tap (traumatic tap shows decreasing RBC count). No universally agreed threshold.
Opening pressure: Elevated → consider CVT, IIH, meningitis. Low → consider spinal dural leak (spontaneous intracranial hypotension).

CTA — what it detects:
Cerebral aneurysms: Sensitivity ~97–99% for aneurysms ≥3mm; lower for very small (<3mm) aneurysms. Does not detect SAH directly — blood must still be visible on CT or LP.
Other vascular pathology: Arterial dissection (carotid/vertebral/intracranial), AVM, dural fistula, RCVS vasospasm pattern, CVT (with CTV component).
Normal CTA does NOT exclude SAH. Up to 15–20% of aneurysmal SAH patients have a negative initial CTA (small or thrombosed aneurysms). DSA (catheter angiography) remains gold standard.
Current investigation plan after negative CT SELECT THE STRATEGY FOR THIS PATIENT
LP timing — is ≥6 hours elapsed since headache onset? (Best sensitivity for xanthochromia; NICE NG228 recommends ≥12 hours in some guidelines)
Why wait for LP? After SAH, red blood cells in the CSF release haemoglobin which is enzymatically converted to oxyhemoglobin (2–4 hours) and then bilirubin (by 12 hours). Bilirubin (xanthochromia) is the most reliable marker of true SAH. If LP is done too early (<2h), xanthochromia may be absent even in true SAH, reducing sensitivity. NICE NG228 recommends LP performed at least 12 hours after onset; some North American guidelines suggest ≥6 hours is adequate.

Controversy — do we still need LP after negative CT <6h? There is ongoing debate about whether a negative modern CT within 6 hours is sufficient to exclude SAH without LP. The Perry 2011 study suggests it may be. However, LP adds value by: detecting other causes (meningitis), capturing rare sentinel bleeds too small for CT, and measuring opening pressure for CVT/SIH. Most neurologists and neurological societies still recommend LP after any negative CT in high-suspicion cases.
Traumatic tap: Occurs in up to 20% of LPs. Distinguishing traumatic tap from true SAH: (1) Decreasing RBC count tube 1 to 4 favours traumatic tap; (2) Xanthochromia on visual inspection, if present, favours SAH (but requires 6–12h to develop); (3) Spectrophotometric bilirubin is more definitive. If the tap is traumatic and xanthochromia is absent, a second LP or CTA should be considered.
LP result (if performed) XANTHOCHROMIA IS THE KEY FINDING
CSF findings — xanthochromia (yellow discolouration), RBC count (tubes 1 and 4), opening pressure, protein, glucose
Interpret all CSF parameters together:
• Xanthochromia (visual or spectrophotometric): Presence confirms haemoglobin breakdown products → SAH or other CNS haemorrhage. Absent xanthochromia in a well-timed LP (≥12h) essentially excludes SAH.
• RBC count tube 1 vs. tube 4: Stable or increasing count (tube 4 ≥ tube 1) suggests true subarachnoid blood; decreasing count suggests traumatic tap.
• Opening pressure: Elevated (>20 cmH2O) → CVT, IIH, meningitis, SAH with IVH; Low (<7 cmH2O) → spontaneous intracranial hypotension (spinal CSF leak).
• WBC, protein, glucose: Elevated WBC ± low glucose → meningitis/encephalitis; elevated protein in SAH (blood breakdown products).
CTA / vascular imaging FOR ANEURYSM DETECTION AND RCVS / DISSECTION
CTA head (and neck for dissection) — performed or planned? Used when SAH is confirmed, LP is positive, or when RCVS/dissection/CVT is suspected
When to perform CTA:
• CT positive for SAH → CTA to identify aneurysm (and plan coiling vs. clipping)
• LP positive for xanthochromia → CTA to identify aneurysm
• Clinical suspicion for RCVS (recurrent TCH, postpartum, drug exposure, bath/exertion triggers) → CTA head and CTV (CT venography) to assess for vasoconstriction and exclude CVT
• Suspected dissection (neck pain, Horner’s, Wallenberg pattern, recent trauma) → CTA neck
• Suspected CVT (positional headache, papilloedema, recent pregnancy, prothrombotic state) → CTV

CTA limitations: Sensitivity ~97% for aneurysms ≥3mm (decreases to ~85% for <3mm). Normal CTA does not exclude SAH. DSA (catheter angiography) is gold standard for aneurysm detection and is used when CTA is negative but SAH is confirmed, or when intervention is planned.
RCVS on CTA: May show diffuse multifocal “beading” of intracranial arteries (alternating narrowing and dilatation). However, CTA/MRA may be completely normal in early RCVS (first 1–2 weeks). Serial CTA at 2–3 weeks may be needed if RCVS is clinically suspected. The vasoconstriction reverses within 3 months by definition.
Step 5 · Secondary Causes Checklist
Thunderclap headache has a broad differential. SAH accounts for ~10% of ED thunderclap headache presentations; the remainder have other secondary causes (~25%) or are benign primary headaches (~65%). Each secondary cause requires specific investigation and management. Work through this checklist systematically — not all causes require the same workup.
Epidemiology of thunderclap headache at ED presentation:
SAH: ~10% of true thunderclap headache presentations in ED; highest priority to exclude
RCVS: Most common cause of thunderclap headache after SAH; often has recurrent episodes, triggered (exertion, Valsalva, hot bath, orgasm, postpartum); >90% benign course
CVT: More gradual or positional headache in some; prothrombotic risk factors; LP may show elevated opening pressure; CTV confirms
Benign primary: Primary thunderclap headache, primary exertional headache, primary sexual (orgasmic) headache, primary cough headache — diagnosed only after secondary causes excluded
Other dangerous: Pituitary apoplexy, hypertensive emergency/PRES, vertebral/carotid dissection, cerebral abscess, meningitis/encephalitis, spontaneous intracranial hypotension (low-pressure headache from spinal CSF leak)
Evaluate each category ALL MUST BE CONSIDERED BEFORE DIAGNOSING PRIMARY TCH

Step 1 · Clinical Presentation Criteria
Both age and at least one qualifying clinical feature must be present before applying imaging criteria.
A · Age MANDATORY
Age ≥50 years
The Boston criteria v2.0 apply only to patients aged 50 or older. This threshold reflects the age-related increase in sporadic CAA prevalence. CAA in younger patients is atypical and should prompt consideration of hereditary CAA (Dutch, Flemish, Arctic, Iowa, Italian, Piedmont mutations) or other amyloidopathies.
B · Clinical Presentation ≥1 REQUIRED

Mark all that are present. At least one must apply.

Spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage
Spontaneous (non-traumatic) ICH, typically lobar (cortical or subcortical). Lobar distribution strongly favors CAA; deep (basal ganglia, thalamus, brainstem) or cerebellar hemorrhage alone is not supportive. The hemorrhage must not be attributable to another structural or systemic cause.
Convexity subarachnoid hemorrhage (cSAH)
Non-aneurysmal subarachnoid blood restricted to cortical sulci of the convexities, sparing the basal cisterns. On MRI: FLAIR or SWI sulcal signal. Must be distinguished from traumatic or aneurysmal SAH. Often presents as transient focal neurological episodes (TFNEs) in CAA.
Transient focal neurological episodes (TFNEs)
Recurrent, stereotyped, brief (<30 min) episodes of positive (spreading) or negative focal neurological symptoms — classically a spreading paresthesia, tingling, or visual phenomenon — often referred to as "cortical spreading depression" events. Associated with cortical superficial siderosis in CAA. Distinguish from TIA (typically negative, abrupt onset) and seizure.
TFNEs in the setting of cortical superficial siderosis are highly specific for CAA. Each episode typically spreads over minutes, unlike the abrupt onset of TIA.
Cognitive impairment or dementia
Cognitive decline attributable to CAA-related mechanisms — white matter disease, microinfarcts, microbleeds, superficial siderosis, or macroscopic hemorrhage. CAA-related dementia may be disproportionately posterior (visuospatial, executive). This presentation is used to capture the non-hemorrhagic cognitive CAA phenotype.
When cognitive impairment is the sole presentation, the white matter features (Step 4) become especially important for diagnosis. A positive CSF or PET amyloid biomarker can support diagnosis but is not currently incorporated into the v2.0 criteria framework.
Step 2 · Exclusion Criteria
Both conditions must be satisfied for the Boston criteria to apply. These are non-negotiable structural prerequisites.
Deep hemorrhagic lesions MUST BE ABSENT
Absence of deep hemorrhagic lesions on T2*-GRE or SWI
Deep hemorrhagic lesions include: deep ICH (basal ganglia, thalamus, internal capsule, brainstem), deep cerebral microbleeds (not lobar or cortical), or deep superficial siderosis. Any single deep hemorrhagic lesion excludes CAA under the Boston criteria framework — it points toward hypertensive small vessel disease.
Mixed microbleeds (both lobar AND deep) also exclude CAA per the criteria, as the presence of any deep hemorrhagic lesion applies the exclusion. Cerebellar hemorrhagic lesions are a special case — they are classified as neither lobar nor deep and do NOT trigger this exclusion. If only cerebellar lesions are present, the exclusion is not applied.
Alternative cause exclusion REQUIRED
No other cause of hemorrhagic lesions identified on clinical history, imaging, and workup
Hemorrhagic lesions must not be better explained by an alternative cause. Causes to exclude based on clinical context:
Anticoagulation-related hemorrhage, arteriovenous malformation (AVM), cavernoma, dural arteriovenous fistula, CNS vasculitis, moyamoya disease, cerebral venous thrombosis, coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia, infective endocarditis, or metastatic disease. Workup should include: blood pressure history (longstanding hypertension favors hypertensive SVD), coagulation studies, vessel imaging (CTA/MRA), and gadolinium-enhanced MRI to exclude structural lesions. Not all workup is mandatory in every case — clinical judgment guides the extent of investigation.
Step 3 · Hemorrhagic Markers
Strictly lobar or superficial hemorrhagic lesions only. Assessed on T2*-GRE or SWI — SWI is preferred for superior sensitivity.
Diagnostic logic: Each marker below counts as one hemorrhagic lesion. Probable CAA requires ≥2 strictly lobar/superficial hemorrhagic lesions (alone), OR 1 hemorrhagic lesion + 1 white matter feature (Step 4). Possible CAA requires 1 hemorrhagic lesion alone, OR 1 white matter feature alone (Step 4). Mark all that are present on current or prior imaging.
Lobar intracerebral hemorrhage (cortical-subcortical)
Hemorrhage centered in the lobar (cortical or immediately subcortical) region — frontal, temporal, parietal, or occipital. Must be strictly lobar — not extending into or originating from deep structures (basal ganglia, thalamus, internal capsule). Prior lobar ICH documented on any imaging modality counts. Hemosiderin cavity on SWI from a prior lobar ICH qualifies as a lobar hemorrhagic lesion.
Lobar cerebral microbleeds — cortical or juxtacortical (≥1)
Small (2–10 mm) rounded hypointense foci on T2*-GRE or SWI in the cortex or immediately adjacent subcortical white matter. Must be strictly lobar — not in basal ganglia, thalamus, internal capsule, brainstem, or cerebellum. Even a single strictly lobar microbleed qualifies as one hemorrhagic lesion. SWI significantly outperforms T2*-GRE for microbleed detection.
Strictly lobar microbleed burden correlates with CAA severity and ICH recurrence risk. Mixed microbleed patterns (lobar + deep) trigger the exclusion criterion and should be assessed in Step 2.
Cortical superficial siderosis (cSS)
Linear hypointensity along the cortical surface on T2*-GRE or SWI, following sulcal contours — represents hemosiderin deposition from prior cortical or leptomeningeal hemorrhage. Distinguished from deep superficial siderosis (infratentorial, spinal) and from remote cortical contusion (traumatic). SWI has superior sensitivity. Focal cSS (1–3 sulci) vs. disseminated cSS (≥4 sulci) — both qualify as one hemorrhagic lesion; disseminated cSS has higher specificity.
Cortical superficial siderosis is closely associated with TFNEs in CAA and may represent the MRI correlate of cortical micro-hemorrhages or leptomeningeal vessel rupture. It was first incorporated in Boston criteria v1.5 (2010).
Convexity subarachnoid hemorrhage (cSAH) on MRI
Acute blood in cortical sulci restricted to the brain convexities — seen as sulcal FLAIR hyperintensity or SWI hypointensity. If the clinical presentation already includes cSAH (Step 1), this MRI finding confirms it as a hemorrhagic lesion. Subacute or chronic convexity SAH may be visible as sulcal hemosiderin on SWI. Distinguishing from aneurysmal SAH (basal cisterns, thick hemorrhage) is critical.

Step 4 · White Matter Features
New in v2.0. Each feature counts as one white matter marker. Assessed on T2-weighted or FLAIR sequences.
Key v2.0 addition: White matter features were incorporated to improve sensitivity, particularly in patients without macroscopic hemorrhage. A single white matter feature can substitute for a hemorrhagic lesion to reach Probable CAA (1 hemorrhagic lesion + 1 WM feature), or alone can establish Possible CAA. These features reflect CAA-related microangiopathy of penetrating cortical arterioles.
Severe perivascular spaces in the centrum semiovale — >20 visible in one hemisphere on T2
Perivascular spaces (PVS) in the centrum semiovale follow penetrating cortical arterioles into the white matter. In CAA, they are dilated by CAA-related angiopathy and reflect subcortical amyloid deposition. Assessed on axial T2-weighted MRI using the validated CS-PVS rating scale — severe = >20 visible PVS in one hemisphere on the slice with the largest number.
Centro-semiovale PVS pattern (upper white matter, following cortical vessels) is distinct from basal ganglia PVS (which follow lenticulostriate vessels, more associated with hypertensive SVD). Severe CS-PVS has specificity ~85% and sensitivity ~40% for CAA. Must be assessed on high-quality T2 sequences — not T2*-GRE or SWI.
White matter hyperintensities in a multispot pattern — >10 small juxtacortical/subcortical round or ovoid lesions on FLAIR
Multispot WMH pattern: small (<10 mm), round or ovoid, juxtacortical or subcortical FLAIR hyperintensities — more than 10 throughout the brain. This pattern reflects CAA-related white matter injury from cortical microinfarcts and amyloid-laden small vessel occlusion. Distinct from the confluent periventricular WMH typical of hypertensive SVD or age-related changes.
The multispot pattern is spatially associated with cortical microinfarcts and is pathologically correlated with amyloid angiopathy of superficial cortical vessels. It requires careful differentiation from MS lesions (juxtacortical, ovoid, periventricular) — though in the appropriate clinical context (age ≥50, no MS history), this distinction is usually straightforward. The juxtacortical/subcortical location is key — not deep periventricular.

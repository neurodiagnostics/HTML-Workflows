Step 1 · Clinical Features & Presentation
REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD) is a REM-stage parasomnia characterised by loss of normal skeletal muscle atonia during REM sleep, resulting in dream enactment behaviours (DEB). It is the only parasomnia for which polysomnography (PSG) is mandatory for diagnosis. Isolated (idiopathic) RBD is a prodromal synucleinopathy — the strongest single predictor of future Parkinson’s disease, DLB, or MSA.
Epidemiology: Prevalence ~0.38–0.5% general population. Isolated RBD (iRBD) predominantly affects males (>80%) aged >50 years. Secondary RBD (associated with overt neurodegeneration or medications) has equal sex distribution and can occur at any age. RBD is present in ~30% of narcolepsy patients (any age, equal sex distribution).

Core clinical triad: (1) Dream enactment behaviours (vocalisation, limb movements, sitting up, falling out of bed); (2) unpleasant/action-filled dream content (being chased, attacked, fighting); (3) absence of confusion on awakening — distinguishing from NREM parasomnias.
Cardinal clinical features — select all present ALL SHOULD BE ASSESSED
Dream enactment behaviour — vocalisation (talking, shouting, laughing, swearing), limb movements (punching, kicking, flailing), sitting up, falling out of bed
Dream enactment in RBD typically occurs in the second half of the night (when REM sleep is most concentrated). Behaviours mirror dream content — the patient often reports dreaming of being attacked, chased, or defending themselves. Witness history is critical: the bed partner can often provide accurate descriptions that the patient cannot (patients may be unaware of mild symptoms). Episodes can be violent enough to cause self-injury (ecchymoses, lacerations, fractures reported in up to 55% of untreated patients) or injury to the bed partner.

RBD1Q screening question (Postuma R et al.): “Have you ever been told, or suspected yourself, that you seem to ‘act out your dreams’ while asleep (for example, punching, flailing your arms in the air, making running movements, etc.)?” — Sensitivity ~94%, specificity ~87% in validation studies.
Forensic implications: Because violent behaviours during RBD occur during sleep and are involuntary, they may carry forensic and medicolegal significance. Video-PSG documentation is critical in legal contexts. Courts in multiple countries have accepted confirmed RBD as a defence in assault cases involving sleep-related violence.
Unpleasant, action-filled, vivid dream content — being chased, attacked, fighting, defending; dream content corresponds to enacted behaviours
Dream content in RBD is characteristically unpleasant and action-oriented — in stark contrast to the often benign or bizarre content of normal REM dreams. Patients frequently report dreams of being attacked by strangers, animals, or threatening figures. The dream narrative directly corresponds to the observed motor behaviour (e.g., a patient dreaming of being punched will make punching motions). However, dream recall is not required for diagnosis — some patients (especially those with cognitive impairment) have no dream recall. Some may recall pleasant dreams but still have violent behaviours.
Clear consciousness on awakening — patient is immediately alert, oriented, not confused; can recall the episode or associated dream
Prompt orientation on awakening is a hallmark of RBD and distinguishes it from NREM parasomnias (sleepwalking, night terrors, confusional arousals) where amnesia and postictal confusion are typical. In RBD, abrupt awakening during an episode yields a fully conscious, oriented individual who may be distressed by what they were dreaming. This distinction is clinically important: waking an NREM parasomnia patient may prolong confusion, while waking an RBD patient typically terminates the episode safely.
Exception — RBD with dementia: In patients with DLB or advanced Parkinson’s dementia, cognitive impairment may blunt post-arousal orientation. In this context, the witness history and PSG findings become even more important for diagnosis.
Sleep-related injury — self-injury (bruising, lacerations, fractures) or bed partner injury from enacted behaviours during sleep
Injury has been reported in up to 55% of RBD patients prior to diagnosis and treatment. Injuries include bruising (most common), lacerations, fractures, subdural haematoma, and dental injuries. Bed partner injury is also common and may drive the patient to seek evaluation. The presence of injury significantly impacts treatment urgency — pharmacological intervention (melatonin or clonazepam) should be considered promptly once diagnosis is confirmed in patients with prior injury.
Timing — episodes occur predominantly in the second half of the night (when REM sleep is most abundant); not at sleep onset
Normal REM sleep is concentrated in the last third of the night. RBD episodes therefore peak in the early morning hours (3–7 AM), which distinguishes them from NREM parasomnias (sleep terrors, sleepwalking), which occur in the first third of the night during deep NREM sleep. Episodes at sleep onset suggest hypnic jerks (hypnagogic myoclonus) or narcolepsy with cataplexy rather than RBD. However, patients may not remember timing, and some episodes can occur earlier in the night — timing should be corroborated by bed partner or video.
Prodromal features of synucleinopathy — hyposmia, constipation, orthostatic hypotension, colour vision impairment, mild cognitive changes, depression, anxiety
In isolated RBD, the presence of prodromal features of PD/DLB/MSA increases phenoconversion risk and should be systematically assessed at diagnosis and at annual follow-up visits. Hyposmia is present in ~50–70% of iRBD patients and is one of the earliest and most persistent markers. Constipation and autonomic dysfunction reflect early enteric and autonomic nervous system alpha-synuclein deposition. Colour vision impairment can be tested with standard colour vision plates. Orthostatic hypotension may indicate MSA spectrum. Mild cognitive impairment (especially visuospatial deficits) suggests DLB trajectory. Depression and anxiety are common in prodromal PD and may precede motor symptoms by years.
MDS Research Criteria for Prodromal PD (Berg D et al. 2015): The Parkinson’s Foundation and MDS have established probability calculators for prodromal PD combining iRBD with additional prodromal markers. Referral to a movement disorder specialist or enrolment in an iRBD cohort study (e.g., PPMI, NAPS) is strongly recommended in confirmed iRBD.
Step 2 · ICSD-3-TR Diagnostic Criteria (AASM 2023)
The International Classification of Sleep Disorders, Third Edition, Text Revision (ICSD-3-TR, AASM 2023) requires ALL four criteria to be met for a diagnosis of RBD. The criteria cannot diagnose RBD on clinical grounds alone — PSG documentation of REM sleep without atonia (RSWA) is mandatory unless the patient has an established synucleinopathy diagnosis.
ICSD-3-TR RBD Diagnostic Criteria (all four required):
Criterion A: Repeated episodes of sleep-related vocalisation and/or complex motor behaviours
Criterion B: Behaviours documented by PSG to occur during REM sleep, OR based on clinical history of dream enactment are presumed to occur during REM sleep AND one of: (1) RSWA on PSG, or (2) established synucleinopathy diagnosis
Criterion C: PSG demonstrates REM sleep without atonia (RSWA)
Criterion D: The disturbance is not better explained by another sleep disorder, mental disorder, medical or neurological condition, medication, or substance
ICSD-3-TR Criterion A REQUIRED
Criterion A: Repeated episodes of sleep-related vocalisation AND/OR complex motor behaviours (at least 2 or more episodes are implied; a single witnessed episode may warrant PSG referral)
“Repeated” implies the behaviours are not an isolated single event. In practice, even a single dramatic episode warrants evaluation given the diagnostic and prognostic implications. Vocalisation includes talking during sleep, shouting, laughing, swearing, or screaming. Complex motor behaviours include punching, kicking, grabbing, sitting up, jumping out of bed, falling, or any purposeful motor activity. Both must occur during (or presumed to occur during) REM sleep.
ICSD-3-TR Criterion B REQUIRED
Criterion B: Behaviours documented by PSG during REM sleep, OR clinically presumed to occur during REM sleep (via dream enactment history) AND [RSWA on PSG OR established synucleinopathy]
Criterion B has two pathways. Pathway 1 (ideal): Video-PSG directly captures behaviours occurring during electrographically verified REM sleep. Pathway 2 (clinical + PSG corroboration): Clinical history strongly suggests dream enactment (matching behaviours to dream content, second-half-of-night timing, immediate post-arousal awareness) AND either (a) RSWA documented on PSG, OR (b) the patient carries an established synucleinopathy diagnosis (PD, DLB, MSA) — in which case PSG with RSWA may not be separately required per ICSD-3-TR, though PSG remains diagnostically valuable to exclude OSA pseudo-RBD.

ICSD-3-TR 2023 update: The ICSD-3-TR clarifies that in patients with established synucleinopathy and typical clinical history, the diagnosis of RBD can be supported without a separate PSG capturing behaviours, provided RSWA is present on any PSG — however, PSG is still strongly recommended to exclude treatable mimics.
ICSD-3-TR Criterion C REQUIRED — RSWA ON PSG
Criterion C: PSG demonstrates REM sleep without atonia (RSWA) — excessive phasic and/or tonic EMG activity during REM sleep on chin or limb leads
RSWA is the neurophysiological hallmark of RBD and is required by ICSD-3-TR (with the exception noted in Criterion B pathway 3). RSWA is defined as: increased chin EMG tone in REM sleep (tonic RSWA: >50% of a 30-second epoch with chin EMG activity > minimum amplitude in NREM) and/or increased phasic chin or limb EMG bursts during REM (phasic RSWA: miniepochs [3-sec] with EMG bursts > 4× background).

AASM scoring criteria (Iber C et al.): Tonic chin EMG criterion: >50% of a 30-second REM epoch has chin EMG amplitude above the minimum amplitude seen in any stage. Phasic EMG criterion: ≥3 transient muscle activity bursts per epoch of at least 0.1–5 seconds duration. RSWA >27% of REM epochs (tonic) or RSWA phasic >15% strongly supports RBD (Frauscher B et al. Sleep 2012).

Important: RSWA quantification should be reported as a percentage of REM epochs meeting criteria, to better distinguish RBD from subclinical RSWA in at-risk populations (Ferri R et al. Sleep 2023).
Subclinical RSWA: RSWA can be present without clinical dream enactment behaviours in patients with synucleinopathies and some healthy older adults. Subclinical RSWA alone does not meet ICSD-3-TR criteria for RBD diagnosis but may be a prodromal biomarker requiring longitudinal surveillance.
ICSD-3-TR Criterion D REQUIRED — EXCLUSION
Criterion D: Disturbance not better explained by another sleep disorder, mental disorder, medical/neurological condition, medication, or substance
Key exclusions to consider (see also Step 4 — Mimics & Causes):
• OSA pseudo-RBD: Severe OSA can cause arousals from REM sleep mimicking RBD (with preserved REM atonia on non-apnoeic REM epochs). PSG AHI >15 with behaviours only during apnoeic events = OSA pseudo-RBD. Treat OSA first and reassess.
• NREM parasomnia: First-half of night timing, amnesia, no RSWA on PSG.
• Nocturnal seizures: EEG-confirmed epileptiform activity; stereotyped events; FLEP scale.
• Antidepressant-induced RBD: SSRIs, SNRIs, TCAs can induce RSWA and RBD-like behaviours. Consider medication withdrawal/switch if feasible before labelling isolated RBD.
• Narcolepsy-associated RBD: Secondary RBD; CSF hypocretin <110 pg/mL; cataplexy; MSLT SOREMP.
Step 3 · Polysomnography & RSWA Assessment
PSG is the only test required for RBD diagnosis. Video-PSG (vPSG) is the gold standard — simultaneous video recording allows correlation of observed behaviours with sleep stage. A single PSG may miss RSWA if no REM sleep is captured (REM suppression by medications, first-night effect, insufficient recording time). Repeat PSG may be required if clinical suspicion is high.
Technical requirements for RBD-focused PSG (AASM Manual):
Full PSG montage: EEG (minimum C3/C4, O1/O2, F3/F4 + M1/M2 references), EOG, submental chin EMG (mandatory), bilateral anterior tibialis EMG, ECG, airflow, respiratory effort, SpO2
Video recording: Synchronised infrared video recording is essential for capturing and correlating behaviours with PSG stage — this is what makes it video-PSG (vPSG)
Extended limb EMG: Bilateral flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS) EMG is recommended by some experts for improved RSWA detection in the arms (RBD behaviours often involve upper extremities)
RSWA scoring: Report the percentage of REM epochs meeting tonic and phasic RSWA criteria separately on chin and limb leads. RSWA >27% tonic or phasic >15% strongly supports RBD (Frauscher B et al. Sleep 2012, n=133 RBD + 136 controls)
PSG performance and findings REQUIRED FOR DIAGNOSIS
PSG performed? Was adequate REM sleep recorded (≥15 minutes of REM sleep for reliable RSWA assessment)?
REM sleep latency: If REM latency is very long (e.g., first REM period at 200+ minutes) or REM sleep is very brief (<15 minutes total), RSWA scoring will be unreliable. REM-suppressing medications (SSRIs, SNRIs, TCAs, clonazepam itself, alcohol) should ideally be tapered before PSG if clinically feasible and safe. First-night effect: The laboratory environment may alter sleep architecture and reduce REM sleep, especially in anxious patients. If clinical suspicion remains high after a normal study, repeat PSG is warranted.

Home sleep testing (HSAT) is NOT appropriate for RBD diagnosis — portable monitoring devices do not include video, full EEG staging, or adequate EMG channels for RSWA scoring. In-laboratory vPSG is mandatory.
Why PSG is mandatory (and cannot be replaced): OSA pseudo-RBD presents identically to true RBD clinically. Without PSG: (1) OSA cannot be excluded, (2) REM atonia cannot be assessed, (3) sleep stage cannot be confirmed. A clinical diagnosis of “probable RBD” without PSG will misclassify a substantial fraction of patients (estimated 20–40% false positives when PSG is not performed).
RSWA quantification MUST BE REPORTED IN PSG
RSWA severity — percentage of REM epochs with tonic or phasic EMG activity above threshold on chin or limb leads
RSWA classification (Frauscher B et al. Sleep 2012, n=269):
• Tonic RSWA (chin): >50% of a 30-second epoch with chin EMG amplitude >minimum. Cut-off >27.3% of REM epochs: sensitivity 81%, specificity 90% for RBD.
• Phasic RSWA (chin or limb): Bursts 0.1–5s duration, ≥3/epoch. Cut-off >15.2% of REM epochs: sensitivity 73%, specificity 91%.
• Combination tonic+phasic: Highest diagnostic accuracy when both assessed. Some labs use the SINBAR criteria (Ferri R et al.) with separate arm and leg EMG electrodes for more precise quantification.

Subclinical RSWA: RSWA may be present in patients with synucleinopathies who do not yet have clinical dream enactment behaviours. These patients should be monitored annually, as RBD behaviours may emerge over time.
Behaviours captured on PSG video IDEAL — CONFIRMS REM TIMING OF EVENTS
Video recording during PSG — were dream enactment behaviours captured on video and correlated with PSG-confirmed REM sleep?
Step 4 · RBD Mimics, Secondary Causes & Classification
Multiple conditions can produce nocturnal behaviours clinically indistinguishable from RBD. PSG is mandatory to exclude the most common mimic (OSA pseudo-RBD). Once RBD is confirmed, it must be classified as isolated (iRBD) or secondary (associated with a known cause), as this distinction has profound prognostic and management implications.
RBD mimic hierarchy (PSG required to differentiate all):
OSA pseudo-RBD (most common mimic): Severe OSA causes REM-fragmentation arousals with complex behaviours. PSG shows normal REM atonia on non-apnoeic epochs; behaviours coincide with apnoeic arousals. AHI often >40/hr. CPAP eliminates behaviours completely. Reported in ~10–20% of patients clinically suspected to have RBD.
NREM parasomnias (disorders of arousal): Confusional arousal, sleepwalking, sleep terrors. First-half of night; amnesia; PSG shows behaviours during N3 NREM, normal REM atonia.
Nocturnal seizures (sleep-related hypermotor epilepsy): Stereotyped, shorter episodes; may occur at any time of night; frontal lobe epilepsy EEG pattern; FLEP scale helpful. EMG on PSG may not show RSWA.
Antidepressant-induced RSWA/RBD: SSRIs (especially paroxetine, fluoxetine), SNRIs, TCAs can induce RSWA in up to 15% of users and frank RBD symptoms. Clue: temporal onset with medication start. Consider medication switch to mirtazapine, bupropion, or agomelatine (less RSWA association).
PLMD pseudo-RBD: Vigorous periodic limb movements can occasionally cause arousals with complex behaviours. PSG shows PLMS in NREM and REM, preserved REM atonia in non-PLM epochs.
Most likely mimic excluded? OSA MUST BE EXCLUDED BY PSG FIRST
Obstructive sleep apnea pseudo-RBD excluded — PSG AHI, REM atonia on non-apnoeic epochs, correlation of behaviours with apnoeic events vs. REM stage
In OSA pseudo-RBD: (1) AHI is typically >30–40 events/hour; (2) behaviours occur at the termination of apnoeic events (arousals from REM), not during sustained REM sleep; (3) REM atonia is preserved on non-apnoeic REM epochs; (4) CPAP therapy eliminates behaviours completely. If both OSA and true RBD coexist (which can happen), CPAP treatment must be trialled first; if behaviours persist after adequate OSA treatment, repeat PSG for RSWA evaluation.
OSA and RBD co-occurrence: True RBD and OSA can genuinely coexist, as both are common in older males. The key question is whether behaviours persist on adequate PAP therapy. Residual behaviours on PAP + RSWA on PSG (in non-apnoeic REM epochs) = true comorbid RBD requiring treatment.
Seizure excluded EEG REQUIRED IF CLINICAL SUSPICION
Nocturnal seizures excluded — clinical features (stereotyped, brief, hypermotor), EEG findings, FLEP scale assessment
Sleep-related hypermotor epilepsy (SHE, formerly nocturnal frontal lobe epilepsy) can closely mimic RBD. Features favouring seizure: highly stereotyped episodes; brief duration (typically <2 minutes); hypermotor activity (cycling, thrashing, jumping); may occur multiple times per night at any sleep stage; personal or family history of epilepsy; PSG EEG showing ictal pattern. The Frontal Lobe Epilepsy and Parasomnias (FLEP) scale can assist differentiation. Full-night vPSG with 18-channel EEG and prolonged montage is gold standard for excluding nocturnal seizures.
RBD classification — isolated vs. secondary CRITICAL FOR PROGNOSIS
Classification: isolated RBD (iRBD) vs. secondary RBD (associated with synucleinopathy, narcolepsy, medications, autoimmune disease, structural lesion)
Isolated (idiopathic) RBD (iRBD): No identifiable cause. The most prognostically significant form — >80% will develop PD, DLB, or MSA within their lifetime (Galbiati A et al. 2019 meta-analysis: 33.5% at 5 years, 82.4% at 10.5 years, 96.6% at 14 years). Term “isolated” is now preferred over “idiopathic” as it accurately reflects the absence of identified cause (rather than implying unknown aetiology).

Secondary RBD causes:
• Neurodegenerative: PD (~30–50%), DLB (~80%), MSA (>90%), Alzheimer’s (less common but reported)
• Narcolepsy: ~30% of narcolepsy patients, typically younger, equal sex distribution, CSF hypocretin deficient
• Medication-induced: SSRIs, SNRIs, TCAs, MAO-B inhibitors
• Autoimmune: Anti-IgLON5 syndrome (RBD + NREM parasomnia + bulbar dysfunction + tauopathy), anti-Ma2, LGI1
• Structural: Brainstem lesion (tumour, stroke, MS plaque affecting subcoeruleus/sublaterodorsal nucleus)
Anti-IgLON5 disease: A rare but treatable autoimmune condition that can cause a complex sleep disorder including both REM and NREM parasomnias, stridor, bulbar dysfunction, gait ataxia, and a tauopathy. Serum and CSF IgLON5 antibodies confirm diagnosis. Responds to immunotherapy. Must be distinguished from synucleinopathy-associated RBD.
Step 5 · Phenoconversion Risk & Prodromal Biomarker Assessment
Isolated RBD is now established as the strongest clinical predictor of alpha-synucleinopathy (PD, DLB, MSA). Galbiati A et al. meta-analysis (Neurology 2019): 33.5% phenoconversion at 5 years, 82.4% at 10.5 years, 96.6% at 14 years. The prodromal period from RBD onset to overt neurodegeneration averages 12–14 years but can span 3–30+ years. Phenotype (PD vs. DLB vs. MSA) cannot be reliably predicted from clinical features alone.
Phenoconversion distribution in iRBD (estimated from longitudinal cohort data):
Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB): ~50% of phenoconverters
Parkinson’s disease (PD): ~45% of phenoconverters
Multiple system atrophy (MSA): ~5% of phenoconverters

Predictors of faster phenoconversion (higher risk): older age at RBD onset; male sex; presence of mild cognitive impairment at diagnosis; greater RSWA burden on PSG; abnormal dopamine transporter imaging (DATscan); hyposmia; colour vision impairment; constipation; orthostatic hypotension; positive alpha-synuclein seed amplification assay (SAA) in CSF or skin; reduced cardiac MIBG uptake.

Prodromal markers assessment — document at diagnosis and annual follow-up STRATIFIES PHENOCONVERSION RISK
Hyposmia — reduced smell identification (UPSIT / Sniffin’ Sticks; present in ~50–70% of iRBD)
Constipation / autonomic dysfunction — <3 stools/week, urinary dysfunction, orthostatic hypotension (≥20 mmHg SBP drop)
Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) — especially visuospatial or executive dysfunction (suggests DLB trajectory)
DATscan (123I-ioflupane SPECT) — reduced striatal dopamine transporter uptake; present in ~30–50% of iRBD at diagnosis
Abnormal DATscan in a patient with iRBD effectively confirms pre-motor dopaminergic neurodegeneration and places the patient on the PD spectrum. Abnormal DATscan in iRBD is associated with 3–4× higher phenoconversion rate and shorter time to conversion. However, DATscan is normal in many iRBD patients (especially early in the disease course) and a normal scan does not reassure regarding long-term risk. DATscan is not required for RBD diagnosis but is a useful prognostic biomarker.
Alpha-synuclein seed amplification assay (SAA): CSF or skin biopsy-based SAA (RT-QuIC or PMCA) detects misfolded alpha-synuclein with >90% sensitivity and specificity in iRBD patients who will phenoconvert. This is currently the most sensitive biomarker for underlying synucleinopathy. Available in specialised research centres and increasingly in clinical practice. A positive SAA in iRBD essentially confirms underlying alpha-synuclein pathology (Parkinson’s Foundation PPMI data, 2023).
Cardiac MIBG scintigraphy — reduced cardiac sympathetic innervation; abnormal in ~60–80% of iRBD; favours PD/DLB over MSA
Alpha-synuclein SAA (CSF or skin biopsy) — >90% sensitivity/specificity in iRBD; confirms underlying synuclein pathology

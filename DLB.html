Step 1 · Essential Feature — Dementia
Dementia is an absolute prerequisite for a DLB diagnosis. The diagnostic criteria cannot be applied without it. DLB should be diagnosed when dementia occurs before or concurrently with parkinsonism.
Essential requirement (Table 1): Dementia defined as a progressive cognitive decline of sufficient magnitude to interfere with normal social or occupational functions, or with usual daily activities.

Cognitive profile: Disproportionate attentional, executive function, and visuoperceptual deficits relative to memory and naming are typical and often occur early. Prominent or persistent memory impairment may not occur in early stages but is usually evident with progression.

DLB vs. PDD (1-year rule): If dementia occurs before or concurrently with parkinsonism → DLB. If dementia develops in the context of well-established Parkinson disease (typically >1 year after PD onset) → Parkinson disease dementia (PDD). The 1-year rule remains recommended for research, though generic terms like "Lewy body disease" are often appropriate clinically.
Progressive cognitive decline sufficient to interfere with social/occupational function or daily activities
The DLB cognitive profile is distinctive: attention, executive function, and visuospatial/perceptual deficits are disproportionate relative to memory and naming. Useful cognitive probes include: trail-making tasks, Stroop tests, phonemic fluency (attention/executive); figure copy, block design, line orientation, pareidolia tasks (visuoperceptual). Memory and object naming tend to be less affected early — in contrast to Alzheimer disease where amnestic deficits dominate from the outset.
Important: If parkinsonism clearly precedes cognitive symptoms by >1 year, consider PDD rather than DLB. In clinical practice, use the term most appropriate to the clinical situation — "Lewy body disease" is often helpful as a generic term. For research purposes, the 1-year rule continues to be recommended.
Step 2 · Core Clinical Features
Four core clinical features — the first three typically occur early and may persist throughout the course. Count how many are present. This determines the diagnostic pathway.
Core clinical features (Table 1):
1. Fluctuating cognition with pronounced variations in attention and alertness
2. Recurrent visual hallucinations — typically well formed and detailed
3. REM sleep behavior disorder — may precede cognitive decline
4. One or more spontaneous cardinal features of parkinsonism (bradykinesia, rest tremor, or rigidity — only one required, not all three)

Combination rules: ≥2 core features → Probable DLB (without needing biomarkers). 1 core feature + ≥1 indicative biomarker → Probable DLB. 1 core feature alone → Possible DLB. Probable DLB should NOT be diagnosed on biomarkers alone.
Fluctuating cognition with pronounced variations in attention and alertness
Core
Delirium-like fluctuations — waxing and waning episodes of behavioral inconsistency, incoherent speech, variable attention, or altered consciousness (staring, zoning out). Differentiated from AD fluctuations by: daytime drowsiness, lethargy, staring into space, episodes of disorganized speech. At least one validated measure of fluctuation should be documented (e.g., Clinician Assessment of Fluctuation scale, Dementia Cognitive Fluctuation Scale). Fluctuations best predict DLB when present early — they also occur in advanced stages of other dementias.
Direct informant questioning about fluctuations may not reliably discriminate DLB from AD. Structured questions about drowsiness, lethargy, staring, and disorganized speech have better discriminating value. Computer-based repeated attentional testing provides an independent method.
Recurrent visual hallucinations — typically well formed and detailed
Core
Occur in up to 80% of DLB patients — a frequent clinical signpost. Typically well-formed, featuring people, children, or animals. Related phenomena include passage hallucinations (fleeting peripheral images of people/animals), sense of presence, and visual illusions. Patients are typically able to report these experiences. Patient responses vary in degree of insight and emotional reaction. Recurrence is characteristic — single isolated episodes carry less diagnostic weight.
Passage hallucinations (brief peripheral sensations of a figure passing) and sense of presence are common accompanying phenomena. These are distinct from the simple, non-formed visual misperceptions of pure visual impairment. Assessment scales (e.g., semi-structured interview by Mosimann et al.) are available for characterization and quantification.
REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD) — may precede cognitive decline
Core
RBD: parasomnia manifested by recurrent dream enactment behavior (movements mimicking dream content) with absence of normal REM sleep atonia. Particularly likely when dreams involve chasing/attacking themes, or if patient or bed partner has sustained injuries from limb movements. May begin many years before other DLB symptoms and may become less vigorous over time. Present in 76% of autopsy-confirmed DLB vs. 4% of non-DLB cases.
Mimics to exclude: Confusional awakenings, severe obstructive sleep apnea, periodic limb movements. Screen using validated tools (Mayo Sleep Questionnaire, single-question RBD screen). If any doubt, refer to sleep clinic or request PSG. PSG confirmation of REM sleep without atonia is one of the indicative biomarkers — if PSG positive in a person with dementia + RBD history, ≥90% likelihood of synucleinopathy.
One or more spontaneous cardinal features of parkinsonism — bradykinesia, rest tremor, or rigidity (not due to antidopaminergic medications or stroke)
Core
Spontaneous parkinsonian features occur in over 85% of DLB eventually. Only one of the three cardinal features (bradykinesia, rest tremor, rigidity) is required — DLB patients' parkinsonism often falls short of the full PD definition of bradykinesia + rest tremor/rigidity. Carefully exclude parkinsonism due to antidopaminergic medications or stroke. In older patients, take care not to misinterpret comorbid signs (arthritis, poor exam compliance due to cognitive impairment).
If parkinsonism is clinically equivocal, a DAT uptake scan may be helpful. Note that parkinsonism as the only core clinical feature, appearing for the first time at a stage of severe dementia, makes DLB less likely (see Step 5). Parkinsonism in DLB is often less responsive to dopaminergic treatments than in PD.

Step 3 · Indicative Biomarkers
Indicative biomarkers have high diagnostic specificity for DLB. One or more indicative biomarkers + ≥1 core clinical feature → Probable DLB. One or more indicative biomarkers alone (no core features) → Possible DLB.
Indicative biomarkers (Table 1):
1. Reduced DAT uptake in basal ganglia — SPECT or PET (sensitivity 78%, specificity 90% vs. AD)
2. Abnormal (low uptake) ¹²³I-MIBG myocardial scintigraphy (sensitivity 69–77%, specificity 87–94% vs. AD)
3. PSG confirmation of REM sleep without atonia — highly specific; if positive in patient with dementia + RBD history, ≥90% likelihood of synucleinopathy

Note: Probable DLB should NOT be diagnosed on the basis of biomarkers alone — at least one core clinical feature must also be present.
Reduced DAT uptake in basal ganglia — ¹²³I-FP-CIT SPECT or PET
Indicative
DAT imaging is well-established for DLB vs. AD: sensitivity 78%, specificity 90%. In DLB: minimal caudate uptake (period/full-stop appearance) vs. robust caudate and putamen uptake in AD and normal controls (comma appearance). If parkinsonism is the only core clinical feature, reduced DAT uptake warrants Probable DLB — provided that other disorders with cognitive impairment and reduced DAT uptake are excluded: PSP, MSA, corticobasal degeneration, frontotemporal dementia.
Important caveat: Normal DAT uptake may occur in autopsy-confirmed DLB — either due to minimal brainstem involvement/limited nigral neuron loss, or balanced dopamine loss across the whole striatum (rather than predominantly in the putamen). A normal scan does not exclude DLB.
Abnormal (low uptake) ¹²³I-MIBG myocardial scintigraphy
Indicative
MIBG quantifies postganglionic sympathetic cardiac innervation, reduced in Lewy body disease. Low heart-to-mediastinum (H/M) ratio. Sensitivity 69% (rising to 77% in milder cases, MMSE >21), specificity 87% (rising to 94% in milder cases) vs. AD. Studies generally excluded patients with comorbidities/medications that cause abnormal MIBG images.
Confounders causing reduced MIBG uptake: Ischemic heart disease, heart failure, diabetes mellitus, peripheral neuropathies, labetalol, reserpine, tricyclic antidepressants, over-the-counter sympathomimetics. Carefully assess for confounding causes before interpreting a low H/M ratio as indicative of DLB. In practice, MIBG has higher specificity than DAT-SPECT when confounders are properly excluded.
PSG confirmation of REM sleep without atonia
Indicative
PSG demonstration of REM sleep without atonia is a highly specific predictor of Lewy-related pathology. If PSG shows REM sleep without atonia in a person with dementia + history of RBD: ≥90% likelihood of a synucleinopathy — sufficient to justify Probable DLB even in the absence of any other core feature or biomarker. PSG is desirable whenever feasible. Conditions mimicking RBD (confusional awakenings, severe OSA, periodic limb movements) must be excluded.

Supportive Biomarkers Consistent with DLB — no standalone diagnostic specificity
Relative preservation of medial temporal lobe (MTL) structures on CT/MRI
Supp BM
AD shows greater MTL/hippocampal atrophy than DLB (hippocampal atrophy correlates strongly with tangle, not plaque or LB, pathology). Absent or minimal MTL atrophy is consistent with DLB but unusual in AD. Sensitivity 64%, specificity 68% (autopsy-confirmed multisite study). MTL atrophy in DLB may signal substantial additional AD copathology and predict a more rapid clinical course.
Generalized low SPECT/PET perfusion/metabolism with reduced occipital activity ± cingulate island sign on FDG-PET
Supp BM
FDG-PET occipital hypometabolism correlates with visual cortex neuropathology in DLB (sensitivity ~70%, specificity ~74% — slightly below indicative biomarker threshold). The cingulate island sign: relative preservation of posterior/midcingulate metabolism on FDG-PET, associated with less concurrent neurofibrillary pathology but no difference in Aβ load relative to AD. HMPAO-SPECT is less accurate (sensitivity 65%, specificity 64%).
Prominent posterior slow-wave EEG activity with periodic fluctuations in the pre-alpha/theta range
Supp BM
Quantitative EEG: pre-alpha dominant frequency, either stable or intermixed with alpha/theta/delta activities in pseudoperiodic patterns — predictive value >90% for DLB vs. AD when these specific posterior abnormalities are present. EEG patterns correlate positively with severity of clinically observed cognitive fluctuations and may be seen at MCI stage.

Step 5 · "DLB Is Less Likely" Conditions
These conditions reduce diagnostic confidence for DLB. They do not automatically exclude DLB but make it less likely. They may indicate mixed or multiple pathologies contributing to the clinical picture.
DLB is less likely (Table 1):
a. In the presence of any other physical illness or brain disorder — including cerebrovascular disease — sufficient to account in part or in total for the clinical picture. (These do not necessarily exclude DLB and may indicate mixed/multiple pathologies.)
b. If parkinsonian features are the only core clinical feature AND appear for the first time at a stage of severe dementia.

These are not absolute exclusions — clinical judgment is required. Mixed pathologies (especially AD + DLB) are common: up to half of carefully diagnosed AD patients may have unsuspected Lewy-related pathology at autopsy.
Other physical illness or brain disorder sufficient to account for the clinical picture (e.g., cerebrovascular disease, metabolic disorder, other structural cause)
Cerebrovascular disease is the most common alternative/contributor. Significant white matter changes, strategic infarcts, or other structural lesions on MRI should prompt consideration of whether they adequately explain the clinical syndrome. However, presence of cerebrovascular disease does not automatically exclude DLB — mixed vascular-DLB pathology exists. Metabolic disorders, toxic encephalopathies, or other treatable causes should be excluded first.
Parkinsonism is the only core clinical feature AND it first appeared at a stage of severe dementia
Late-onset parkinsonism appearing only at a stage of severe dementia — when parkinsonism is the sole core feature — reduces diagnostic confidence for DLB. Parkinsonism emerging early (before or concurrently with dementia) carries much more diagnostic weight. This condition does not apply if multiple core features are present or if indicative biomarkers are positive.

Lesson 01 · 40 minutes

TCD Fundamentals

Before residents can read a single number, they need to understand what the machine is actually measuring and why the vessels appear where they do. This is the scaffolding for everything else.

Opening Frame — 5 min
What TCD Tells You That Nothing Else Does

Open with this question: "MRI, CTA, and MRA tell you what a vessel looks like. What does TCD tell you?" Let them guess. The answer is: TCD tells you how blood is moving through a vessel right now, in real time, at the bedside.

This distinction matters clinically. A CTA might show a 60% MCA stenosis and look the same before and after a blood pressure drop. TCD would show you the hemodynamic consequence — post-stenotic dampening — that the CTA cannot. Conversely, TCD cannot show you vessel wall anatomy, plaque, or vessel caliber directly.

Core Concept
TCD measures flow velocity (cm/s), not flow volume (mL/min). Velocity = volume ÷ cross-sectional area. A narrowed vessel therefore has higher velocity for the same flow volume — this is the physical basis of all stenosis detection on TCD.
Physics — 10 min
The Doppler Equation (Just Enough)

Residents do not need to memorize the Doppler equation, but they must understand its two practical consequences:

  1. 1
    The probe emits 2 MHz ultrasound toward the skull. The 2 MHz frequency is chosen specifically because it penetrates the temporal bone acoustic window. Higher frequencies (e.g., 8 MHz used for carotid duplex) cannot penetrate bone. This is why TCD is window-dependent — approximately 8% of patients have inadequate windows, more common in older women and people of West African descent.
  2. 2
    Moving red blood cells reflect sound back at a shifted frequency. Blood moving toward the probe returns a higher frequency (upward deflection above baseline on the display). Blood moving away returns a lower frequency (deflection below baseline). This is the basis for identifying vessel direction — and for detecting reversed flow in collateral scenarios.
  3. 3
    The depth of the sample gate determines which vessel you're in. The probe gates at a specific depth (e.g., 52 mm) to sample only that location. By sweeping depth from ~40 mm to ~80 mm, you march through different vessel segments. This is why vessel identification is depth-dependent — the MCA lives at 45–65 mm through the temporal window; the basilar lives at 75–110 mm through the suboccipital window.

Waveform Morphology — 10 min
Reading the Shape Before the Number

Before residents look at any MFV number, they should describe the waveform shape. The shape encodes information the number alone cannot convey.

Normal MCA — Low Resistance
Broad rounded peak, sustained diastolic flow well above baseline. Brain is a low-resistance bed demanding continuous perfusion. PI 0.7–1.0.
Normal Ophthalmic — High Resistance
Sharp narrow peak, prominent dicrotic notch, near-zero diastolic flow. Orbit is high-resistance. Any deviation from this pattern in the ophthalmic artery is significant.
Post-Stenotic Dampened
Low-amplitude, monophasic, blunted waveform. Seen distal to a tight stenosis. The pressure gradient across the stenosis dampens pulsatility in the distal vessel.

Lesson 02 · 45 minutes

Intracranial Stenosis

Teach residents to distinguish the three stenosis patterns they will encounter on rounds: focal stenosis with a jet, focal stenosis with post-stenotic dampening, and extensive disease. Then teach them the extracranial localization logic using sentinel vessels.

The Core Concept — 5 min
Bernoulli's Principle at the Bedside

When a vessel narrows, flow must accelerate through the stenotic segment to maintain volume. This is exactly analogous to water flowing faster through a kinked hose. The TCD detects this focal acceleration as a velocity jet at one specific depth. The key teaching point:

The Depth Specificity Rule
A stenotic jet appears at one or two consecutive depths and disappears at adjacent depths. If you find a high velocity that persists across 6+ depths, it is not a focal stenosis — it is a globally hyperdynamic state (fever, anemia, hyperemia). This is the single most important rule for distinguishing focal from diffuse pathology.
Three Patterns — 15 min
Focal Stenosis · Post-Stenotic · Extensive

What you see: A sudden jump in MFV at a single depth. Adjacent depths are normal or lower. The waveform at that depth shows spectral broadening — the color fill becomes dense and wide because turbulent flow contains many different velocity vectors simultaneously.

On the audio: The machine's audio signal becomes harsh and rough at the stenotic depth — described as a "seagull cry" or machinery murmur. This is often the first clue before you look at the numbers.

Example: MCA at 46mm: 72 cm/s → 48mm: 148 cm/s → 50mm: 78 cm/s. The jet is at 48mm.

Focal Stenotic Jet — Depth-by-Depth MFV Profile
The jet at 48 mm (148 cm/s) is flanked by normal velocities at 46 mm (72 cm/s) and 50 mm (76 cm/s). This depth-specificity is the defining feature. All bars except one are below the 100 cm/s threshold (dashed red). If all bars were elevated, this would be hyperemia — not a focal jet.
Threshold (SONIA Trial)
MCA MFV ≥100 cm/s at a single depth with spectral broadening = ≥50% stenosis on DSA (sensitivity 70%, specificity 90%). MFV ≥120 cm/s increases specificity further.

What you see: Low MFV across all sampled depths of a vessel — but no high-velocity jet found. The waveform is dampened, monophasic, blunted. This means the probe is sampling distal to a stenosis, in reconstituted post-stenotic flow. The stenosis itself may be proximal to your accessible depth range, or the window may not allow you to find it.

Key distinguishing feature: In vasospasm, MFV is globally elevated and the Lindegaard Ratio is high. In post-stenotic dampening, MFV is low and the LR is low. These are opposite patterns.

Clinical implication: A negative TCD (no jet found) does not exclude stenosis. If the waveform is dampened and the clinical picture suggests stenosis, the absence of a jet means you're downstream. Recommend CTA or MRA.

Post-Stenotic — Uniformly Low MFV at All Depths
All depths below normal range. No single depth stands out. Contrast this with the focal jet where one bar towers above its neighbours.
Post-Stenotic Waveform — Blunted, Monophasic
Low-amplitude blunted waveform (amber) compared to ghost of normal (grey dashes). The absence of a sharp systolic peak and the near-flat appearance are hallmarks of post-stenotic flow.
Teaching Point
Show residents that in your sample report, the L MCA showed MFV 28–36 cm/s with no jet. The LR was low. The ophthalmic PI was high and antegrade. That constellation = post-stenotic dampening from intracranial MCA disease, not extracranial ICA disease.

What you see: Elevated MFV across multiple consecutive depths spanning a long segment, without one clear peak-and-return pattern. This suggests either a long-segment stenosis, diffuse arteriopathy (e.g., ICAD with multifocal disease), or cerebral hyperemia.

Distinguishing from hyperemia: Use the Lindegaard Ratio. If LR >3 with bilateral MCA elevation, vasospasm or bilateral stenosis is more likely. If LR <3 with elevated MFV bilaterally and symmetric PI, consider systemic hyperemia (fever, severe anemia, hypercapnia).

Clinical relevance: Extensive MCA stenosis (long-segment M1 disease) on TCD correlates with higher stroke risk than focal disease in the WASID cohort, though TCD cannot grade length precisely. CTA or MRA is needed.

Extensive Disease — Broad Elevation Across Multiple Depths (vs Focal Jet)
Left (faded): focal jet — only 48mm spikes above threshold, flanked by normal depths. Right: extensive disease — all sampled depths are above 100 cm/s with no clear peak-and-return, forming a plateau. The plateau pattern cannot be explained by focal stenosis at one point. Use the Lindegaard Ratio to distinguish from hyperemia.
Reference
WASID trial: Chimowitz MI et al., NEJM 2005;352:1305–1316. Demonstrated annual stroke risk 12% for symptomatic 50–99% intracranial stenosis on warfarin, informing the high-risk nature of extensive disease.

This is the most sophisticated skill in basic TCD reading. The ophthalmic artery and carotid siphon together answer a single binary question: Is the extracranial ICA intact?

The mechanism: if the extracranial ICA is severely stenosed or occluded, the ophthalmic artery becomes a collateral conduit — ECA branches supply blood backward through the ophthalmic artery into the intracranial ICA territory. This reverses the direction of ophthalmic flow (now negative on the display) and changes the waveform from high-resistance to low-resistance (sustained diastolic flow, low PI), because it's now feeding the low-resistance brain rather than the high-resistance orbit.

① Antegrade · High PI (>1.2) → ICA Patent
Spiky waveform deflecting above baseline (toward probe = antegrade). Near-zero diastole = high resistance orbit. PI >1.2. Normal ophthalmic — ICA is patent.
② Reversed Flow (Negative) → ICA Occluded
Flow is inverted — away from probe (negative). Waveform is broad with sustained diastole (now low resistance because feeding the brain, not the orbit). ICA must be occluded for this to occur.
③ Antegrade · Low PI (<0.8) → Partial Collateral
Still antegrade (above baseline) but waveform is rounded with sustained diastolic flow — lower resistance than normal. Partial ICA stenosis driving partial collateralization through the ophthalmic artery.
④ Absent Signal → Non-diagnostic
Flat trace — no Doppler signal detected. Cannot distinguish ICA disease from intact ICA. Poor temporal bone window (~8% of patients). Does not localise the lesion.

Lesson 02b · Bonus

TCD-Detectable Collateral Pathways

The ophthalmic artery is only one of six collateral channels the brain recruits when the anterior circulation fails. Each has a distinct TCD signature. Recognising these patterns tells you which routes are active, how complete collateralisation is, and — critically — whether the ischaemic territory is being rescued or not.

The Core Principle
A collateral vessel is identifiable on TCD when it shows: (1) elevated MFV — it is carrying more blood than usual, (2) altered flow direction — it is receiving flow from an unexpected source, or (3) abnormally low PI — it is now feeding a low-resistance ischaemic territory rather than its normal bed. Any of these three changes in a vessel that was previously normal = that vessel is now a collateral conduit.
Pathway 1 — Already Covered
Ophthalmic Artery (ECA → ICA Retrograde)

Covered in the sentinel vessels section above. Reversed flow + low PI in the ophthalmic artery = ECA branches supplying the intracranial ICA territory retrograde. Active when the extracranial ICA is severely stenosed or occluded. Most commonly assessed collateral on TCD.

Pathway 2 — Most Important Intracranial Collateral
Anterior Communicating Artery (AComm) — Cross-Filling from Contralateral ICA

The AComm connects the two A1 segments of the ACA at the midline. When the ipsilateral ICA is compromised, the contralateral ICA can push blood across the AComm into the ipsilateral A1 → ipsilateral MCA territory. This is the single most powerful intracranial collateral and is present in the majority of patients with haemodynamically significant ICA disease.

TCD cannot directly insonaten the AComm itself — it is too small and too medial. Instead, you infer its activity through four indirect signs that together constitute the AComm collateral signature.

Sign 1 — Ipsilateral A1 ACA: Reversed Flow
Normally the ipsilateral A1 ACA flows away from the temporal probe (negative, below baseline). When AComm is active, contralateral blood pushes through AComm and enters the ipsilateral A1 in the opposite direction — it now flows toward the probe (positive, above baseline). This reversal is pathognomonic for AComm collateralisation.
Sign 2 — Contralateral A1 ACA: Hyperflow
The contralateral A1 ACA carries flow for both its own hemisphere and the ischaemic ipsilateral territory. Its MFV is therefore elevated above normal. Compare contralateral A1 to the reversed ipsilateral A1 — asymmetry confirms cross-filling direction and volume.
Sign 3 — Contralateral MCA: Elevated MFV
The contralateral MCA reflects the increased demand on the contralateral ICA. Elevated contralateral MCA MFV (>120% of expected for age) corroborates AComm cross-filling and quantifies how much extra flow is being recruited through the collateral.
Sign 4 — Cross-Compression Test (Confirmatory)
Manual compression of the contralateral CCA for 3–5 seconds momentarily stops cross-filling. If the ipsilateral MCA velocity drops during compression and recovers on release, AComm patency and cross-filling are confirmed. Contraindicated in acute stroke, known contralateral carotid stenosis, or cardiac instability.
Primary Source
Ringelstein EB et al. Noninvasive assessment of CO₂-induced cerebral vasomotor response. Stroke 1988;19:963–969. AComm collateral identification: Schneider PA et al., J Vasc Surg 1988. Cross-compression protocol: Caplan LR, Transcranial Doppler, 1999.
Pathway 3 — Posterior-to-Anterior Rescue
Posterior Communicating Artery (PComm) — Basilar → Ipsilateral MCA

The PComm connects the PCA (P1 segment) to the ICA siphon, providing a potential bridge between the posterior circulation and the anterior circulation. In most adults the PComm is small — but in approximately 20–25% of individuals it is a fetal PComm (large caliber, providing the dominant supply to the PCA territory). A fetal PComm is both a collateral asset and an anatomical variant that changes how you read the TCD.

When ipsilateral ICA flow is compromised, the PComm can carry flow retrograde from the basilar artery into the ipsilateral ICA → MCA. You cannot directly insonate the PComm, but you detect its activity through changes in the PCA and siphon.

Normal PCA (P1) — Away from Probe, Low MFV
Normal P1 PCA segment: flows away from the temporal probe (below baseline), MFV 35–55 cm/s. Both hemispheres symmetric. This is your baseline before looking for PComm collateral activity.
PComm Active — Ipsilateral PCA: Reversed + Elevated
When PComm collateralises, the ipsilateral P1 reverses — basilar flow now pushes forward through PComm into the ipsilateral ICA. The P1 signal appears above baseline (toward probe) and MFV is elevated. Ghost of normal P1 (below baseline) shown in grey for comparison.
Fetal PComm Variant — Recognising It on TCD
Fetal PComm: The ipsilateral P1 segment is absent or tiny (MFV <20 cm/s or no signal) because the PCA territory is supplied entirely from the ICA via the large PComm. The siphon is therefore large and intact. Critical trap: absent ipsilateral P1 does not mean basilar occlusion — check if the contralateral P1 and basilar are normal. A fetal PComm on one side makes that hemisphere dependent on the ipsilateral ICA for both MCA and PCA territory.
The Fetal PComm Trap
Fetal PComm (~22% of hemispheres) produces absent or very small ipsilateral P1 on TCD. The clinical implication: an ipsilateral ICA occlusion in a patient with fetal PComm causes both MCA and PCA territory infarction simultaneously — a much larger stroke than expected. Always check P1 symmetry when assessing posterior circulation.
Pathway 4 — Inferring the Uninferrable
Leptomeningeal (Pial) Collaterals — What TCD Can and Cannot Tell You

Leptomeningeal collaterals are tiny pial arteries on the cortical surface that interconnect MCA, ACA, and PCA terminal branches at the watershed zones. They are too small (~0.1–0.5 mm) to be directly insonated by TCD. However, their haemodynamic effect — rescuing distal MCA territory from proximal MCA or ICA occlusion — produces several indirect TCD fingerprints.

Sign 1: The "Stump Phenomenon" — Preserved Distal MCA Flow Despite Proximal Occlusion▼

If the proximal MCA (M1) is occluded but distal MCA branches (M2/M3) are still detectable, this implies retrograde filling of distal MCA via pial collaterals from ACA or PCA territory. The distal MCA signal will be dampened and low-MFV — but its presence at all, when the proximal signal is absent, is the fingerprint of pial collateral rescue.

On TCD: absent or severely dampened signal at proximal MCA depths (46–52 mm) with a residual low-MFV signal detectable at deeper depths (60–68 mm, which sample more distal MCA segments insonated through the temporal window at different angles). The proximal-to-distal gradient is reversed.

Clinical importance: In acute MCA occlusion, the presence of any distal MCA signal on TCD — even dampened — predicts better collateral grade on CTA/MRA and better outcome after thrombectomy. It implies some pial collateral flow to the penumbra.

Sign 2: Retrograde ACA Flow — ACA Territory Lending to MCA Watershed▼

When ACA-to-MCA pial collaterals are active, the ACA is carrying additional flow to supply the MCA watershed zone. The ipsilateral ACA may show: elevated MFV above expected (carrying more flow than normal for its territory), and in some cases the flow direction changes at the ACA/MCA junction depth (66 mm, where the TCD samples the MCA/ACA bifurcation) to show bidirectional flow reflecting dual-direction collateral supply.

This is the least precise TCD collateral sign — it requires serial comparison studies or knowledge of the patient's baseline ACA velocity. An isolated elevated ipsilateral ACA in the setting of proximal MCA disease is a supportive finding, not definitive proof of pial collateral activity.

Sign 3: Cerebrovascular Reactivity (CO₂ / Breath-Holding Index) — The Haemodynamic Reserve Test▼

This is the most physiologically sophisticated TCD collateral test and the most directly relevant to stroke prevention in ICAD and carotid stenosis. The principle: normal cerebral vasculature vasodilates in response to hypercapnia (breath-holding or CO₂ inhalation), increasing MCA MFV by ~40–50%. When the collateral supply is already maximally vasodilated — because it is working at capacity just to maintain baseline flow — there is no further reserve. This blunted or absent CO₂ reactivity is called vasomotor exhaustion and indicates haemodynamic failure.

The Breath-Holding Index (BHI): Patient holds breath for 30 seconds → measure % increase in MCA MFV. BHI <0.69 (or <30% MFV increase) = impaired vasomotor reserve. Validated as a predictor of ipsilateral stroke risk in patients with carotid occlusion (Silvestrini et al., Neurology 1999).

Clinical use: In a patient with ICA occlusion or severe ICAD who you are considering for extracranial-intracranial (EC-IC) bypass, demonstrating haemodynamic failure via absent CO₂ reactivity on TCD is the key physiological criterion for surgical candidacy.

Sign 4: Microembolic Signals in Distal MCA Despite Proximal Occlusion▼

If MES are detected in a distal MCA territory despite an occluded proximal MCA or ICA, the emboli must have arrived via a collateral route. This is a rare but definitive sign that a collateral pathway (AComm, PComm, or pial) is patent and actively carrying flow — because emboli can only travel down a patent vessel.

This finding is also clinically alarming: it means the ischaemic territory is still being seeded with emboli despite being partially protected by collaterals, suggesting the embolic source is upstream of the collateral entry point.

Stump Phenomenon — Proximal MCA Absent, Distal MCA Residual Signal
Proximal MCA depths (44–52 mm): no signal — M1 is occluded. Distal MCA depths (56–68 mm): low-MFV residual signal (12–24 cm/s), dampened and blunted. This distal signal can only exist if blood is arriving retrograde from pial collaterals. The increasing signal with depth (56→68 mm) reflects flow arriving from the cortical surface backward toward the M2/M1 junction.
Pathway 5 — Posterior Rescue of Posterior Circulation
Vertebral Cross-Filling — One VA Supplying Both Territories

When one vertebral artery is occluded or severely stenosed, the contralateral vertebral artery must supply the entire basilar artery and both posterior territories. TCD detects this as asymmetric vertebral MFV — the dominant (compensating) vertebral shows elevated MFV, while the affected side shows absent or dampened signal.

The compensating vertebral may also show reversal at the junction between V4 and the basilar — blood entering the basilar from the dominant side and flowing retrograde in the proximal basilar (away from its normal rostral direction) before turning around. This retrograde basilar segment is an important finding in basilar occlusion that can be missed if you only sample the mid-basilar.

Clinical Pearl
In posterior circulation TIA, always compare bilateral vertebral MFV side to side. Asymmetry >30% with the contralateral side elevated suggests the elevated side is compensating for a compromised ipsilateral vertebral. This is the haemodynamic correlate of subclavian steal when the ipsilateral vertebral is reversed.

Lesson 03 · 40 minutes

Vasospasm in SAH

Vasospasm monitoring is one of the most time-critical TCD applications in the NeuroICU. Residents need to understand the Lindegaard Ratio deeply — not just memorize the cutoffs, but know why it exists and what it can and cannot tell them.

This lesson is best taught at the bedside of a Hunt-Hess III or IV SAH patient in the NeuroICU, ideally between days 4–14 post-bleed when vasospasm risk peaks. If that isn't possible, bring the prior TCD report and walk through it in the team room before rounds.
Pathophysiology — 10 min
Why Subarachnoid Blood Causes Vasospasm

Subarachnoid hemorrhage deposits oxyhemoglobin in the CSF surrounding the intracranial arteries. Oxyhemoglobin is a potent spasmogen — it triggers endothelin-1 release, reduces nitric oxide bioavailability, and causes sustained smooth muscle contraction in the arterial wall over 4–14 days post-bleed.

The consequence for TCD: the arterial lumen narrows. By Bernoulli's principle, velocity must increase to maintain the same flow volume. Unlike a stenosis from atherosclerosis which is fixed, vasospasm can be dynamic — worsening and improving over hours. This is why serial TCD monitoring is performed daily in high-grade SAH.

Evidence Base
Vasospasm monitoring protocol based on: Sloan MA et al., Stroke 2004 — American Academy of Neurology practice parameter. Peak vasospasm risk: days 4–14 post-SAH. TCD sensitivity for vasospasm ~67%, specificity ~99% (Lysakowski et al., Crit Care Med 2001).
The Core Tool — 15 min
The Lindegaard Ratio — Why It Exists

Here is the problem the Lindegaard Ratio solves: an elevated MCA MFV can come from two completely different causes — vasospasm or systemic hyperemia. Patients post-SAH are frequently febrile, anemic, and receiving IV fluids, all of which increase cardiac output and raise cerebral blood flow velocity globally. If you see MCA MFV 130 cm/s, is that vasospasm in the MCA or is it a globally hyperdynamic patient whose MCA happens to be running fast?

The Lindegaard Ratio divides the MCA MFV by the extracranial ICA MFV sampled submandibularly. In systemic hyperemia, BOTH vessels speed up — the ratio stays low because the numerator and denominator rise together. In focal vasospasm, only the intracranial MCA accelerates — the extracranial ICA is unaffected — so the ratio rises.

Lindegaard Ratio Formula
LR = MCA MFV ÷ Extracranial ICA MFV
LR <3 = Normal (vasospasm excluded regardless of MCA MFV)
LR 3–6 = Borderline — need MFV >120 cm/s to call vasospasm
LR >6 = Vasospasm (severe if MFV >200 cm/s)

Source: Lindegaard KF et al., J Neurosurg 1988;69:516–523

Posterior Circulation — 5 min
Basilar Vasospasm — A Different Ratio

For the basilar artery, the equivalent of the Lindegaard Ratio is the Soustiel Ratio (or basilar/vertebral ratio): Basilar MFV ÷ Extracranial Vertebral MFV. Thresholds: >2 = mild-moderate vasospasm, >3 = severe. This is less well-validated than the Lindegaard Ratio but useful when basilar MFV is elevated after posterior fossa or basilar aneurysm SAH.

Basilar vasospasm is clinically under-recognized — it can present as fluctuating brainstem symptoms, nausea, and depressed consciousness that get attributed to hydrocephalus or re-bleed. TCD can flag it before CT does.

Reference
Soustiel JF et al., Stroke 2002;33:1886–1893. Basilar artery MFV threshold for vasospasm: ≥60 cm/s (same SONIA-derived stenosis threshold), but in the SAH context, rising trend on serial studies is often more useful than a single threshold value.

Lesson 04 · 35 minutes

Microembolic Signals (MES)

Microembolic signals are the TCD finding that most directly changes acute stroke management — detecting ongoing embolism from a culprit source. Residents need to know the consensus criteria, the solid/gaseous distinction, and when to order monitoring.

What Produces a MES — 8 min
The Physics of an Embolus on Doppler

When a microembolus (platelet aggregate, thrombus fragment, lipid particle, or air bubble) passes through the Doppler sample gate, it reflects ultrasound with dramatically higher intensity than surrounding red blood cells — because the acoustic impedance mismatch between the embolus and blood is much greater. This produces a brief, high-intensity transient signal (HITS) superimposed on the background Doppler spectrum.

On the audio output, a MES sounds like a chirp, click, or whistle — distinctly different from the rhythmic whooshing of background flow. This audio character is one of the HITS diagnostic criteria and is reproducible enough that experienced operators can identify MES by ear before seeing the waveform.

MES Illustrations — Meets Criteria vs Does Not Meet Criteria
✓ Valid MES — All ARTHUR Criteria Met
Classic valid MES: single high-intensity spike rising sharply above the background spectrum, unidirectional (above baseline only), brief (<300 ms), occurring at a random point in the cardiac cycle. Clinically significant — count it.
✗ Artefact — Bidirectional (crosses baseline)
Artefact: the spike crosses the baseline — it appears both above AND below zero simultaneously. This is the defining feature of artefact vs true MES. True MES never cross the baseline because an embolus only moves in one direction relative to the probe.
✗ EKG Artefact — Fixed Timing (not random)
EKG artefact: a spike appears at an identical phase of every cardiac cycle — locked to systole. True MES occur randomly. If you see spikes at the same moment in every beat, this is artefact from electrical coupling with the cardiac monitor.
Critical: Artifacts Look Like MES
Common artifacts that mimic MES: patient movement (bidirectional, often prolonged), probe tapping (bidirectional, high intensity), EKG artifact (fixed timing with cardiac cycle), and power-line interference (rhythmic, fixed frequency). The distinguishing features: artifacts are bidirectional (appear both above and below baseline simultaneously) and often have prolonged duration.
Solid vs Gaseous — 8 min
The Clinical Importance of Embolus Type

Not all MES are equal. Gas emboli (air bubbles, agitated saline bubbles in PFO testing) and solid emboli (thrombus, platelets) produce different intensities and frequency characteristics. This distinction is now possible with dual-frequency TCD (using simultaneous 2 MHz and 2.5 MHz probes), which the HITS Automatic Embolus Differentiation (HITS-AED) algorithm exploits.

Clinical Contexts — 10 min
When to Order MES Monitoring and What It Changes
Atrial Fibrillation▼

In AF, thrombus in the left atrial appendage can fragment and embolize. MES monitoring in the bilateral MCA over 30 minutes can detect ongoing cardioembolism. Bilateral simultaneous MES strongly suggest a cardiac or proximal aortic source (because both MCAs are being seeded from the same upstream source).

Clinical utility: if a patient with "cryptogenic" stroke is found to have bilateral MES, this shifts the pre-test probability toward cardioembolic source and may support earlier anticoagulation or extended cardiac monitoring.

Carotid Stenosis / Post-Endarterectomy▼

High-grade ipsilateral carotid stenosis generates MES from ulcerated plaque or platelet aggregation. In symptomatic carotid stenosis, MES are found in ~40–50% of patients within 24–48h of TIA/stroke and predict early recurrence.

CARESS trial evidence: Dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin + clopidogrel) reduced MES rate by 39.8% vs aspirin alone in symptomatic carotid stenosis (Markus HS et al., Lancet Neurol 2005). This is one of the few RCT-level data supporting MES monitoring as a therapeutic endpoint.

Post-CEA: MES within 6 hours of surgery predict hyperperfusion syndrome and early recurrent stroke. This is a direct TCD application in the post-op period.

Intracranial Atherosclerosis (ICAD)▼

In symptomatic ICAD, artery-to-artery MES are detected in the downstream MCA territory. MES in ICAD are unilateral (ipsilateral to the culprit vessel) — a key feature distinguishing them from bilateral cardiac MES.

The SAMMPRIS trial established aggressive medical management for 50–99% symptomatic ICAD (aspirin + clopidogrel + risk factor control) over stenting. MES monitoring is used in research to evaluate treatment response, though not yet standard of care in most centers.

During Cardiac and Endovascular Procedures▼

Intraoperative TCD MES monitoring during cardiac surgery (CABG, valve replacement) can detect cerebral embolism in real time. High MES counts correlate with post-operative neurocognitive decline. MES are primarily gaseous (air) during cardiac bypass and solid (particulate/thrombus) during valve manipulation.

During carotid stenting, MES count during and after stent deployment predicts periprocedural stroke risk and can be used to guide embolic protection device use.

  • B. Cardioembolic or proximal aortic source
  • C. Technical artifact from probe movement
  • D. Bilateral intracranial atherosclerosis
Lesson 05 · 35 minutes

PFO Detection & Grading

Contrast TCD (c-TCD) is one of the most important PFO screening tools available — it is more sensitive than c-TTE and approaches the sensitivity of c-TEE in experienced hands. Residents need to understand the technique, the Valsalva maneuver's role, and the Spencer grading scale.

The Technique — 10 min
Agitated Saline and the Valsalva Maneuver

The principle: inject agitated saline (creating microbubbles) into a peripheral vein. In a normal heart, bubbles are trapped in the pulmonary capillary bed and never reach the arterial circulation. In a right-to-left shunt (PFO, ASD, pulmonary AVM), bubbles cross directly into the arterial circulation and appear as MES in the MCAs within 5–25 cardiac cycles of injection.

  1. 1
    Set up bilateral MCA monitoring Both MCAs simultaneously at ~52 mm depth. Ensure adequate signal quality bilaterally before starting.
  2. 2
    Prepare agitated saline 10 mL saline + 0.5 mL air in two 10 mL syringes connected by a 3-way stopcock. Agitate 10–15 times rapidly between syringes until a frothy, opaque mixture is achieved. Some centers add 0.5 mL of blood to stabilize bubble size.
  3. 3
    Inject at rest first Inject rapidly through an antecubital or femoral vein. Watch MCA signal for 25 cardiac cycles (~20–25 seconds). Count MES. A positive result at rest = large shunt.
  4. 4
    Repeat with Valsalva Inject a second bolus. At 5 seconds post-injection, instruct patient to strain hard (bear down, as if having a bowel movement) for 5–10 seconds, then release abruptly. The Valsalva transiently raises right atrial pressure above left atrial pressure, forcing the PFO open if present. The abrupt release then creates a bolus of right atrial flow.
Valsalva Timing Is Everything
The Valsalva must be performed during injection — not before, not after. A poorly timed Valsalva is the most common cause of false-negative c-TCD. Teach residents to coach the patient clearly: "Start straining when I say GO, and keep straining until I say STOP."
Grading — 10 min
Spencer Grading Scale

The Spencer scale grades shunt size based on the total number of MES appearing in the MCA within 25 cardiac cycles of contrast injection. It is the most widely used grading system for c-TCD PFO assessment.

Visual scale of Spencer grades. Reference
Spencer MP et al., J Neuroimaging 1997;7(suppl 1):S11–S16. Sensitivity of c-TCD for PFO: ~96% vs c-TEE ~89% in meta-analysis by Serena J et al., Stroke 2005;36:602–606, when Valsalva is performed correctly. The superiority of c-TCD reflects its sensitivity to even small shunts and the patient's ability to perform a more forceful Valsalva when not sedated.
Clinical Integration — 8 min
What to Do With a Positive c-TCD

A positive c-TCD does not, by itself, determine stroke mechanism. PFO is present in approximately 25% of the general population (Hagen PT et al., Mayo Clin Proc 1984). The clinical question is: is this PFO causally related to the stroke, or is it an incidental finding?

The RoPE score (Risk of Paradoxical Embolism) was developed to estimate the probability that a detected PFO is stroke-related. High RoPE score (≥7) indicates higher likelihood of PFO-attributable stroke and was used to enrich populations in the major closure trials.

Clinical Pearl — Closure Trial Evidence
Three major RCTs support PFO closure in selected cryptogenic stroke patients: RESPECT (extended follow-up), CLOSE, and REDUCE trials. Meta-analysis: PFO closure reduced recurrent stroke RR ~0.36 vs medical therapy in patients aged <60 with cryptogenic stroke and large or high-risk PFO (Lee PH et al., JAMA 2020). A positive c-TCD with Spencer Grade ≥III supports referral to structural cardiology for closure discussion.

Case-Based Consolidation

Four cases designed to integrate all prior lessons. Work through each case together before revealing the answer. Encourage residents to narrate their reasoning out loud — correct interpretation requires sequential logic, not just pattern matching.

Run this as a Socratic session. For each case, read the history aloud and then stop. Ask: "Before I show you any numbers — what do you want to assess first?" Guide them toward the 5-step framework: waveform morphology, then MFV thresholds, then asymmetry, then Lindegaard, then sentinels. Case 03
44M · Cryptogenic Stroke · PFO Evaluation

History: No vascular risk factors. DWI shows small cortical infarct right parietal lobe. Holter negative. Echo shows possible small PFO on bubble study. RoPE score 8. c-TCD ordered.

Setup: Bilateral MCA monitoring established. Signal quality confirmed bilaterally. Agitated saline prepared. Pause here — before injection, ask residents what a positive result looks like, what distinguishes a cardiac shunt from a pulmonary AVM on timing, and what Spencer grade would change management.

c-TCD result: 0 MES bilateral at rest. Valsalva injection: 2 MES right MCA, 3 MES left MCA within cardiac cycles 6–9. No MES after cycle 15.

→ Reveal Interpretation▼

Spencer Grade I (1–10 MES). Small shunt, only with Valsalva, bilateral. The timing (cycles 6–9) is consistent with a cardiac shunt — pulmonary AVM would appear earlier (cycles 3–5) because the pulmonary transit time is shorter. Absence at rest indicates small shunt not crossing without augmented right atrial pressure.

Clinical interpretation: Small PFO confirmed. With a RoPE score of 8, this PFO is more likely to be causally related to the stroke. However, Spencer Grade I (small) is below the threshold most closure trials used for enrollment. Recommend structural cardiology discussion regarding risk-benefit of closure vs long-term antiplatelet therapy. This case illustrates why the size of the shunt matters — not just its presence.

Case 04
58M · ICAD Follow-Up · Symptom Recurrence

History: Known 70% right MCA stenosis on prior MRA. Presenting with brief left-hand clumsiness. On aspirin + clopidogrel post-SAMMPRIS protocol. TCD + MES monitoring ordered.

Waveform description: At depth 52 mm in the right MCA, a sudden velocity increase is heard on audio — the Doppler signal becomes harsh and rough. The spectral display at this depth is dense and widened. At 48 mm and 56 mm the signal returns to a normal morphology. Left MCA is symmetric and unremarkable. Pause here — ask residents what this depth-specific finding represents, what the audio character tells them, and what additional 30-minute monitoring study they want to add.

TCD + MES data: R MCA depth 52: MFV 138 cm/s, spectral broadening noted. R MCA depth 48 and 56: MFV 84 cm/s and 76 cm/s respectively. LR 3.2. 30-min MES monitoring: 11 unidirectional HITS in right MCA, 0 in left MCA. All HITS unidirectional, lasting 40–90 ms, random timing, chirp quality.

→ Reveal Interpretation▼

Stenosis: Focal velocity jet at 52 mm (138 cm/s), with normal-range velocities at 48 mm and 56 mm — this is depth-specific, consistent with a focal stenosis at M1. LR 3.2 (borderline) — vasospasm not applicable here (no SAH). This is an atherosclerotic jet. The MFV has risen from prior study, suggesting progression.

MES: 11 HITS, all unilateral (right MCA only), meeting all HITS consensus criteria. Unilateral MES from an ICAD lesion = artery-to-artery embolism from the stenotic MCA segment. This is the likely mechanism of his recurring symptoms.

Clinical action: He is already on dual antiplatelet per SAMMPRIS protocol. Ongoing MES despite DAPT is concerning — discuss with neuroendovascular team. Review BP targets, statin dose, and compliance. Consider adding cilostazol as a third antiplatelet agent (limited evidence in ICAD). If breakthrough symptoms continue, DSA and endovascular discussion may be warranted despite SAMMPRIS data showing stenting inferiority — this patient represents a high-risk refractory case.

The One Rule They Must Never Forget
A focal stenotic jet is depth-specific. If velocity is elevated across many depths simultaneously, think hyperemia, not stenosis. If you find one high-velocity depth flanked by normal depths with spectral broadening, that is your jet. Everything else in TCD interpretation builds on understanding this distinction.

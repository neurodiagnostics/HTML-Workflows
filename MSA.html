Step 1 · Essential Features
Prerequisites for a clinical MSA diagnosis at all certainty levels. All three must be present.
Essential features (all clinical categories): Sporadic, progressive, adult-onset (>30 years) disease.
The MDS-MSA criteria define four certainty levels: Clinically Established MSA (maximum specificity), Clinically Probable MSA (balanced sensitivity/specificity), Possible Prodromal MSA (research category, limited specificity), and Neuropathologically Established MSA (autopsy gold standard).
Symptom onset after age 30 years
No postmortem-proven MSA cases with onset in the third decade or earlier exist. Age >30 is therefore a hard prerequisite. Disease onset is defined as the first subjective complaint of symptoms related to MSA.
Negative family history (no first-degree relative with same condition)
MSA is a sporadic disease. A positive family history should prompt consideration of genetic causes of ataxia or parkinsonism — particularly SCA (SCA1, SCA2, SCA3, SCA6, SCA7, SCA12, SCA17), FRDA, FXTAS, CANVAS, and other genetic MSA look-alikes. Genetic screening is recommended for patients with cerebellar features.
Progressive disease course
The course must be clearly progressive — not static, episodic, or fluctuating. A progressive course is defined from the first symptom of MSA onset. Sudden onset, stepwise worsening, or very rapid deterioration should prompt consideration of alternative diagnoses (vascular, autoimmune, prion).

MSA is classified by predominant motor syndrome. This determines which MRI markers apply for Clinically Established MSA.
Subtype determines:

The core features of autonomic failure, parkinsonism, and cerebellar syndrome apply to both subtypes, but parkinsonism in MSA-P must be poorly L-dopa responsive for Established criteria, and cerebellar syndrome requires ≥2 features for Established (≥1 for Probable).
Step 3 · Core Autonomic Features
Autonomic dysfunction is required for Clinically Established MSA. For Probable MSA, it is one of three core features of which ≥2 are needed. Note the different thresholds between categories.
Autonomic dysfunction core feature — at least one of:
For Established: Voiding difficulties with PVR ≥100 mL, OR urinary urge incontinence, OR nOH ≥20/10 mmHg within 3 minutes
For Probable: Voiding difficulties with PVR of any volume, OR urinary urge incontinence, OR nOH ≥20/10 mmHg within 10 minutes
Autonomic dysfunction is not required for Probable MSA — it is only one of three possible core features (≥2 needed).
Unexplained voiding difficulties with elevated post-void residual (PVR)
Voiding difficulties with urine retained in the bladder after a voluntary void, measured by bladder ultrasound, urodynamics, or in-out catheterization. Bladder outflow obstruction (prostate enlargement in men) must be excluded. PVR ≥100 mL qualifies for Established criteria. Any PVR volume qualifies for Probable criteria.
Specificity data: PVR ≥100 mL is the most specific sign of bladder dysfunction in MSA vs. PD (sensitivity 34%, specificity 95%). PVR in MSA increases progressively — from ~71 mL in year 1 to ~129 mL in year 2 from onset. Not useful to distinguish MSA from PSP.
Unexplained urinary urge incontinence
Complaint of involuntary urine leakage associated with urgency, in the absence of urinary tract infections. Non-neurogenic causes (previous pelvic surgery, pelvic floor prolapse) should be excluded. Qualifies for both Established and Probable criteria. Less specific than PVR (sensitivity 48%, specificity 34% vs. PD).
Neurogenic orthostatic hypotension (nOH) — ≥20 mmHg systolic BP drop (with blunted HR response)
≥20 mmHg systolic BP drop usually accompanied by ≥10 mmHg diastolic BP drop and ΔHR/ΔSBP ratio <0.5 bpm/mmHg. Neurogenic cause confirmed by blunted HR rise. Oscillometric measurements. Secondary causes (diabetic autonomic neuropathy) and medications impairing HR response (beta-blockers) must be excluded.

Timing distinction: Within 3 minutes for Established MSA criteria. Within 10 minutes for Probable MSA (delayed nOH is more sensitive, captures earlier disease). An isolated SBP drop without DBP drop is accepted (isolated DBP drop is non-specific).
Sensitivity for nOH within 3 min: MSA-P vs. PD 20–61%; MSA-C vs. SAOA 32–56%. Including 10-min window increases sensitivity. Continuous non-invasive BP/HR monitoring may be used where available. Gold standard for neurogenic cause: prolonged BP recovery time on Valsalva maneuver.
—Autonomic
Step 4 · Core Motor Features
Parkinsonism and/or cerebellar syndrome. Note different thresholds between Established and Probable categories.
Motor core features — different thresholds apply:
Parkinsonism: Bradykinesia + rigidity or tremor. For Established: must be poorly L-dopa responsive (<30% UPDRS-III improvement on ≤1,000 mg). For Probable: any levodopa response qualifies.
Cerebellar syndrome: For Established: ≥2 of gait ataxia, limb ataxia, cerebellar dysarthria, oculomotor features. For Probable: ≥1 of these features.
Oculomotor features = sustained nystagmus (gaze-evoked horizontal or downbeat) OR saccadic hypermetria.
Parkinsonism
Parkinsonism — bradykinesia + rigidity or tremor (excluding intentional tremor in cerebellar syndrome)
Assessed by a movement disorder specialist per MDS-UPDRS III. Bradykinesia = slowness with decrement in amplitude/speed as movements are continued. Rigidity = velocity-independent resistance to passive movement. Tremor = rhythmic or arrhythmic involuntary movement in arms or legs (intentional tremor in a cerebellar syndrome does NOT count). A beneficial levodopa response occurs in 42.5–56.7% of MSA-P and 12.9–25% of MSA-C.
Acute levodopa challenge test cannot reliably assist in early MSA diagnosis — a negative test result should not discourage initiation of chronic levodopa treatment. Poor levodopa responsiveness is defined as <30% UPDRS-III improvement on ≤1,000 mg/day if tolerated, for at least 1 month.
Cerebellar Syndrome
Cerebellar syndrome — gait ataxia, limb ataxia, cerebellar dysarthria, or oculomotor dysfunction
For Clinically Established MSA: ≥2 of the following: gait ataxia, limb ataxia, cerebellar dysarthria, oculomotor dysfunction (sustained gaze-evoked horizontal or downbeat nystagmus, or saccadic hypermetria).

For Clinically Probable MSA: ≥1 of these features is sufficient.

For Possible Prodromal MSA: Subtle cerebellar signs — at least one of impaired tandem gait, gait ataxia, limb ataxia, cerebellar dysarthria, or oculomotor features — judged as subtle by a movement disorder specialist.
—Motor Core
Step 5 · Supportive Clinical Features
Established MSA requires ≥2 supportive features. Probable MSA requires ≥1 (excluding erectile dysfunction as an isolated supportive feature). Mark all that apply.
Supportive features and MSA "red flags" are synonymous. Accumulation over a lifetime increases postmortem MSA accuracy. Note: within 3 years from disease onset, supportive features do not differentiate MSA from MSA look-alikes (PD, DLB, PSP) — their significance increases with the clinical constellation.
Supportive Motor Features
Rapid progression — needs help with chores or greater disability within 3 years of motor onset
Assessed by history. Rate of progression rapid compared to what a movement disorder specialist would anticipate for Parkinson's disease. Median latency to falls: MSA 24 months vs. PD 118 months.
Moderate to severe postural instability within 3 years of motor onset
Deficient postural response: ≥3 steps backwards OR tendency to fall if not caught on pull-test within 3 years of motor onset. Specificity vs. PD: 88.7%. Specificity if within first year of motor onset: 94%.
Craniocervical dystonia induced or exacerbated by L-dopa in absence of limb dyskinesia
Involuntary dystonic movements of the face (orofacial dystonia) induced or exacerbated by L-dopa in the absence or presence of very mild limb dyskinesia. High specificity for MSA vs. PD and LBD.
Severe speech impairment within 3 years of motor onset
Slow, slurred, or dysphonic speech severe enough to require occasional repetition of statements during interview, within 3 years of motor onset.
Severe dysphagia within 3 years of motor onset
Unexplained Babinski sign
Other causes (mass lesions, vascular, demyelinating, metabolic, cervical myelopathy, infections) must be excluded. An unexplained extensor plantar response in this context supports MSA.
Jerky myoclonic postural or kinetic tremor
Irregular small-amplitude postural or kinetic tremor of the hands or fingers with stimulus-sensitive myoclonus. Polyminimyoclonus — distinct from the classic pill-rolling rest tremor of PD, which is infrequent in MSA.
Postural deformities (anterocollis, laterocollis, camptocormia, Pisa syndrome, contractures of hands or feet)
At least one of: disproportionate anterocollis or laterocollis, camptocormia (severe anterior flexion of spine), Pisa syndrome (severe lateral flexion), or contractures of hands or feet. Excludes Dupuytren's or contracture due to other known causes including corticobasal syndrome.
Supportive Non-Motor Features
Stridor — high-pitched inspiratory breathing sound (during sleep or awake)
High-pitched inspiratory sound during sleep or while awake. Laryngoscopy may be considered to exclude mechanical lesions or functional vocal cord abnormalities. Stridor is distinctive of both MSA subtypes vs. their mimics — considered a supportive non-motor feature of high specificity.
Inspiratory sighs — involuntary deep inspiratory sighs or gasps
Cold discolored hands and feet — newly developed coldness and colour change (purple/blue) with blanching
Erectile dysfunction — age <60 years for Probable MSA; any age for Established
Persistent inability to achieve or maintain erection. For Clinically Established MSA: any age, but must be associated with ≥1 more supportive clinical feature (not isolated). For Clinically Probable MSA: only counts if manifesting before age 60 AND associated with another supportive feature. Not valid as an isolated supportive feature for Probable MSA.
Pathologic laughter or crying — emotional incontinence

Step 6 · Brain MRI Markers
Required for Clinically Established MSA only. Not required for Probable. Each affected brain region (atrophy or increased diffusivity) counts as one MRI marker. At least one is needed for Established MSA.
MRI markers are subtype-specific. Select subtype in Step 2. Analysis (structural MRI 1.5T or 3.0T) is based on visual inspection by a neuroradiologist explicitly advised by a movement disorder specialist. Diffusion MRI analysis is based on quantitative assessments. The putaminal rim sign is omitted from MDS MSA criteria (common in healthy subjects at 3T; limited differential diagnostic potential vs. PSP).
MRI Markers for MSA-P (at least one required for Established)
Putamen atrophy (+ signal decrease on iron-sensitive sequences)
Middle cerebellar peduncle (MCP) atrophy
MCP atrophy on T2-weighted images. MCP-sign (hyperintensity in the MCP) may accompany atrophy. Increased diffusivity of MCP combined with normal superior cerebellar peduncle diffusivity is useful for separating MSA from PSP (sensitivity 91%, specificity 84%).
Pons atrophy
Cerebellar atrophy
"Hot cross bun" sign — cruciform T2 hyperintensity in pons
Cruciform hyperintensity in the pons on T2 images. May also be seen in non-degenerative parkinsonism and SCA2/3 — not pathognomonic, but characteristic in the MSA clinical context.
Increased diffusivity of putamen
Diffuse hyperintensity in posterior putamen on diffusion imaging. Sensitivity 90%, specificity 93% for MSA vs. PD. May be visible early — one case showed putaminal diffusivity changes 6 months after levodopa-responsive parkinsonism onset, 24 months before probable MSA diagnosis.
Increased diffusivity of middle cerebellar peduncle (MCP)
MRI Markers for MSA-C (at least one required for Established)
Putamen atrophy (+ signal decrease on iron-sensitive sequences)
Infratentorial atrophy — pons AND middle cerebellar peduncle
Atrophy of infratentorial structures including pons and middle cerebellar peduncle. Note: isolated cerebellar atrophy alone does NOT support Clinically Established MSA-C due to overlaps with sporadic and genetic disorders (paraneoplastic ataxia, genetic ataxia). Must include pontine or MCP involvement.
"Hot cross bun" sign
Increased diffusivity of putamen

ⓘ Increased diffusivity of MCP alone (without putaminal changes) and isolated cerebellar atrophy do NOT support Established MSA-C diagnosis due to overlap with sporadic/genetic cerebellar disorders.

ⓘ Brain MRI markers are not required for Clinically Probable MSA. If clinical features of Established MSA are met but MRI markers are absent, classify as Clinically Probable MSA. Biomarkers should not substitute for absent MRI in Established category.

Step 7 · Exclusion Criteria
All exclusion criteria must be absent for any MSA diagnosis. Note: some features (anosmia, levodopa responsiveness) may occasionally manifest in MSA, but are excluded to secure diagnostic specificity.
Substantial and persistent beneficial response to dopaminergic medications
As judged by a movement disorder specialist. A robust, sustained levodopa response maintained over time excludes MSA in Established and Probable categories. Note: this exclusion does not apply to Possible Prodromal MSA (patients may have subtle parkinsonian signs not yet requiring dopaminergic treatment). Initial partial levodopa response occurs in ~43–57% of MSA-P and 13–25% of MSA-C.
Unexplained anosmia on olfactory testing
Anosmia not explained by allergic rhinitis, smoking, nasal structural lesions, or nasal surgery. Hyposmia is common in Parkinson's disease and Lewy body disorders (>90% of PD) but atypical in MSA. Anosmia points strongly toward Lewy body pathology. For Possible Prodromal MSA: anosmia is an exclusion criterion specifically to exclude Lewy body disease as the phenoconversion target.
Fluctuating cognition with pronounced variation in attention and alertness and early decline in visuoperceptual abilities (suggestive of DLB)
Recurrent visual hallucinations not induced by drugs within 3 years of disease onset
Dementia (per DSM-V) within 3 years of disease onset
Early dementia (within 3 years of onset) is atypical for MSA and suggests Lewy body disease (DLB), PSP, or other causes. Mild cognitive impairment can occur in MSA but frank dementia this early is an exclusion criterion.
Downgaze supranuclear palsy or slowing of vertical saccades
Downgaze supranuclear gaze palsy or selective slowing of vertical saccades is pathognomonic for PSP-Richardson syndrome and excludes MSA. Confirm supranuclear origin by intact VOR (doll's-eye maneuver). Sustained downbeat nystagmus within MSA does not qualify — it is a cerebellar oculomotor feature.
Brain MRI findings suggestive of an alternative diagnosis (PSP, MS, vascular parkinsonism, symptomatic cerebellar disease)
Documentation of an alternative condition (MSA look-alike including genetic or symptomatic ataxia/parkinsonism) known to produce autonomic failure, ataxia, or parkinsonism
MSA look-alikes include: SCA1, SCA2, SCA3, SCA6, SCA7, SCA12, SCA17, FRDA, FXTAS, CANVAS (for cerebellar features). Also: genetic or symptomatic parkinsonism plausibly causing the clinical syndrome. Genetic screening for common SCAs is recommended, particularly in patients with cerebellar features.

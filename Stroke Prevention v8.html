Step 1 · IV Thrombolysis and Endovascular Treatment Status
Whether the patient received IV tenecteplase (TNK) or underwent endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) directly governs when antithrombotic therapy can be safely initiated. This is the first question to answer before any other timing decision.
Why IV TNK status gates all antithrombotic timing:
IV TNK given: Antithrombotic therapy (antiplatelet or anticoagulation) must be withheld for 24 hours after IV tenecteplase (TNK). AHA/ASA guidelines (Class III — Harm) explicitly prohibit initiating any antithrombotic within 24h of TNK due to increased symptomatic ICH risk. After 24h, obtain repeat neuroimaging to rule out hemorrhagic transformation, then initiate per etiology.

No IV TNK (non-disabling minor stroke / TIA): Per POINT trial (Johnston, NEJM 2018) and CHANCE (Wang, NEJM 2013), DAPT with aspirin + clopidogrel can be initiated immediately (within 12 hours) in patients with minor non-disabling stroke (NIHSS ≤3) or high-risk TIA who did not receive TNK. Both trials explicitly excluded patients who received IV thrombolysis — applying immediate DAPT to a post-TNK patient is not supported by trial evidence and violates the 24h safety mandate.

EVT (mechanical thrombectomy) without IV TNK: EVT alone does not mandate a 24-hour antithrombotic hold. However, post-EVT reperfusion hemorrhagic transformation is common (any HT 20–40%; PH-type ~5–10%). Obtain post-EVT CT or MRI at 24 ± 6 hours, classify HT type (Step 3), then initiate antithrombotic per etiology pathway.

IV TNK + EVT (bridging): The 24-hour TNK hold applies. The mandatory 24h post-EVT imaging window aligns with this hold period — use this time to characterize HT before initiating any antithrombotic.
Acute treatment received REQUIRED — select the scenario that applies
Did the patient receive IV tenecteplase (TNK) for this stroke?
AHA/ASA 2019/2021 Acute Stroke Guidelines: Class III recommendation (Harm) — do not administer aspirin or other antithrombotic agents within 24 hours of IV tenecteplase (TNK). This applies to all antithrombotics, not just aspirin.

After the 24h hold: Obtain CT or MRI (GRE/SWI preferred) to evaluate for hemorrhagic transformation. If no PH-1 or PH-2 is present, proceed with antithrombotic initiation per etiology and infarct size. For AF-related stroke, the ELAN early window (≤48h for minor/moderate infarct) remains open after the 24h TNK hold completes.

POINT/CHANCE TNK exclusion: Both trials excluded patients who received thrombolysis. The demonstrated benefit of immediate DAPT applies only to thrombolysis-naive patients. This is not a minor technicality — early antithrombotic after TNK causes measurable harm.
Ref: Powers WJ et al. AHA/ASA Acute Ischemic Stroke Guidelines. Stroke 2019;50:e344–e418. Johnston SC et al. POINT. N Engl J Med 2018;379:215–225 (TNK exclusion criterion). Wang Y et al. CHANCE. N Engl J Med 2013;369:11–19 (TNK exclusion criterion).
EVT / post-procedure context EXPAND FOR HT RISK AND IMAGING TIMING DETAILS
EVT / intracranial stenting details (see combined selector above) — expand for EVT evidence
Mechanical thrombectomy restores large vessel patency but substantially increases hemorrhagic transformation risk through reperfusion injury. Post-EVT HT rates: any HT ~20–40%, PH-type ~5–10% depending on territory, NIHSS, and TICI reperfusion score.

Post-EVT antithrombotic timing (AF-related stroke): ELAN included patients who had undergone EVT. The same imaging-guided framework applies. Classify HT on 24h post-EVT imaging, then initiate DOAC per ELAN criteria. Do not initiate anticoagulation without post-EVT HT classification.

Residual intraluminal thrombus post-EVT: If residual ILT is identified in an incompletely recanalized segment, inpatient UFH infusion may be considered in consultation with the stroke team — balancing recanalization benefit against hemorrhagic risk.
Post-EVT HT classification uses identical ECASS criteria (HI-1/HI-2/PH-1/PH-2). PH-2 post-EVT carries the same prognosis as spontaneous PH-2: OR 32.3 for early neurological deterioration (Larrue et al., Stroke 1999).
Intracranial stenting CRITICAL — immediate antiplatelet protocol required
Did the patient undergo intracranial stenting (e.g., Wingspan, Neuroform, Enterprise, or flow diverter) for this stroke?
Intracranial stent placement requires immediate and potent antiplatelet therapy to prevent in-stent thrombosis — a catastrophic early complication. Unlike extracranial carotid stenting, intracranial stenting demands more aggressive perioperative antiplatelet protocols due to the high thrombogenic risk of the stent surface in small, tortuous vessels at high flow.

Ticagrelor (P2Y12 inhibitor — preferred for intracranial stenting):
• Loading dose: Ticagrelor 180 mg PO at the time of stent placement (or as soon as patient can swallow post-procedure)
• Maintenance: Ticagrelor 90 mg BID + aspirin 81 mg/day (DAPT). Ticagrelor is preferred over clopidogrel for intracranial stenting due to more predictable pharmacokinetics and greater platelet inhibition — critical given the high-flow intracranial environment.
• Duration: DAPT for at least 3–6 months post-stenting, then reassess based on stent type, vessel patency, and bleeding risk. For elective Wingspan stenting for ICAD, SAMMPRIS-era practice used aspirin + clopidogrel ×90 days; ticagrelor is increasingly preferred in contemporary practice.

Cangrelor (IV P2Y12 inhibitor):
• Cangrelor is an IV P2Y12 inhibitor with immediate onset (<2 min), linear dose response, and rapid offset (~60 min after stopping infusion). It is used when oral antiplatelet loading is not feasible (e.g., patient unable to swallow, severe altered consciousness, emergent stenting before oral medications can be given).
• Typical dose: 0.75 µg/kg/min infusion, adjusted per institutional protocol. Transition to oral ticagrelor or clopidogrel once oral route is available.

Aspirin pre-loading: For planned procedures, aspirin 325 mg should be given 24h before stenting when possible. For emergency procedures, aspirin 325 mg IV or rectal at the time of stent placement.
Refs: Chimowitz MI et al. SAMMPRIS. NEJM 2011 — stenting protocol used aspirin 325 mg + clopidogrel ×90 days. Contemporary intracranial stenting often uses ticagrelor based on extrapolation from coronary stenting pharmacology and higher platelet inhibition reliability. Stenting for stroke in acute setting (RESCUE-Japan, DIRECT-MT) used individualized antiplatelet protocols. Cangrelor: Bhatt DL et al. CHAMPION PHOENIX (coronary). NEJM 2013 — IV P2Y12 with immediate-onset antiplatelet effect.
Step 2 · Infarct Size on Imaging
Infarct size is the primary imaging variable that governs anticoagulation timing per the ELAN trial framework — but only when anticoagulation is the indicated therapy (e.g., known AF, LV thrombus). For antiplatelet-based secondary prevention (ICAD, SVD, cryptogenic stroke), infarct size does not alter the antithrombotic choice, though large infarcts may delay initiation to reduce hemorrhagic transformation risk. Assess the largest single infarct lesion. MRI DWI is the gold standard; CT alone is used when MRI is unavailable or delayed.
ELAN Trial (Fischer et al., NEJM 2023) — Imaging-based size definitions for anticoagulation timing:
Important: The ELAN size thresholds govern the timing of anticoagulation (DOAC/warfarin) and are only relevant when anticoagulation is indicated — e.g., confirmed atrial fibrillation, LV thrombus, or other high-risk cardioembolic source. For patients who will receive antiplatelet therapy (ICAD, SVD, cryptogenic stroke without AF), proceed to the etiology and DAPT timing steps.

The ELAN trial (n=2,013, 103 sites, 15 countries) defined stroke severity by imaging, not NIHSS. Infarct size on DWI determined early vs. delayed anticoagulation timing:
  • Minor (≤1.5 cm): DOAC within 48 hours
  • Moderate (1.5 cm – 1/3 MCA territory): DOAC within 48 hours
  • Major (>1/3 MCA territory or equivalent): DOAC on day 6–7

Key result: Composite outcome (recurrent ischemic stroke, systemic embolism, major extracranial bleeding, symptomatic ICH, vascular death) at 30 days ranged from 2.8 percentage points lower to 0.5 percentage points higher with early vs. later DOAC (95% CI — no formal hypothesis test). Recurrent ischemic stroke: 1.4% early vs. 2.5% later (OR 0.57, 95% CI 0.29–1.07). Symptomatic ICH: 0.2% both arms. Early treatment was thus numerically favorable and not significantly more harmful.

OPTIMAS trial (Werring, Lancet 2024, n=3,621): Used NIHSS-based severity rather than imaging. Early DOAC (≤4 days) was noninferior to delayed (7–14 days): primary outcome 3.3% both arms. PH2 excluded. Convergent evidence with ELAN.

Largest infarct on DWI — or estimated on CT if MRI unavailable
ELAN definitions (imaging-based) — applies when anticoagulation is indicated:
⚠ These timing thresholds apply specifically to anticoagulation (DOAC/warfarin) and are most relevant when AF, LV thrombus, or another cardioembolic source has been identified. For antiplatelet-based secondary prevention, infarct size does not drive the same timing framework.

Minor: DWI lesion ≤1.5 cm in anterior or posterior circulation
Moderate: DWI lesion >1.5 cm but not exceeding 1/3 of MCA territory (or equivalent in posterior circulation). Treated equivalently to minor in ELAN — early DOAC within 48h.
Major: DWI lesion >1/3 of MCA territory; or large posterior circulation infarct. Anticoagulation deferred to day 6–7.

CT-based estimation (when MRI unavailable): Non-contrast CT in the first 24h is insensitive for early infarct detection (sensitivity ~40–60% at 3h). However, ASPECTS (Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score) can estimate territory involvement. ASPECTS ≤7 approximates a large territorial infarct (roughly correlating to >1/3 MCA territory on DWI). If CT shows clear large territory hypoattenuation, classify as major. If CT is normal or shows only subtle findings, classify presumptively as minor/moderate pending MRI. Obtain MRI urgently for definitive sizing.

Why size matters: Large infarcts disrupt the blood-brain barrier more severely, increasing PH-type hemorrhagic transformation risk with early anticoagulation. ELAN JAMA Neurology subanalysis (2024): effect modification by infarct size was not statistically significant, but major strokes remained in the delayed-initiation protocol arm.
Ref: Fischer U et al. ELAN. N Engl J Med 2023;388:2411–2421. Rohner R et al. (ELAN-HT subanalysis). Circulation 2024. Werring DJ et al. OPTIMAS. Lancet 2024;404. Barber PA et al. ASPECTS. Lancet 2000;355:1670–1674.
Infarct count and distribution SUPPLEMENTARY
Number and distribution of DWI lesions
Multiple DWI lesions in different vascular territories (bilateral, anterior + posterior, or multiple discrete cortical lesions) strongly favor a cardioembolic mechanism — the "shower embolism" pattern. This itself does not alter the ELAN-based timing calculation (which applies per the largest lesion), but it raises the urgency of identifying and treating the embolic source. Multiple territory involvement in a young patient without AF should prompt urgent echocardiography and aortic arch evaluation.
Multiple territory DWI involvement: sensitivity 75%, specificity 98% for cardioembolic etiology (Kang et al., Stroke 2003). Bilateral simultaneous cortical DWI lesions are pathognomonic of a proximal embolic source (cardiac or proximal aortic).
Step 3 · Hemorrhagic Transformation
Hemorrhagic transformation (HT) type profoundly alters anticoagulation timing. The ECASS classification (HI1/HI2/PH1/PH2) stratifies HT by radiographic severity and clinical impact. Assess on CT or MRI (GRE/SWI sequences most sensitive).
ECASS Hemorrhagic Transformation Classification:
HI-1: Small petechiae along the margins of the infarct — no mass effect
HI-2: Confluent petechiae throughout the infarct zone — no mass effect
PH-1: Homogeneous hematoma ≤30% of infarct area — mild mass effect
PH-2: Homogeneous hematoma >30% of infarct area — significant mass effect

Clinical significance (ECASS-1 data, Larrue et al., Stroke 1999): HI1, HI2, and PH1 did not independently increase early neurological deterioration, death, or disability. PH2 had devastating impact: OR for early deterioration 32.3 (95% CI 13.4–77.7); OR for 3-month death 18.0 (95% CI 8.1–40.1).

ELAN-HT subanalysis (Rohner, Circulation 2024): Outcome in both arms similar in HI. A 25% higher risk of poor 90-day functional outcome was observed in PH + early DOAC vs. late. PH2 was excluded from OPTIMAS.
Is there hemorrhagic transformation on current imaging? REQUIRED
Hemorrhagic transformation present on CT or MRI (GRE/SWI)?
HT occurs in up to 40–65% of cardioembolic strokes. Spontaneous HT (without thrombolysis) is most common in large cortical infarcts, particularly with complete MCA territory involvement. GRE (T2*) and SWI sequences detect microbleeds and petechial hemorrhage with higher sensitivity than standard T2 or CT. Reperfusion after thrombectomy significantly increases HT risk — assess post-procedure imaging carefully.

Timing of HT: HT peaks within 24–72 hours of stroke onset. Serial imaging at 24–72h after large infarcts or thrombectomy is standard practice before anticoagulation decisions.
Ref: Larrue V et al. Hemorrhagic transformation in ECASS-1. Stroke 1999. Paciaroni M et al. Hemorrhagic transformation in AF-associated stroke. JAHA 2018;7:e010133. ELAN-HT (Rohner et al.). Circulation 2024.
HT Classification (ECASS) REQUIRED IF HT PRESENT
Which ECASS hemorrhagic transformation type is present?
HI-1 — Petechial hemorrhage, marginal: Small, scattered hyperdense foci strictly along the infarct border. No confluent blood. No mass effect. Radiographically benign; does not independently worsen outcome. Early DOAC generally supported by observational and trial data (Rohner 2024 ELAN-HT; Takashima 2022).

HI-2 — Petechial hemorrhage, confluent: More extensive petechial hemorrhage throughout the infarct, but still without mass effect. Outcome similar to HI-1. Early anticoagulation can be considered with close monitoring.

PH-1 — Parenchymal hematoma, small (<30%): Dense, homogeneous blood clot occupying ≤30% of infarct zone. Mild mass effect. Anticoagulation should generally be delayed — typically day 7–10 with repeat imaging confirming stability. No independent mortality signal by ECASS criteria but clinical surveillance required.

PH-2 — Parenchymal hematoma, large (>30%): Dense hematoma >30% of infarct with significant mass effect. Strongly associated with early neurological deterioration (OR 32.3) and 3-month death (OR 18.0). Anticoagulation must be significantly delayed — minimum 2–4 weeks with serial neuroimaging. Warfarin/DOAC initiation requires neurology + neurosurgery consensus. OPTIMAS excluded PH2 patients.
Ref: Larrue V et al. ECASS-1 HT classification. Stroke 1999;30(10):1535–1540. Rohner R et al. ELAN-HT subanalysis. Circulation 2024. Takashima Y et al. Timing of DOACs in HT after EVT. J Stroke Cerebrovasc Dis 2022.
Step 4 · Stroke Etiology
The etiology of the ischemic stroke determines which secondary prevention medication is appropriate. Select the primary mechanism established by workup. If multiple coexisting etiologies, select the one most directly causative.
Why etiology determines therapy: Anticoagulation is appropriate for cardioembolic strokes (AF, LV thrombus) but not for non-cardioembolic strokes (SVD, ICAD) where antiplatelet therapy is standard. Misclassification results in either inadequate protection (undertreating AF) or harm (anticoagulating SVD/ICAD without benefit). ARCADIA (JAMA 2024) confirmed that anticoagulation is not beneficial in cryptogenic stroke with atrial cardiopathy alone, reinforcing the need for precise etiology classification.

Intraluminal thrombus (ILT): Intraluminal thrombus identified in a major artery (ICA, MCA M1, basilar, vertebral) is a high-urgency finding. Unlike atherosclerotic stenosis, ILT represents a freely mobile or mural thrombus with extremely high short-term embolic risk. The preferred inpatient treatment is unfractionated heparin (UFH) infusion — UFH allows rapid titration, immediate reversibility, and avoidance of renal dosing concerns. Transition to a DOAC or warfarin at discharge after thrombus stability is confirmed. ILT associated with carotid atherosclerosis (artery-to-artery embolism) has an OR of 103.6 for being the causal lesion (Balu et al., 2014).
Primary ischemic stroke etiology REQUIRED
Established primary etiology based on clinical workup
Cardioembolic (CE): AF/flutter, LV thrombus, low-EF cardiomyopathy, valvular disease (MS, mechanical valve), intracardiac mass. Requires anticoagulation.

Large vessel atherosclerosis / ICAD: ≥50% stenosis ipsilateral to infarct (extracranial) or symptomatic intracranial stenosis 70–99% (ICAD). Antiplatelet + statin ± aggressive medical management (SAMMPRIS protocol).

Small vessel disease (SVD/lacunar): Perforator territory infarct ≤2 cm, no cortical involvement, no cardiac source found. Antiplatelet + risk factor control.

Cryptogenic / ESUS: No source identified after standard workup. Antiplatelet therapy is standard (RE-SPECT ESUS, NAVIGATE ESUS both failed to show benefit for anticoagulation in unselected ESUS). ARCADIA showed apixaban = aspirin in atrial cardiopathy ESUS (HR 1.00, 95% CI 0.64–1.55).

Arterial dissection: Antiplatelet preferred over anticoagulation based on CADISS trial equipoise and lower bleeding risk. Anticoagulation considered if intraluminal thrombus is present at the dissection site.

Carotid web: A shelf-like intraluminal protrusion on the posterior wall of the carotid bulb, representing an intimal variant of fibromuscular dysplasia. It causes stasis-related thrombus formation and downstream embolism — particularly in young patients (especially young Black women) with cryptogenic or ESUS-pattern stroke and no conventional risk factors. Diagnosis by CTA or DSA. Treatment: antiplatelet therapy as initial management, with CEA (webectomy) or CAS for recurrent ischemic events or symptomatic carotid web without other identified source. ESVS 2023 guidelines recommend CEA/CAS for symptomatic patients with no other identified etiology.

Reversible Cerebral Vasoconstriction Syndrome (RCVS): Characterized by recurrent thunderclap headache and reversible multifocal vasoconstriction of cerebral arteries ("string of beads" on angiography). RCVS is a distinct non-atherosclerotic, non-inflammatory vasculopathy. Antiplatelet and anticoagulation therapies are NOT indicated — the mechanism is vasoconstriction, not thromboembolism. Steroids are contraindicated (worsen vasoconstriction). Treatment: calcium channel blockers (verapamil SR or nimodipine), trigger removal, BP management, and supportive care.

Intraluminal arterial thrombus (ILT): High-urgency finding — mobile or mural thrombus in a major artery with very high short-term embolic risk. Inpatient UFH infusion is the preferred initial treatment; allows titration and immediate reversal. ILT on carotid vessel wall MRI: OR 103.6 for carotid-source stroke (Balu et al., Stroke 2014). After imaging stability confirmed, transition to DOAC at discharge.
Ref: Kamel H et al. ARCADIA. JAMA 2024. NAVIGATE ESUS (Hart RG, NEJM 2018). RE-SPECT ESUS (Diener HC, NEJM 2019). CADISS (Markus HS, Lancet Neurol 2015).

Step 5 · Atrial Fibrillation and Cardioembolic Details
If etiology is cardioembolic, characterize the specific source and assess DOAC eligibility. This section is most relevant for AF-related stroke — the mechanism addressed by ELAN, OPTIMAS, TIMING, and the 1-3-6-12 day rule.
AF-related stroke — critical context: AF accounts for ~20% of ischemic strokes. Anticoagulation reduces recurrent stroke by ~60–70% vs. no treatment. DOACs are preferred over warfarin (lower ICH risk, equivalent/superior efficacy). The pivotal question is when to start after acute stroke — too early risks hemorrhagic transformation; too late risks early recurrence (highest in first 2 weeks).
AF type and workup status IF CARDIOEMBOLIC
AF characterization and prior anticoagulation status
Known AF (pre-existing): Was patient already on anticoagulation? If on a DOAC and had a recurrent stroke, assess compliance, DOAC level, and consider switching agent or addressing concurrent risk factors (uncontrolled HTN, etc.).

Newly detected AF: Includes paroxysmal AF detected on telemetry during admission. May be caused by the stroke itself (neurogenic AF) or be the causal arrhythmia. In newly detected paroxysmal AF, standard DOAC initiation timing (ELAN framework) applies.

Pre-existing DOAC non-compliance or breakthrough: If INR/DOAC level subtherapeutic, this does not change the urgency of restarting — it reinforces the importance of ensuring reliable drug delivery. Address adherence barriers proactively.

Hyperacute / Unknown AF status: In very early presentations (first 1–6 hours), the cardiac rhythm workup is often incomplete — telemetry running but no arrhythmia captured yet, prior records unavailable, echocardiography not yet performed. Select "N/A / Unknown" if AF status has not been established. If AF is subsequently confirmed during the admission workup, revisit this step to update the anticoagulation plan.
CHA₂DS₂-VASc ≥2: DOAC indicated for all post-stroke AF patients. All patients with AF who have had a stroke have an automatic score ≥2 from the stroke alone (2 points). Net clinical benefit of treatment is well-established in this group.
LV Thrombus specifics IF LVT ETIOLOGY
Left ventricular thrombus — confirmed on echo or cardiac MRI
LV thrombus most commonly occurs with anterior MI + wall motion abnormality, dilated cardiomyopathy (EF <35%), or Takotsubo cardiomyopathy. The risk of embolism is highest in the first 3 months. After 3 months, LV remodeling incorporates the thrombus into the akinetic wall segment, substantially reducing embolic risk.

Anticoagulant choice for LVT: AHA/ASA 2021 guidelines recommend therapeutic warfarin (VKA) for at least 3 months (strong recommendation, Level B). Evidence for DOACs in LVT is conflicting — observational studies suggest comparable thrombus resolution and embolism rates, but no large RCT has been conducted. DOACs are used off-label in LVT at many centers when warfarin adherence is a concern.

Timing: Start anticoagulation as soon as LVT is confirmed, subject to infarct size and hemorrhagic transformation constraints (Steps 1–2). No specific trial defines the optimal start day — expert consensus suggests within 2–14 days, parallel to the cardioembolic framework.
Ref: AHA/ASA Stroke Prevention Guidelines 2021. OR for embolism reduction with VKA: 0.14 (95% CI 0.04–0.52). Camaj A et al. Left Ventricular Thrombus. JACC 2022;79:2592. Dalia T et al. DOACs vs. warfarin in LVT. Cardiovasc Revasc Med 2022.
DOAC eligibility REQUIRED FOR CE STROKE
Contraindications to DOAC therapy
DOACs (apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran, edoxaban) are preferred over warfarin for NVAF-associated stroke prevention. Assess for absolute/relative contraindications:

DOAC absolute contraindications: Mechanical heart valve, moderate-to-severe mitral stenosis (warfarin required — RE-ALIGN trial showed dabigatran inferior for mechanical valves), severe renal impairment (CrCl <15 mL/min — agent-specific; apixaban has most favorable renal profile), active major bleeding, severe thrombocytopenia (<50k).

Renal dosing thresholds: Apixaban: dose reduction if ≥2 of: age ≥80, weight ≤60 kg, SCr ≥1.5 mg/dL. Rivaroxaban: avoid if CrCl <15. Dabigatran: avoid if CrCl <30. Edoxaban: avoid if CrCl >95 or <15.

For LVT: Warfarin (VKA) is guideline-preferred; DOAC use is off-label but evidence is emerging.
DOAC vs. warfarin for NVAF: 4 large RCTs (RE-LY, ROCKET-AF, ARISTOTLE, ENGAGE-AF) all demonstrated either superiority or noninferiority to warfarin for stroke/SEE with reduced ICH risk. For post-stroke AF patients, DOAC is first-line unless mechanical valve or significant mitral stenosis.
Step 6 · Intracranial Atherosclerotic Disease (ICAD) and Large Vessel Atherosclerosis
SAMMPRIS established that aggressive medical management is superior to stenting for symptomatic ICAD (70–99% stenosis). This step defines the appropriate antiplatelet regimen and targets.
SAMMPRIS trial (Chimowitz, NEJM 2011, n=451): Randomized patients with TIA/minor stroke within 30 days attributed to 70–99% intracranial stenosis to: (A) Aggressive Medical Management (AMM) alone vs. (B) AMM + Wingspan stenting (PTAS).

Trial stopped early — 30-day rates of stroke or death: 14.7% PTAS vs. 5.8% AMM (p=0.002). 1-year rates: 20.0% PTAS vs. 12.2% AMM. Stenting was inferior to medical therapy alone.

AMM protocol (SAMMPRIS):
  • Aspirin 325 mg/day (indefinite)
  • Clopidogrel 75 mg/day × 90 days (then aspirin monotherapy)
  • Rosuvastatin — target LDL <70 mg/dL
  • SBP target <140 mmHg (<130 if diabetic)
  • Intensive lifestyle modification

Extracranial carotid (≥70% symptomatic): CEA within 2 weeks if surgically eligible (AHA Class I, Level A). Antiplatelet therapy perioperatively. CAS as alternative in high surgical-risk patients.
ICAD / LVD characterization IF LVD OR ICAD ETIOLOGY
Intracranial stenosis degree on CTA/MRA or DSA
ICAD defined as ≥50% stenosis of a major intracranial artery (MCA M1/M2, basilar, intracranial vertebral, intracranial ICA, ACA, PCA). The SAMMPRIS protocol addressed 70–99% stenosis specifically — the highest-risk group. Patients with 50–69% stenosis have lower event rates, but aggressive medical management is still warranted.

WASID trial (2005): 2-year stroke/death rate with aspirin alone in 50–99% symptomatic ICAD was 22.1%. SAMMPRIS AMM achieved 12.2% at 1 year — demonstrating superiority of the intensive regimen.

High-risk plaque features (extracranial carotid) — key point: Carotid revascularization is not exclusively triggered by degree of stenosis. Symptomatic patients with any degree of carotid stenosis who harbor high-risk plaque features on vessel wall MRI or CTA may be candidates for CEA/CAS — and should be maintained on DAPT (aspirin + clopidogrel) as a bridge until revascularization is completed, then transitioned to antiplatelet monotherapy post-procedure. High-risk plaque features include:
• Intraplaque hemorrhage (IPH): OR 25.2 for carotid-source stroke; HR 5.7 for future ipsilateral events (meta-analysis). Detected as T1-hyperintense signal on fat-saturated vessel wall MRI.
• Lipid-rich necrotic core (LRNC): OR 1.30 per unit volume increase for ipsilateral cerebral infarction (CARE-II study, Zhao et al., J Cardiovasc Magn Reson 2019).
• Thin fibrous cap / surface ulceration: High embolic potential; visible on high-resolution vessel wall MRI or CTA.
• Intraluminal thrombus (ILT): OR 103.6 for carotid-source stroke (Balu et al., 2014) — the strongest single imaging predictor of carotid-source ischemic stroke.

The presence of ≥1 high-risk plaque feature should prompt vascular surgery / interventional neuroradiology consultation regardless of degree of stenosis, with DAPT maintained as bridge therapy until revascularization.
Key point on anticoagulation for ICAD: Warfarin (WASID, NEJM 2005) was NOT superior to aspirin and had HR 1.28 for intracranial bleeding. Anticoagulation is not recommended for ICAD unless a concurrent cardioembolic source is present. High-risk plaque features → DAPT + surgical referral, not anticoagulation.

DAPT suitability for ICAD SAMMPRIS PROTOCOL
Contraindications to dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin + clopidogrel)
DAPT (aspirin 325 mg + clopidogrel 75 mg) × 90 days is the SAMMPRIS-established regimen for severe symptomatic ICAD (70–99%). After 90 days, transition to aspirin monotherapy is standard based on the trial protocol. Prolonged DAPT beyond 90 days has not been shown to provide incremental benefit in ICAD and increases bleeding risk (SPS3, MATCH trials data).

Clopidogrel CYP2C19 loss-of-function alleles: CHANCE-2 trial (N Engl J Med 2021) demonstrated that in CYP2C19 LOF carriers, ticagrelor + aspirin was superior to clopidogrel + aspirin (HR 0.77, 95% CI 0.64–0.94). Genotype-guided DAPT is emerging practice, particularly in Asian populations with high LOF carrier frequency.
Aspirin dose: SAMMPRIS used 325 mg. For non-ICAD non-cardioembolic strokes, aspirin 81 mg is equivalent per ESPS-2/ESPRIT meta-analysis. For ICAD specifically, 325 mg aligns with the evidence base.
Step 7 · Non-Cardioembolic Stroke — Antiplatelet Strategy
For non-cardioembolic ischemic stroke (SVD, cryptogenic/ESUS without AF, non-severe LVD), antiplatelet therapy is the standard of secondary prevention. Select the appropriate regimen based on stroke severity and timing.
CHANCE trial (Wang, NEJM 2013, n=5,170, China): Minor stroke (NIHSS ≤3) or high-risk TIA randomized to clopidogrel + aspirin × 21 days then clopidogrel alone, vs. aspirin alone. Primary outcome (any stroke at 90 days): 8.2% DAPT vs. 11.7% aspirin (HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.57–0.81). No significant difference in hemorrhage.

POINT trial (Johnston, NEJM 2018, n=4,881, international): Same population, DAPT × 90 days vs. aspirin. Major ischemic events: 5.0% DAPT vs. 6.5% aspirin (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.59–0.95). Major hemorrhage: 0.9% DAPT vs. 0.4% (HR 2.32, p=0.02). Pooled analysis (CHANCE+POINT): benefit confined to first 21 days — DAPT beyond 21 days increases bleeding without added stroke reduction.

CHANCE-2 (2021): In CYP2C19 LOF carriers, ticagrelor+aspirin significantly superior to clopidogrel+aspirin (HR 0.77). Genetic testing guides optimal DAPT choice.

ARCADIA (JAMA 2024, n=1,015): Cryptogenic stroke with atrial cardiopathy — apixaban vs. aspirin. Annualized recurrent stroke: 4.4% both arms (HR 1.00, 95% CI 0.64–1.55). Anticoagulation showed no benefit over aspirin in this ESUS population. Antiplatelet therapy remains standard for ESUS.
Stroke severity for antiplatelet timing REQUIRED FOR NON-CE STROKE
NIHSS score at presentation
CHANCE and POINT enrolled patients with minor stroke (NIHSS ≤3) or high-risk TIA (ABCD2 ≥4 or DWI positive). DAPT benefit in these trials was strongest in the first 21 days. For moderate-to-severe non-cardioembolic stroke (NIHSS >3), antiplatelet therapy is still initiated but DAPT duration benefit is less well defined.

For NIHSS ≤3 (minor stroke) or TIA: DAPT (aspirin + clopidogrel) × 21 days is supported by CHANCE/POINT (Class I, Level A in AHA/ASA 2021 guidelines). Then transition to antiplatelet monotherapy.

For NIHSS 4–15 (moderate stroke): Antiplatelet monotherapy (aspirin or clopidogrel) is standard. Some clinicians extend DAPT based on individual risk/benefit. Aspirin 325 mg loading then 81 mg/day if no ICAD context.
Pooled CHANCE + POINT analysis (Johnston et al., JAMA Neurol 2019): clopidogrel-aspirin vs. aspirin — major ischemic events 5.3% vs. 7.0% (HR 0.74, 95% CI 0.62–0.89). Benefit confined to first 21 days; benefit-to-harm ratio turns unfavorable after 21 days of DAPT.
D-dimer level IMPORTANT — cancer-associated hypercoagulability
D-dimer level at admission (especially relevant in cryptogenic stroke)
D-dimer is a fibrin degradation product reflecting ongoing coagulation and fibrinolysis. In cryptogenic stroke, markedly elevated D-dimer is a strong signal for cancer-associated hypercoagulability and is the primary biomarker guiding treatment escalation to LMWH.

D-dimer units — US hospital standard: Most US hospital laboratories report D-dimer in ng/mL FEU (fibrinogen equivalent units) or equivalently µg/L FEU. Normal is ≤500 ng/mL FEU. Some labs report in mg/L (divide ng/mL by 1,000) or µg/mL (same as mg/L). All thresholds below use ng/mL FEU.

Key D-dimer thresholds — Choi et al. 2021 ESUS-specific evidence (primary reference):
Choi KH et al. Stroke 2021;52:2292–2301 (n=1,431 ESUS patients, single-center, retrospective): Evaluated D-dimer as a predictor of 1-year recurrent stroke in ESUS. The primary analysis used the normal range threshold of 0.50 mg/L (500 ng/mL FEU) and a secondary sensitivity analysis cutoff of 1.0 mg/L (1,000 ng/mL FEU). High D-dimer (≥1,000 ng/mL FEU) was independently associated with recurrent stroke (adjusted HR per the median split analysis). Patients with elevated D-dimer and recurrent stroke were more likely to have a cancer-associated or hypercoagulable etiology, and the recurrent strokes in this subgroup were more often cryptogenic/cardioembolic in pattern.

• Normal: ≤500 ng/mL FEU — below clinical threshold (0.50 mg/L), not suggestive of hypercoagulable state
• Elevated — low range (500–1,000 ng/mL FEU): Above upper limit of normal. Warrants clinical context interpretation — age, recent surgery, infection, VTE, malignancy. Not sufficient alone to change antithrombotic strategy.
• Elevated — high range (≥1,000 ng/mL FEU, i.e., ≥1.0 mg/L): Choi 2021 ESUS cutoff for significantly elevated D-dimer. Independently associated with recurrent stroke in ESUS (1-year follow-up). In this range, consider hypercoagulable workup, occult malignancy screening, and LMWH if cancer-associated pattern is confirmed. This is distinct from the OASIS-CANCER (>3,000 ng/mL) cutoff — Choi 2021 is specifically ESUS-derived.
• Cancer-associated range (≥3,000 ng/mL FEU): OASIS-CANCER study threshold for cancer-related hypercoagulability correction target (<3,000 ng/mL FEU post-LMWH). At this level, particularly with multi-territory DWI and no conventional source, cancer-associated stroke is highly probable.
• Markedly elevated (≥5,500 ng/mL FEU): Guo et al. (2014) — specificity 99.7%, PPV 92.9% for cancer-associated stroke when combined with multi-territory DWI. Independent predictor of poor survival (HR 2.01, 95% CI 1.26–3.21).
• Severely elevated (≥10,000 ng/mL FEU): Post-treatment D-dimer ≥10,000 ng/mL (despite anticoagulation): independently associated with recurrent stroke and mortality (Nakajima, Front Neurol 2022). Enoxaparin reduced D-dimer from 17,420 → 3,880 ng/mL; warfarin had no effect (Heo 2015).

Why LMWH over DOAC for cancer-associated stroke: Cancer-associated hypercoagulability is driven by tissue factor and prothrombinase complexes that are partially resistant to factor Xa inhibition by DOACs at standard doses, but reliably suppressed by weight-based LMWH. Enoxaparin 1 mg/kg BID (or dalteparin 200 IU/kg once daily) is the preferred agent. Warfarin is largely ineffective — D-dimer does not normalize with warfarin in cancer-associated stroke (Heo et al., 2015).

Clinical pattern suggesting cancer-associated stroke: Markedly elevated D-dimer + multi-territory DWI infarction + no conventional cardioembolic source + active or newly diagnosed malignancy. Urgent oncologic evaluation is warranted.
Primary ESUS reference: Choi KH et al. D-dimer as predictor of recurrent stroke in ESUS. Stroke 2021;52:2292–2301. Cutoffs: 500 ng/mL (normal) and 1,000 ng/mL FEU (high-risk ESUS threshold). Cancer-specific refs: OASIS-CANCER (Bang OY et al.) — cutoff >3,000 ng/mL FEU; LMWH target <3,000 ng/mL FEU. Nakajima S et al. Post-treatment D-dimer ≥10,000 ng/mL FEU. Front Neurol 2022. Heo JH et al. Enoxaparin vs. warfarin. J Stroke Cerebrovasc Dis 2015. Guo YZ et al. D-dimer ≥5,500 ng/mL FEU + multi-territory: specificity 99.7%. 2014.
Cryptogenic / ESUS specifics IF CRYPTOGENIC ETIOLOGY
Atrial cardiopathy markers (ARCADIA biomarkers) and PFO status
ARCADIA biomarkers for atrial cardiopathy: P-wave terminal force >5,000 µV×ms in ECG lead V1, NT-proBNP >250 pg/mL, left atrial diameter index ≥3 cm/m². Despite biologic plausibility, apixaban did not reduce recurrent stroke vs. aspirin in ARCADIA patients with ≥1 marker (HR 1.00) — anticoagulation is not supported by evidence for this population.

PFO closure: Multiple RCTs (RESPECT, CLOSE, REDUCE, DEFENSE-PFO) demonstrated PFO closure + antiplatelet superior to antiplatelet alone in patients aged ≤60 with cryptogenic stroke and PFO, particularly large shunts. Meta-analysis: HR 0.32 (95% CI 0.13–0.82) for recurrent stroke with closure. Moderate-to-large shunt (RoPE score ≥7 favors PFO as the cause) — refer for PFO closure evaluation.

Prolonged cardiac monitoring for occult AF: CRYSTAL-AF (2014) showed implantable loop recorder detected AF in 12.4% at 30 months vs. 2.0% with standard monitoring in cryptogenic stroke. Insertable cardiac monitor for ≥6 months is recommended in ESUS with no alternative etiology.
Ref: ARCADIA (Kamel H et al., JAMA 2024). RESPECT (Carroll JD, NEJM 2017). REDUCE (Søndergaard L, NEJM 2017). CRYSTAL-AF (Sanna T, NEJM 2014;370:2478). Meta-analysis PFO closure: HR 0.32 for recurrent stroke (Vaduganathan M, Eur Heart J 2018).
Step 8 · Vascular Risk Factor Targets
Regardless of etiology, aggressive vascular risk factor modification is integral to secondary stroke prevention. These targets should be initiated during the hospitalization and communicated at discharge.
Key modifiable targets (evidence-based):
LDL cholesterol: Target <70 mg/dL for all atherosclerotic strokes (SAMMPRIS, SPARCL trial — atorvastatin 80 mg reduced recurrent stroke by 16%, RRR, HR 0.84). For ICAD specifically, LDL <70 mg/dL is mandatory per SAMMPRIS protocol. High-intensity statin (atorvastatin 40–80 mg or rosuvastatin 20–40 mg) is first-line.

Blood pressure: SBP target <130 mmHg (AHA/ASA 2021, Class I, Level A based on SPS3 trial data and multiple RCTs). In acute stroke, BP lowering is deferred (permissive hypertension) unless >220/110 mmHg or >185/110 if TNK candidate. After 24h, gradual BP lowering initiated per target. For ICAD: SBP <140 (<130 if diabetic) per SAMMPRIS.

Glucose: Intensive glycemic control does not reduce stroke recurrence but normoglycemia (avoid hypoglycemia, which worsens infarct) is important acutely. Long-term HbA1c target <7% (ADA).
Statin initiation or continuation ALL ATHEROSCLEROTIC / CRYPTOGENIC
High-intensity statin — atorvastatin 40–80 mg or rosuvastatin 20–40 mg
SPARCL trial (Amarenco, NEJM 2006, n=4,731): Atorvastatin 80 mg vs. placebo in patients with recent stroke/TIA (1–6 months) and no known CAD. Fatal/nonfatal stroke: 11.2% atorvastatin vs. 13.1% placebo (HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.71–0.99, ARR 2.2%). A small excess in hemorrhagic stroke was observed in the statin arm (HR 1.66), but this did not offset the ischemic benefit. Net benefit was clear for ischemic stroke prevention.

Statin for all ischemic stroke: High-intensity statin is recommended for all ischemic stroke patients regardless of baseline LDL (Class I, Level A). Even for cardioembolic/AF strokes, statin is appropriate given its pleiotropic anti-inflammatory and plaque-stabilizing effects.
Avoid very high-intensity statin in patients with recent hemorrhagic stroke or large PH-type hemorrhagic transformation (small excess ICH signal in SPARCL). For LDL already <70 on statin, continue current therapy. If LDL >70 on maximum-tolerated statin, add ezetimibe (IMPROVE-IT) or PCSK9 inhibitor (ODYSSEY OUTCOMES).

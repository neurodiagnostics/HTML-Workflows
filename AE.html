Educational Use Only This tool is for clinical education and training only. It does not replace expert clinician assessment or comprehensive neurological evaluation. All diagnostic and treatment decisions must be made by qualified clinicians.
Step 1 · Possible Autoimmune Encephalitis (Panel 1)
All three criteria must be present to qualify as possible AE and proceed with this diagnostic pathway. Active exclusion of alternative diagnoses — especially infectious encephalitis — is mandatory before immunotherapy.
Why this threshold exists: Prior encephalitis criteria assumed infectious aetiology and required fever, CSF pleocytosis, and altered consciousness — features frequently absent in autoimmune encephalitis. Panel 1 (Graus 2016) lowers the entry threshold to ensure early recognition. The most critical mimic to exclude is HSV encephalitis — CSF PCR can be falsely negative within 24 h. Repeat PCR if clinical suspicion persists. Primary CNS lymphoma can mimic AE and may transiently respond to steroids.
A · Onset & Core Presentation REQUIRED
Subacute onset (<3 months) of working memory deficit, altered mental status, or psychiatric symptoms
Working memory deficit — inability to form new long-term memories (hippocampal dysfunction), or problems with temporary storage/manipulation of information. Altered mental status — decreased or altered level of consciousness, lethargy, or personality change. Psychiatric symptoms — new-onset psychosis, agitation, affective changes, or behavioural disturbance without prior psychiatric history. Autoimmune encephalitis can present with purely psychiatric features without fever or impaired consciousness — a pattern that does not meet traditional infectious encephalitis criteria.
In children younger than 5 years, syndromic recognition is more difficult and these criteria should be applied with particular caution. Paediatric AE is more antibody-dependent for definitive diagnosis (Armangue et al. J Child Neurol 2012).
B · Supporting Feature (≥1 required) REQUIRED
New focal CNS findings, unexplained seizures, CSF pleocytosis (>5 WBC/mm³), or MRI suggestive of encephalitis
At least one of the following must be present:

• New focal CNS findings — deficits, brainstem signs, movement disorder, dysautonomia
• Seizures not explained by a previously known seizure disorder
• CSF pleocytosis — WBC >5 cells/mm³ (note: absent in 59% of LGI1 antibody LE — absence does not exclude AE)
• MRI features suggestive of encephalitis — T2/FLAIR hyperintensity in one or both medial temporal lobes, or multifocal grey/white matter signal compatible with demyelination or inflammation
EEG alone is rarely specific but supports the diagnosis — particularly temporal slow-wave activity in limbic encephalitis, or extreme delta brush (highly specific for NMDAR encephalitis). Actively exclude subclinical seizures and non-convulsive status epilepticus.
C · Exclusion of Alternative Diagnoses REQUIRED
Alternative causes reasonably excluded (infectious, structural, metabolic, toxic, prion)
The following should be evaluated before initiating immunotherapy:

• Infectious: HSV encephalitis (CSF PCR — repeat if negative within 24 h and suspicion remains high), VZV, EBV, HHV-6 (especially in immunocompromised), bacterial meningitis, neurosyphilis, HIV, progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy
• Neoplastic: Primary CNS lymphoma (can respond transiently to steroids — brain biopsy if atypical or treatment-refractory), leptomeningeal carcinomatosis
• Metabolic/toxic: Wernicke encephalopathy (thiamine empirically if at risk), hepatic encephalopathy, uraemia, drug toxicity (lithium, metronidazole, cefepime), hyponatraemia
• Prion disease: CJD — DWI cortical ribboning/striatal signal; 14-3-3 protein; RT-QuIC on CSF; no fever
Primary CNS lymphoma is a critical mimic — it can present with subacute encephalopathy, show MRI lesions, and transiently respond to corticosteroids (tumour lysis). Steroid response alone does not confirm AE. Brain biopsy may be required in ambiguous cases.
Step 2 · Syndrome Recognition
Certain clinical syndromes allow classification as probable or definite AE on clinical grounds before antibody results are available. Identify the best-fitting syndrome to direct the evidence pathway. Assess all four syndromes — features may overlap.
Algorithm principle (Figure 1, Graus 2016): Limbic encephalitis, anti-NMDAR encephalitis, ADEM, and Bickerstaff's brainstem encephalitis each have panel criteria allowing probable/definite classification using clinical features, MRI, and CSF alone. If none fit, the patient remains possible AE pending antibody results or Panel 7 criteria. Syndrome recognition should prompt empirical immunotherapy if clinical suspicion is high — early treatment improves outcome (Byrne et al. 2015; Titulaer et al. 2013).
A · Limbic Encephalitis (Panel 2) SYNDROME-BASED
Subacute onset (<3 months) of working memory deficit, seizures, or psychiatric symptoms suggesting limbic involvement
The hallmark of limbic encephalitis is subacute short-term memory loss — often overlooked when behavioural or seizure symptoms dominate. Rapid confusion, mood change, and temporal lobe seizures are characteristic. CSF shows lymphocytic pleocytosis in 60–80% of patients and elevated IgG index or OCBs in ~50%. Notable exception: LGI1 antibody LE has CSF pleocytosis in only 41% and elevated protein in only 47% (Jarius et al. J Neurol Sci 2008) — normal CSF should not redirect the diagnosis.
Syndrome clues by antibody: LGI1 — faciobrachial dystonic seizures (pathognomonic, may precede LE), hyponatraemia (SIADH), responds well to steroids. GABA-B — refractory seizures, ~50% paraneoplastic (SCLC). AMPA — ~65% paraneoplastic (thymoma/SCLC). Hu/Ma2 — paraneoplastic in >95%, near-universal cancer association.
Brain MRI: bilateral T2/FLAIR signal highly restricted to medial temporal lobes (or FDG-PET equivalent)
Bilateral medial temporal T2/FLAIR hyperintensity is required for definite LE without antibody data. Unilateral involvement alone requires antibody confirmation. ¹⁸F-FDG-PET may substitute for MRI — studies from 2008–2015 suggest PET is more sensitive than MRI for demonstrating medial temporal involvement in early disease (Baumgartner et al. J Neurol 2013).
MRI mimics of LE: (1) HHV-6 encephalitis in immunocompromised — can be radiologically identical; clinical context distinguishes. (2) HSV encephalitis — less restricted, often haemorrhagic, DWI restriction, contrast enhancement. (3) Low-grade glioma — unilateral, mass effect, no restricted diffusion. (4) Status epilepticus — reversible unilateral T2 signal. (5) Transient epileptic amnesia — may be a presenting feature of early LGI1 LE.
CSF pleocytosis (>5 WBC/mm³) OR EEG: epileptic or slow-wave activity in temporal lobes (≥1 required)
Panel 2 Criterion 3 requires at least one of: CSF pleocytosis >5/mm³, or EEG with epileptic or slow-wave activity involving the temporal lobes. Both can be absent in LGI1 antibody LE — in that case, bilateral MRI plus subacute onset plus antibody positivity allows definite classification. If CSF and EEG are both normal and MRI is bilateral, the case is still probable LE if the onset criterion is met; antibody testing confirms.
If all four Panel 2 criteria are met (onset + bilateral MRI + CSF/EEG + exclusion), definite LE is established even without antibody results — immune-mediated LE can occur without detectable autoantibodies (Graus et al. Neurology 2008; Najjar et al. 2011).
B · Anti-NMDAR Encephalitis (Panel 4) SYNDROME-BASED
Rapid onset (<3 months) of ≥4 of 6 major symptom groups, with ≥1 abnormal investigation
6 major symptom groups (Panel 4):
(1) Abnormal psychiatric behaviour or cognitive dysfunction
(2) Speech dysfunction — pressured speech, verbal reduction, mutism
(3) Seizures
(4) Movement disorder, dyskinesias, rigidity, or abnormal postures
(5) Decreased level of consciousness
(6) Autonomic dysfunction or central hypoventilation

Required investigation (≥1): Abnormal EEG (focal/diffuse slow activity, epileptic activity, or extreme delta brush — highly specific); CSF pleocytosis or oligoclonal bands.
Exception: 3 symptom groups are sufficient if a systemic teratoma is present.
Titulaer et al. Lancet Neurol 2013 (n=577): 95% <45 y; 37% <18 y; F:M=4:1. Tumour frequency: 0–5% in children, 58% in women >18 y (ovarian teratoma), 23% in adults >45 y (carcinoma). By month 1, 87% of patients had ≥4 symptom groups. Post-HSV NMDAR encephalitis: new choreoathetosis (children) or psychiatric relapse (adults) weeks after HSV encephalitis — occurs in ~20% of HSV cases; test CSF for NMDAR Abs.
C · ADEM (Panel 3) SYNDROME-BASED
First multifocal CNS demyelinating event + encephalopathy + large MRI T2 lesions
Panel 3 — 5 criteria (all required for definite ADEM):
(1) First multifocal CNS event of presumed inflammatory demyelinating cause
(2) Encephalopathy not explained by fever
(3) Brain MRI: diffuse, poorly demarcated large (>1–2 cm) T2/FLAIR lesions predominantly in white matter ± deep grey matter (thalamus/BG)
(4) No new clinical or MRI findings after 3 months — cannot be confirmed at onset; treat meeting criteria 1–3+5 as probable ADEM
(5) Reasonable exclusion of alternatives

CSF: mild pleocytosis <50/mm³; OCBs uncommon (<7%). MOG Abs transiently present in ~50% of children with ADEM (Brilot et al. Ann Neurol 2009).
Susac syndrome — critical ADEM mimic: autoimmune microangiopathy of brain + retina + cochlea. Key MRI finding: snowball/hole lesions in central corpus callosum (distinctly different from ADEM periventricular pattern). Simultaneous triad at onset in only 13% of patients (Dörr et al. Nat Rev Neurol 2013; n=304 cases). Requires fluorescein angiography for branch retinal artery occlusions.
D · Bickerstaff's Brainstem Encephalitis (Panel 5) SYNDROME-BASED
Subacute onset (<4 weeks): decreased consciousness + bilateral ophthalmoplegia + ataxia
Probable BBE (Panel 5): Subacute onset <4 weeks of all 3 core features — decreased LOC, bilateral external ophthalmoplegia, ataxia — with alternatives excluded. Often preceded by infectious event. Usually monophasic with good outcome. Additional features: pupillary abnormalities, bilateral facial palsy, Babinski sign, bulbar palsy. CSF pleocytosis in 45%; MRI brainstem T2 abnormalities in 23% (Koga et al. J Neurol Neurosurg Psychiatry 2012; n=53 Japanese patients).

Definite BBE: IgG anti-GQ1b antibodies positive — allows diagnosis even if ophthalmoplegia is incomplete or ataxia is unassessable, or if recovery has already occurred within 12 weeks. Up to 32% of BBE patients are GQ1b-seronegative.
Critical mimics: Listeria rhombencephalitis, EV71 brainstem encephalitis (children), CLIPPERS (chronic lymphocytic inflammation with pontine perivascular enhancement — responds to steroids, recurs on tapering), neurosarcoidosis, primary CNS lymphoma, paraneoplastic brainstem encephalitis.
Step 3 · Ancillary Work-up
Complete regardless of which syndrome is suspected. Paraneoplastic tumour screening is mandatory in all cases. For patients without a clear syndrome, Panel 6 (Hashimoto's) and Panel 7 (antibody-negative probable AE) criteria are applied here.
Paraneoplastic risk stratification: Tumour association varies dramatically by antibody — from >95% with Hu/Ma2 to 0% with MOG/AQP4. Age- and sex-appropriate tumour screening is mandatory: CT chest/abdomen/pelvis and ovarian ultrasound/CT pelvis in women with suspected NMDAR encephalitis. PET-CT has higher sensitivity for occult SCLC and lymphoma than CT alone. If initial imaging is negative and paraneoplastic risk is high, repeat at 3–6 months.
A · Paraneoplastic Tumour Screening MANDATORY ALL CASES
Tumour workup performed (CT chest/abdomen/pelvis ± PET-CT ± ovarian imaging)
Tumour frequencies by antibody (Graus 2016, Table):
• Hu/ANNA1: >95% — SCLC
• Ma2: >95% — testicular seminoma (young men)
• CV2/CRMP5: >95% — SCLC
• AMPA receptor: 65% — thymoma, SCLC, breast
• GABA-B receptor: 50% — SCLC
• CASPR2: 20–50% — thymoma (Morvan's > LE)
• mGluR5: 70% — Hodgkin's lymphoma (Ophelia syndrome)
• NMDAR: varies — 0–5% in children, 58% in women >18 y (ovarian teratoma), 23% in adults >45 y
• LGI1: 5–10% — thymoma
• GABA-A: <5% — thymoma
• MOG / AQP4 / GQ1b: 0%
GAD antibodies in LE: cancer risk is preferentially higher in patients >50 y or with concomitant GABA-B receptor Abs. In GAD-LE, median age is 23 y with predominantly young women; seizures are the main symptom and cancer is uncommon (Ariño et al. JAMA Neurol 2015).
B · Hashimoto's Encephalopathy / SREAT (Panel 6) IF NO CLEAR SYNDROME
Anti-thyroid antibodies (TPO / thyroglobulin) + thyroid disease + encephalopathy, no well-characterised neuronal Abs
Panel 6 — all 6 required: (1) Encephalopathy with seizures, myoclonus, hallucinations, or stroke-like episodes; (2) Subclinical or mild overt thyroid disease (usually hypothyroidism); (3) Normal or non-specific brain MRI; (4) Serum anti-thyroid Abs (TPO or TG); (5) Absence of well-characterised neuronal Abs in serum and CSF; (6) Reasonable exclusion of alternatives.

Critical caveats: Anti-thyroid Abs present in 13% of healthy individuals (27% in white women >60 y) — not diagnostic in isolation. Renamed SREAT in 2006. In a review of 85 patients, 56 had seizures and 66/69 treated with corticosteroids improved — but this outcome is expected by definition (Chong et al. Arch Neurol 2003). Classification remains probable AE given unclear pathophysiology.
α-enolase Abs found in up to 68% of Hashimoto's encephalopathy patients (Yoneda et al. 2007) but also in healthy individuals and other autoimmune disorders — cannot serve as a disease biomarker.
C · Antibody-Negative Probable AE (Panel 7) IF NO SYNDROME AND NO Ab
No well-characterised Abs; ≥2 of: suggestive MRI / CSF inflammation / brain biopsy inflammation
Panel 7 — all 4 required:
(1) Rapid progression <3 months of memory deficit, altered mental status, or psychiatric symptoms
(2) Exclusion of well-defined AE syndromes (LE, BBE, ADEM)
(3) Absence of well-characterised Abs in serum and CSF, AND ≥2 of: (a) MRI abnormalities suggestive of AE; (b) CSF pleocytosis, oligoclonal bands, or elevated IgG index; (c) brain biopsy showing inflammatory infiltrates excluding tumour
(4) Reasonable exclusion of alternatives

59% of LGI1 Ab patients lack CSF pleocytosis — normal routine CSF does not exclude AE. Brain biopsy shows inflammation but cannot establish autoimmune cause alone; detection of CSF Abs against cell-surface antigens (even unknown ones) in a research lab is more informative.
In children: genetic disorders, mitochondrial diseases, and leukodystrophies can present with symmetric MRI lesions, CSF pleocytosis, and steroid responsiveness mimicking AE — maintain broad differential especially in paediatric patients.
Step 4 · Antibody Test Results
Antibody results refine classification from probable to definite, identify immunological subtype, guide tumour surveillance, and predict prognosis. Both CSF and serum should be sent simultaneously — do not wait for serum before testing CSF.
Why CSF is critical (Graus 2016): Up to 14% of confirmed NMDAR encephalitis cases are serum-negative but CSF-positive (Gresa-Arribas et al. Lancet Neurol 2014). Serum-only testing risks false-negatives and false-positives — particularly IgM/IgA NMDAR Abs, found in 10% of patients with unrelated disorders and in healthy controls. VGKC-complex Abs that do not target LGI1 or CASPR2 are not syndrome-specific and should not be used as proof of immune-mediated disease (Klein et al. JAMA Neurol 2013; Huda et al. J Neurol 2015).
A · Specimen Collection BOTH PREFERRED
CSF and serum sent simultaneously for neuronal antibody testing
Send both CSF and serum simultaneously. The strategy of testing serum first and proceeding to CSF only if negative risks delayed diagnosis. The Ab repertoire in CSF and serum can differ in the same patient — CSF Abs generally correlate better with the clinical picture and disease course. For NMDAR encephalitis, CSF Ab titres correlate better with clinical course than serum titres and should guide treatment monitoring.
Cell-based assays (CBA) are required for most cell-surface Abs — ELISA and immunoblot are inadequate because these antibodies recognise conformational (not linear) epitopes. If serum is positive and CSF is negative, or if the clinical picture is discordant with the antibody identified, request confirmatory testing (live neuron immunocytochemistry or brain tissue immunohistochemistry).
B · Cell-Surface / Synaptic IgG Antibody Result HIGHEST DIAGNOSTIC YIELD
IgG antibody against cell-surface, synaptic, or ion-channel antigen (NMDAR, LGI1, CASPR2, GABA-B, GABA-A, AMPA, mGluR5, DPPX, D2R, MOG, AQP4)
Cell-surface antibodies are pathogenic (demonstrated in neuronal cultures and in-vivo models — Moscato et al. Ann Neurol 2014; Planagumà et al. Brain 2015), recognise conformational epitopes (require CBA), and are detectable in CSF of most patients. A positive IgG result in CSF establishes definite AE of the specific subtype.

NMDAR-specific caveats: (1) IgG anti-GluN1 is highly specific — IgM/IgA anti-NMDAR are not; (2) Antibodies against GluN2 (linear epitopes) are found in many disorders with uncertain significance and are not equivalent; (3) Overlapping syndromes: ~4% of NMDAR encephalitis patients develop concurrent MOG or AQP4 demyelinating disease — test for both if atypical features (optic neuritis, myelopathy, prominent demyelination on MRI) are present.
If serum is positive but CSF is negative: request confirmatory testing (live hippocampal neuron ICC or brain IHC). Contact the laboratory before dismissing a serum-only positive result as false-positive — clinical context is essential. A single positive confirmatory test upgrades to definite AE.
C · Onconeuronal / GAD Antibodies PARANEOPLASTIC MARKER
Antibodies against intracellular antigens (Hu, Ma2, CV2/CRMP5, amphiphysin, GAD)
Onconeuronal Abs (Hu, Ma2, CV2, amphiphysin) target intracellular proteins, are detectable by ELISA/immunoblot (linear epitopes), and are associated with cancer in >95% of cases. They are immune markers of the tumour response, not directly pathogenic — LE with onconeuronal Abs is much less responsive to immunotherapy than LE with cell-surface Abs. Treatment of the underlying tumour is the primary intervention.

High-titre GAD Abs: Serum GAD Abs at 100–1000× the level seen in T1DM are associated with limbic encephalitis, stiff person syndrome, and cerebellar ataxia. Low-titre GAD Abs (present in 1% of healthy individuals and 80% of T1DM) have no neurological significance. Specific intrathecal GAD Ab production or CSF OCBs support a neurological association (Saiz et al. Brain 2008).
Hu (ANNA1): >95% SCLC. Ma2: >95% testicular seminoma (young men). Urgent comprehensive tumour search mandatory. Simultaneous oncological treatment and immunotherapy required. Prognosis is significantly worse than for cell-surface Ab AE.
Step 5 · APE2 Score
The APE2 (Antibody Prevalence in Epilepsy and Encephalopathy) score estimates the pre-test probability of a neural antibody being detected. Developed and validated by Dubey et al. Score each feature present at time of initial presentation.
Reference: Dubey D, Pittock SJ, Kelly CR, et al. Autoimmune encephalitis epidemiology and a comparison to infectious encephalitis. Ann Neurol. 2018;83(1):166–177. Prospective cohort (n=226). APE2 ≥4: sensitivity 96%, specificity 84% for predicting a positive neural antibody. The score does not replace Graus criteria — it provides a pre-test probability estimate to support decisions about empirical immunotherapy while awaiting antibody results.

Step 1 · Select Clinical Syndrome
FTD encompasses a clinically heterogeneous group of syndromes united by frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD). The two dominant phenotypic clusters are behavioral (bvFTD) and language (primary progressive aphasia — PPA). Each has distinct criteria, neuroimaging signatures, and pathologic correlates. Select the primary presenting syndrome.
FTLD overview: FTD is the second most common cause of early-onset dementia (onset age 45–65 years) after early-onset AD. Estimated prevalence 15–22 per 100,000 (ages 45–65). FTLD accounts for ~5–17% of autopsy-confirmed dementia cases presenting before age 70.

bvFTD (~50–60% of FTD): Progressive behavioural and personality change with relative preservation of episodic memory. Core behavioral features emerge first.
svPPA (~15–20%): Progressive loss of semantic knowledge (word meaning, object recognition). Fluent, anomic speech; severely impaired single-word comprehension.
nfvPPA (~20–25%): Progressive effortful, non-fluent speech with agrammatism; apraxia of speech; intact comprehension of single words.
lvPPA (~25–30% of PPA): Impaired sentence repetition; anomia; posterior perisylvian/parietal atrophy. Most closely associated with AD pathology — amyloid PET frequently positive.

Step 2 · Core Diagnostic Features
Assess core features for the selected syndrome. Features must show progressive deterioration — not a single static examination finding.
bvFTD progressive deterioration prerequisite (Rascovsky et al. 2011, Brain 134:2456–2477):
Prerequisite A: Shows progressive deterioration of behaviour and/or cognition by observation or history (as provided by a knowledgeable informant). This must be met before any feature count applies.

Diagnostic thresholds:
Possible bvFTD: ≥3 of 6 core features (from F1–F6 below)
Probable bvFTD: Possible bvFTD + significant functional decline + frontal/temporal neuroimaging
Definite FTLD pathology: Possible/probable bvFTD + histopathologic or genetic confirmation

Diagnostic performance (autopsy-confirmed cohort, n=239, Harris et al. Neurology 2013): Possible bvFTD: sensitivity 95%, specificity 82%. Probable bvFTD: sensitivity 85%, specificity 95%. New FTDC criteria improved sensitivity vs. 1998 Neary criteria (86% vs. 53%, Rascovsky 2011 original validation).

F1 · Early behavioural disinhibition (one of: socially inappropriate behaviour, loss of manners/decorum, impulsive/reckless/careless actions)
Disinhibition is the most specific early bvFTD feature when confirmed in an early-onset dementia cohort (specificity ~90% in autopsy series). Examples: making inappropriate comments to strangers, shoplifting, public urination, inappropriate sexual remarks, loss of social tact, reckless financial decisions. In late-onset frontal syndromes the specificity is lower due to overlap with depression, AD, and vascular disease.
Frequency (Rascovsky 2011, n=203 FTLD cases): Behavioural disinhibition present in ~73% at clinical presentation in FTLD-confirmed cohort. Sensitivity of F1 alone: ~65% for possible bvFTD diagnosis.
F2 · Early apathy or inertia (persistent reduction in motivation, spontaneous behaviour, and/or social engagement; not explained by psychiatric illness)
Apathy is the single most common bvFTD feature (~80–90% prevalence). However, it has low specificity — also seen in depression, major psychosis, other dementias, and frontal lesions from any cause. Its value in diagnosis is primarily as one of 3 required features combined with more specific criteria. Distinguished from depression by absence of sustained dysphoria, vegetative symptoms, or response to antidepressants. "Early" = within first 3 years of symptom onset.
Pitfall: Apathy alone should NEVER be used to diagnose bvFTD. The 2025 Piguet et al. (Alzheimer's & Dementia) review recommends adding social cognition impairment as a core criterion given its high discriminating value vs. other conditions.
F3 · Early loss of sympathy or empathy (diminished response to others' needs and feelings, decreased interest in others, decreased social interest/personal warmth)
Loss of empathy and sympathy is among the most specific bvFTD features when present early. In one autopsy-confirmed early-onset cohort, early loss of sympathy/empathy had specificity of ~90%. Manifests as coldness or indifference to family members, failure to respond to distressing events, apparent selfishness, and lack of interest in others. Mediated by right-dominant temporal and orbitofrontal/insular damage. Early loss of empathy is a proposed core feature addition in the evolving 2025 criteria revision (Piguet et al. 2025).
Clinical tip: Ask informants: "Has there been a change in warmth toward family?" "Does s/he respond to others' distress the way they used to?" "Does s/he seem to 'care less' about others?" This feature can be subtle early but informants typically confirm it when asked directly.
F4 · Perseverative, stereotyped, or compulsive/ritualistic behaviour (simple repetitive movements, complex compulsive/ritualistic behaviours, stereotypy of speech)
Stereotypy has high specificity (~85%) for bvFTD in autopsy-confirmed early-onset cohorts. May present as: simple repetitive movements (rubbing, tapping, clapping), complex compulsive rituals (hoarding, counting, ordering), rigid adherence to routines, fixed daily schedules, or repetitive use of the same phrases (verbal stereotypy). These behaviours may initially be mistaken for OCD or anxiety disorders. Stereotyped speech — using the same words or phrases repetitively — is particularly characteristic.
Differential: OCD onset in mid-to-late life without family history is atypical and should prompt FTD workup. The "phenocopy" bvFTD syndrome (static or slowly progressive behavioural change without imaging or pathologic confirmation) often lacks F4 — its absence in the context of F1–F3 should raise doubt about the diagnosis.
F5 · Hyperorality and dietary changes (altered food preferences, overeating, binge eating, oral exploration, food fads, consumption of non-food items)
Orbitofrontal and insular cortex degeneration drives hyperorality. Dietary changes are an early discriminating feature. May include: preference for sweet/carbohydrate foods, overeating formerly disliked foods, eating from others' plates, mouthing non-food objects (pica), excessive alcohol or cigarette use. Marked weight gain may occur. Hyperorality is part of the broader Klüver-Bucy-like syndrome seen in severe bilateral temporal lobe involvement.
Key differentiator from AD: Pronounced hyperorality and dietary change early in the course strongly favors FTD over AD, particularly if episodic memory is relatively preserved. This feature is less common in early AD.
F6 · Neuropsychological profile — executive dysfunction with relative sparing of episodic memory and visuospatial functions (on formal testing)
Typical profile: deficits on frontal/executive tests (Trails B, verbal fluency, go/no-go, phonemic fluency) with relative preservation of episodic memory (delayed recall) and visuospatial function (Rey-O copy, VOSP). Standard cognitive screens (MMSE, MoCA) can be normal or near-normal in early bvFTD. Important caveat (Piguet et al. 2025, Alzheimer's & Dementia): A 2025 multicenter review of 100+ bvFTD cases found that memory and visuospatial deficits are actually common in bvFTD and do not preclude the diagnosis — the authors propose removing "relative sparing of episodic memory" (F2) and "relative sparing of visuospatial skills" (F3) from the core criteria in future revisions. Currently, however, the 2011 criteria remain clinically operative.
Key tests: Frontal Assessment Battery (FAB); FrSBe or Cambridge Behavioural Inventory-Revised (CBI-R) for informant-rated behaviour; Trail Making B; phonemic verbal fluency (FAS). Memory impairment in bvFTD is often a secondary encoding deficit, not a true hippocampal/storage failure — recognition may be preserved despite poor free recall.

Diagnosis requires: Both core features (C1 AND C2) must be present, plus at least 3 of 4 supportive features (S1–S4).
Imaging-supported: Above PLUS expected atrophy pattern on MRI or FDG-PET/SPECT
Pathologic correlate: FTLD-TDP type C in ~80–90% of autopsy-confirmed cases. MAPT tau in minority.
Core features — BOTH required BOTH C1 + C2 REQUIRED
C1 · Impaired confrontation naming (severe anomia — inability to name objects or pictures when shown)
Confrontation naming is the cardinal feature — often the earliest and most prominent deficit. Object naming is severely impaired; the patient cannot provide the name and typically gives a semantic substitute (e.g., "thing you use for cutting" for "scissors") or a circumlocution. Boston Naming Test scores are markedly reduced. Naming errors in svPPA are semantic, not phonemic (no phonemic paraphasias as seen in nfvPPA/lvPPA).
Typical pattern: Severely impaired naming of low-frequency items initially; high-frequency items fail as disease progresses. The patient often says "I know what it is, I just can't say the word" — reflecting preserved visual object recognition with failed word-to-concept mapping.
C2 · Impaired single-word comprehension (inability to understand the meaning of individual words — e.g., "What is a ferret?")
Semantic knowledge loss is the defining feature of svPPA — the patient loses the stored meaning of words and concepts. This is distinct from lvPPA, where single-word comprehension is typically preserved. Tests: Pyramids and Palm Trees (PPT) — severely impaired; WASI vocabulary — impaired; picture-to-word matching for low-frequency items — impaired. Sentence comprehension may be deceptively intact early because the patient understands sentence structure but not lexical content.
Key differentiator from nfvPPA/lvPPA: Both nfvPPA and lvPPA have preserved single-word comprehension. If single-word comprehension is clearly impaired, this points strongly to svPPA (or severely advanced disease in another PPA variant).
Supportive features — ≥3 of 4 required AT LEAST 3/4
S1 · Impaired object knowledge (particularly for low-frequency items) — failure on object knowledge tests
Tests: Pyramids and Palm Trees; picture-to-picture or word-to-picture matching; familiarity judgments. Low-frequency items (e.g., rare animals) fail before high-frequency items. Correlates with anterior temporal lobe degeneration.
S2 · Surface dyslexia or dysgraphia (regularisation errors: reading/spelling words by phonological rules, not by lexical storage)
Surface dyslexia: irregular words (e.g., "yacht," "pint") are mispronounced by regularisation ("yaht", "pynt"). Reflects loss of the lexical reading route as semantic knowledge degrades. Surface dysgraphia similarly produces regularisation errors in spelling. Present in >70% of svPPA cases — a robust supportive feature.
S3 · Spared repetition of words and non-words (repetition is intact — contrast with lvPPA)
S4 · Spared speech production (fluent, grammatically correct speech — no agrammatism, no articulatory distortion)
nfvPPA classification (Gorno-Tempini et al. 2011):
Diagnosis requires: At least one of two core features (C1 OR C2), plus at least 2 of 3 supportive features (S1–S3).
Imaging-supported: Above PLUS left posterior frontal/insula atrophy or hypoperfusion
Pathologic correlate: FTLD-tau in ~70% (PSP- or CBD-type 4R tau most common); FTLD-TDP in ~20%.
Core features — at least 1 of 2 required C1 OR C2 (OR BOTH)
C1 · Agrammatism in language production (omission/substitution of grammatical morphemes; simplified sentence structures; telegraphic speech)
Agrammatism: simplified sentence structure, omission of function words, incorrect verb morphology (e.g., "She go store yesterday"), production of shorter utterances. Important to distinguish from alexia/agraphia. Assessed during spontaneous speech and connected speech samples. Related to left inferior frontal gyrus (Broca's area) and left supplementary motor area degeneration. May co-occur with apraxia of speech.
Assessment tip: Ask the patient to describe a complex scene (e.g., Cookie Theft picture). Look for grammatical simplification, omitted function words, and telegraphic utterances. Preserved sentence comprehension differentiates nfvPPA from svPPA.
C2 · Apraxia of speech (effortful, halting speech; distorted sound substitutions; groping or trial-and-error articulatory movements)
Apraxia of speech (AOS) is a motor programming/planning disorder — distinct from dysarthria (muscular weakness) or aphasia (linguistic). Features: distorted consonant substitutions, articulatory groping, inconsistent errors that worsen with longer/more complex words, slow and effortful rate, prosodic abnormalities, islands of error-free production. Can occur without agrammatism (primary AOS, typically tau pathology — often PSP or CBD). Key clue: the patient struggles with multisyllabic words more than monosyllabic ones.
Key data: AOS is strongly predictive of underlying tau pathology — particularly PSP-tau and CBD-tau. In a Mayo Clinic series, the combination of AOS and agrammatism was associated with tau (PSP or CBD) pathology in ~70% of autopsy cases.
Supportive features — ≥2 of 3 required AT LEAST 2/3
S1 · Impaired comprehension of syntactically complex sentences (difficulty with passive, embedded clauses, or other non-canonical sentence structures)
Single-word comprehension is typically preserved in nfvPPA (this distinguishes it from svPPA). However, complex sentence structures (e.g., "The dog was chased by the cat — who ran away?") are difficult because their processing requires holding grammatical structure in working memory. Tests: CYCLE-R, Northwestern Assessment of Verbs and Sentences (NAVS).
S2 · Spared single-word comprehension (individual word meaning is understood)
S3 · Spared object knowledge (semantic memory for objects intact)
lvPPA classification (Gorno-Tempini et al. 2011):
Diagnosis requires: Both core features (C1 AND C2), plus at least 3 of 4 supportive features (S1–S4).
Imaging-supported: Above PLUS posterior perisylvian and/or parietal atrophy or hypoperfusion
Critical consideration: lvPPA is most commonly caused by AD pathology (~85–90% in autopsy and amyloid PET series). Amyloid PET is frequently positive. FTLD-tau is an uncommon cause. Consider AD biomarker testing.
Core features — BOTH required BOTH C1 + C2 REQUIRED
C1 · Impaired single-word retrieval in spontaneous speech and naming (anomia; struggles to find words in connected speech and on formal naming tests)
Anomia is prominent in all PPA variants but is particularly marked in lvPPA in spontaneous conversation and narrative speech. The patient frequently pauses, uses fillers ("um," "you know"), or circumlocutes. Unlike svPPA, the patient retains knowledge of the word's meaning — they know the concept but cannot access the phonological form. Boston Naming Test is impaired, typically moderately. Phonemic cues often help (distinguishing from svPPA where conceptual knowledge is lost).
C2 · Impaired repetition of sentences and phrases (difficulty repeating multi-word sentences; single-word repetition relatively preserved)
Impaired sentence repetition is the cardinal discriminating feature of lvPPA — distinguishing it from svPPA and nfvPPA. The phonological loop deficit (left posterior perisylvian region) causes breakdown with longer, more complex sentences. Single words and short phrases may be repeated accurately. Western Aphasia Battery (WAB) repetition subtest is typically impaired. Note (Josephs et al. 2024, Neurology): Updated Mayo analysis advocates for "moderate/severe" repetition deficit as a core requirement to tighten pathologic correlation with AD.
AUC for lvPPA + amyloid PET: In meta-analyses, amyloid PET positivity in clinically defined lvPPA ranges from 75–95%, making this the PPA variant most likely to have underlying AD pathology. CSF or plasma p-tau217 testing is clinically reasonable.
Supportive features — ≥3 of 4 required AT LEAST 3/4
S1 · Speech errors in spontaneous speech and naming (phonemic paraphasias — sound-level errors, not articulatory)
Phonemic paraphasias (sound substitutions/additions — e.g., "lampoon" for "lampshade") reflect posterior perisylvian dysfunction. Unlike the articulatory groping of apraxia of speech, these are linguistic errors occurring without visible articulatory struggle.
S2 · Spared single-word comprehension and object knowledge (understanding of individual word meaning is preserved)
S3 · Spared motor speech (no apraxia of speech — speech production is not effortful or distorted at the articulatory level)
S4 · Absence of frank agrammatism (grammatical structure in connected speech and writing is preserved or minimally affected)
Step 3 · Exclusion Criteria
The following criteria, if present, should raise doubt about an FTD diagnosis. They do not necessarily exclude FTD absolutely, but substantially lower diagnostic confidence. For bvFTD specifically, Rascovsky et al. 2011 identifies both mandatory exclusions and features that make the diagnosis highly unlikely.
General FTLD exclusion criteria (apply to bvFTD and all PPA variants):
Mandatory: Symptoms better explained by a non-neurodegenerative or non-psychiatric medical disorder (stroke, tumor, metabolic, inflammatory)
Mandatory: Biomarker evidence definitively showing alternative pathology — e.g., amyloid PET clearly positive in bvFTD (strongly suggests AD behavioral variant or lvPPA)
Relative: Prominent early episodic memory impairment and spatial disorientation in bvFTD (raises AD concern). Note: this is now a relative rather than absolute exclusion in 2011 criteria.
Mandatory exclusion criteria ANY = STRONGLY RECONSIDER
Pattern better explained by another neurological, psychiatric, or medical disorder (e.g., stroke, CNS tumor, encephalitis, severe systemic disease)
Rule out: focal stroke or strategic infarct (frontal, thalamic); CNS tumor (frontal glioma, meningioma, lymphoma); autoimmune/inflammatory encephalitis (VGKC-complex, NMDAR — can mimic bvFTD with behavioral change); severe psychiatric disorder (late-onset mania, schizophrenia, severe depression). Systematic workup: brain MRI with contrast, basic metabolic panel, TSH, B12, RPR/VDRL, HIV, consider autoimmune antibody panel if atypical or rapid.
Psychiatric mimics: Psychiatric disorders (late-onset bipolar, severe depression, OCD) are the most common cause of bvFTD misdiagnosis. The "FTD phenocopy" — patients meeting behavioral criteria without imaging change or FTLD pathology — often represents primary psychiatric disease. Functional decline (CDR-FTD) and imaging change are the critical differentiators.
Biomarker evidence clearly inconsistent with FTD/FTLD (e.g., amyloid PET clearly positive — especially relevant in bvFTD and lvPPA)
A clearly positive amyloid PET in a patient with bvFTD phenotype substantially raises concern for behavioral variant AD (bvAD) rather than FTLD. In lvPPA, amyloid positivity (~85–90%) is the expected finding and is consistent with the diagnosis (lvPPA due to AD). In svPPA and nfvPPA, amyloid positivity is less common but does not absolutely exclude FTLD — co-pathology is possible. CSF p-tau/Aβ42 ratio, plasma p-tau217, and amyloid PET remain important tools for excluding AD as the underlying pathology in FTD syndromes.
FTLD-specific biomarkers under development: Plasma GFAP is elevated in GRN mutation carriers. Plasma progranulin is markedly reduced (~50% of normal) in GRN haploinsufficiency — a useful screening test. TDP-43 biomarkers remain investigational. NfL is elevated in symptomatic FTD vs. primary psychiatric disease but is non-specific across neurodegenerative conditions.
Functional decline assessment (required for Probable bvFTD) PROBABLE REQUIRES THIS
Significant functional decline (by caregiver report or CDR-FTD/FTLD-CDR ≥0.5) OR patient continues to fulfill all professional/personal roles without significant impairment
Functional decline differentiates probable from possible bvFTD and is critical for excluding "phenocopy" cases — patients with behavioral features but no imaging change and no functional decline who often have primary psychiatric disease. Instruments: CDR-FTD (frontotemporal dementia-specific; includes 6 boxes including memory, orientation, judgment, community affairs, home/hobbies, personal care); Functional Activities Questionnaire (FAQ); ADCS-ADL. Even in early disease, subtle functional changes should be documentable by an informant.
Clinical data: In a prospective bvFTD cohort, patients without functional decline and without imaging change at 2 years had a >70% rate of non-neurodegenerative diagnosis on long-term follow-up (phenocopy). Functional change is the strongest single predictor of true FTLD pathology when imaging is equivocal.
Step 4 · Neuroimaging
Neuroimaging supports syndrome classification and is required for "imaging-supported" or "probable" levels of diagnostic certainty. MRI (structural) and FDG-PET (metabolic) provide complementary information. Pattern recognition by an experienced neurologist or neuroradiologist is essential.
FDG-PET diagnostic performance in FTD (pooled data): Sensitivity 47–90%, specificity 68–98% for distinguishing FTD from AD — wide ranges reflect differences in syndrome subtype and clinical context. Hypometabolism typically precedes structural atrophy on MRI by months to years. Sensitivity and specificity both highest in bvFTD vs. AD comparisons using anterior cingulate + frontal hypometabolism patterns.

MRI structural atrophy: Present in ~70% of bvFTD cases; absence does not exclude FTD (especially early disease). Right-predominant or bilateral symmetric frontal/anterior temporal atrophy is typical. Frontal atrophy visible on MRI: ~70% of bvFTD. Symmetric vs. asymmetric pattern varies by underlying pathology.
MRI structural findings REQUIRED FOR PROBABLE bvFTD / IMAGING-SUPPORTED PPA
MRI/CT pattern of atrophy consistent with syndrome
Expected patterns by syndrome:
bvFTD: Bilateral (often asymmetric) frontal and anterior temporal atrophy — orbitofrontal, medial prefrontal, anterior cingulate, anterior insula. May be predominantly right-sided. Posterior regions (parietal, occipital) are relatively spared.
svPPA: Anterior temporal lobe atrophy — bilateral but typically left > right (or right > left). Temporal poles distinctly atrophied. May include amygdala, perirhinal cortex.
nfvPPA: Left posterior frontal and insular atrophy. Left inferior frontal gyrus (Broca's area), supplementary motor area, left anterior insula.
lvPPA: Posterior perisylvian and/or inferior parietal atrophy — left-dominant angular gyrus, supramarginal gyrus, posterior superior temporal gyrus. More posterior than other FTD variants.
MRI tip: Frontotemporal atrophy in bvFTD is typically accompanied by disproportionate white matter involvement. Posterior cortical sparing (intact occipital lobes) is a useful feature distinguishing bvFTD from posterior cortical atrophy (AD variant). If parietal > frontal atrophy, reconsider bvFTD diagnosis.
FDG-PET or SPECT SUPPORTS PROBABLE / ADDS CONFIDENCE
FDG-PET (or SPECT perfusion) — frontal/anterior temporal hypometabolism or hypoperfusion
FDG-PET shows hypometabolism in frontal and anterior temporal cortices with relative sparing of posterior regions (parietal, occipital). In bvFTD: bilateral medial and lateral prefrontal cortex, anterior/mid-cingulate, bilateral insula, and caudate nuclei are most affected. In svPPA: anterior temporal (L > R) hypometabolism predominates. In nfvPPA: left posterior frontal and perisylvian hypometabolism. In lvPPA: posterior perisylvian/inferior parietal (left-dominant) — may closely resemble AD hypometabolism pattern. Hypometabolism may antedate detectable MRI atrophy, making FDG-PET valuable in early disease.
Amyloid PET: Negative amyloid PET strongly supports FTLD (not AD) — useful in bvFTD and nfvPPA where AD pathology is less common. A meta-analysis (2024 AJNR) found pooled FDG-PET sensitivity 0.86 (95% CI 0.80–0.91) and specificity 0.85 (95% CI 0.79–0.90) for FTD vs. other dementias; ASL-MRI performed inferiorly (sensitivity ~0.75).
Amyloid PET (particularly relevant for lvPPA / suspected bvAD) IMPORTANT IN lvPPA AND ATYPICAL bvFTD
Amyloid PET — relevant when AD co-pathology or bvAD is in the differential
Amyloid PET positivity frequencies by syndrome (approximate clinical cohort data):
bvFTD: ~10–15% positive — a positive result strongly suggests behavioral variant AD (bvAD) rather than FTLD
svPPA: ~10–15% positive — usually coincidental co-pathology or misclassification
nfvPPA: ~15–25% positive — co-pathology possible; does not rule out concurrent tau pathology
lvPPA: ~85–90% positive — expected; reflects underlying AD as primary pathology
Key decision point: Amyloid PET negative in any FTD syndrome provides strong support for FTLD-TDP or FTLD-tau as underlying pathology, helping stratify for future disease-modifying trials. Negative amyloid PET in bvFTD can also help exclude the bvAD phenocopy.
Step 5 · Genetic Workup and Family History
FTD is one of the most heritable dementias. A pathogenic monogenic cause is found in approximately 10–40% of patients depending on cohort and family history. Genetic diagnosis enables presymptomatic testing in family members, establishes a definite pathologic diagnosis, and is critical for clinical trial eligibility as targeted therapies develop.
Major genetic causes (North American and European cohorts):
C9orf72 hexanucleotide repeat expansion (GGGGCC): Most common genetic cause of FTD in Western populations. 6–30% of all FTD; ~40% of familial FTD. Accounts for ~50% of genetic bvFTD. Associated with both FTD and ALS (FTD-ALS overlap in ~15% of C9orf72 FTD). Clinical: bvFTD ± ALS; psychiatric features (psychosis, paranoia) common. Pathology: FTLD-TDP type B. Duration averages 6.4 years.
GRN (progranulin) mutation: Haploinsufficiency (loss-of-function). 1–16% of all FTD; ~15% of familial FTD. Pathology: FTLD-TDP type A. Phenotype: heterogeneous — bvFTD, nfvPPA, corticobasal syndrome. Often asymmetric parietal atrophy. Plasma progranulin markedly reduced (~50% normal) — useful inexpensive screen.
MAPT (microtubule-associated protein tau): 3–14% of all FTD; ~10–15% of familial FTD. Pathology: FTLD-tau. Phenotype: heterogeneous — bvFTD, PSP-like, CBS-like, parkinsonism. Typically autosomal dominant with high penetrance. Family history of dementia with parkinsonism is a key clue.
Family history INFORMS PRE-TEST PROBABILITY
Family history of dementia, FTD, ALS, or parkinsonism in first-degree relative
Autosomal dominant family history (parent or sibling with dementia, especially early-onset) is the strongest predictor of a monogenic cause. Key clues in history: dementia + parkinsonism in family (MAPT); dementia + ALS in family (C9orf72); dementia + psychiatric features or psychosis (C9orf72); multiple generations with dementia (any of three major genes). Incomplete penetrance occurs, especially in GRN. Even apparently sporadic FTD can harbor a pathogenic variant — particularly in younger onset (age <60) cases.
GENFI consortium data (n=3,401 mutation carriers): Symptomatic mutation carriers first present with bvFTD in ~74%, language variant in ~16%, and motor presentations in ~10%. Age at onset: C9orf72 median 58y; GRN median 63y; MAPT median 49y. Disease duration: MAPT longest (~9–10y), C9orf72 shortest (~6.4y).
Genetic testing status ENABLES DEFINITE DIAGNOSIS
Genetic panel result — C9orf72, GRN, MAPT (at minimum); TBK1, FUS, TARDBP for extended panel
Standard clinical panel includes: C9orf72 repeat expansion PCR/Southern blot (normal <25 repeats; pathogenic typically >400); GRN sequencing (pathogenic loss-of-function variants; also check plasma progranulin — <125 ng/mL in symptomatic, <90 ng/mL in presymptomatic); MAPT sequencing (exon/intron mutations and splicing variants). Extended panels include TBK1, TARDBP, FUS, VCP, CHCHD10. In a German multicenter cohort (n=509), diagnostic yield of genetic testing was 18.1% overall. Among familial cases, yield is 30–50%. C9orf72 most common (n=47), followed by GRN (n=26), MAPT (n=11), TBK1 (n=5).
When to test: Strong family history, onset age <60, FTD-ALS overlap, psychiatric prodrome, or clinical trial participation. Per current AFTD and EFNS guidance, genetic counseling should precede testing in presymptomatic family members. A positive genetic result upgrades diagnostic certainty to "with definite pathology" (Rascovsky criteria) or "with definite pathology" (Gorno-Tempini criteria).
Motor features — FTD-ALS and FTD-Parkinsonism overlap MODIFIES PATHOLOGIC PREDICTION
Co-existing motor neuron disease (MND/ALS) or parkinsonism (PSP-like, CBS-like features)
Motor features markedly narrow the pathologic differential:
FTD + ALS (MND): Strongly predicts C9orf72 expansion (~50% of FTD-ALS) or TDP-43 pathology (FTLD-TDP). ALS may precede, co-occur, or follow FTD. Upper and lower motor neuron signs on exam — EMG/NCS often required.
FTD + PSP-like parkinsonism (vertical gaze palsy, axial rigidity, falls): Predicts FTLD-tau (4R tau: PSP-tau type). MAPT mutation possible; sporadic 4R-tauopathy also.
FTD + Corticobasal syndrome (CBS: limb apraxia, alien limb, cortical sensory loss, asymmetric rigidity): CBD-tau (4R) most common; also FTLD-TDP, FTLD-FUS. MAPT mutation possible.
FTD-ALS co-occurrence rate: Approximately 15% of FTD patients develop ALS; ~50% of ALS patients show cognitive/behavioral changes meeting FTD criteria. In C9orf72 expansion carriers, concurrent FTD + ALS is seen in ~20% of cases.

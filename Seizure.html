⚠
Educational / Clinical Support Use OnlyThis tool generates a structured semiology-driven localization hypothesis. It does not replace clinician assessment, video-EEG, epilepsy-protocol MRI, or multidisciplinary epilepsy conference localization. No semiology algorithm achieves 100% accuracy.
Step 1 · Is this plausibly a focal epileptic seizure?
Separate focal from generalized-onset, syncope, migraine, parasomnia, and functional events before localizing. Also record handedness — it determines dominant hemisphere mapping throughout the tool.
Core principle: The first ictal symptom (aura) is the most localizing feature — it reflects activation closest to the seizure onset zone. Later dramatic signs reflect propagation, not onset. Always reconstruct the full chronology: prodrome → aura → ictal evolution → postictal state.
Event Pattern Focal confidence
Highly stereotyped, recurrent events
Focal seizures repeat in near-identical sequence because the onset network and propagation are fixed. Ask the witness for the first observable sign and the patient for the first subjective symptom — these are often different and both matter.
Clear focal onset feature before bilateral spread or impaired consciousness
Examples: rising epigastric sensation, déjà vu, unilateral paresthesia, forced head/eye version, unilateral clonic activity, hemifield visual symptoms, speech arrest, laryngeal tightness, or unilateral dystonia appearing before a bilateral tonic-clonic seizure.
Chronology quality: prodrome → aura → ictal evolution → postictal
Prodrome = hours-days of nonspecific mood/sleep/headache changes — not localizing. Aura = the focal aware portion at onset, often the single best localization clue. Ask the patient to describe the very first thing they notice before the seizure takes hold; document verbatim.
Step 2 · Aura — Earliest Subjective Symptom
The aura reflects activation of the symptomatogenic zone closest to the seizure onset zone and is the single most localizing feature in focal epilepsy. Select the earliest symptom the patient reliably reports. Multiple selections are allowed when onset is uncertain, but weight the chronologically first feature most heavily.
A · Mesial Temporal (Limbic) Amygdala / Hippocampus / Entorhinal
Rising epigastric sensation
Classic "rising" feeling from the abdomen up to the chest or throat — generated by amygdalar and hippocampal limbic-autonomic pathways. One of the most reliably localizing auras in focal epilepsy. Can also occur in insular epilepsy; the subsequent ictal evolution (automatisms, postictal confusion) helps distinguish.
Key: Early mesial temporal. If followed by oroalimentary automatisms + ipsilateral hand automatisms + postictal confusion → mesial TLE pattern is very strong.
📍 Mesial temporal
Intense fear / anxiety / sense of impending doom
Unprovoked, intense fear out of proportion to context, generated by amygdalar activation. Can also arise from orbitofrontal, anterior cingulate, or anterior insular cortex. Most localizing when it is the isolated first symptom preceding behavioral arrest.
Key: Isolated fear aura preceding arrest = amygdala/mesial temporal. Fear embedded within hypermotor activity = more likely cingulate or frontal.
📍 Mesial temporal
Déjà vu / jamais vu
Déjà vu (inappropriate familiarity) and jamais vu (unfamiliarity in familiar context) are generated by hippocampus and entorhinal cortex activation. Highly specific for temporal lobe epilepsy — among the most reliable temporal auras.
Key: Déjà vu = very high PPV for temporal lobe epilepsy. Mesial temporal (hippocampal/entorhinal) onset favored.
📍 Mesial temporal
Olfactory aura (smell) or gustatory aura (taste)
Unpleasant smell (burnt rubber, feces) = uncal/uncinate fit — amygdala/uncus mesial temporal. Unpleasant or unusual taste can also arise from insular/opercular cortex. Isolated olfactory aura strongly favors mesial temporal (uncal) onset.
Distinguish: Olfactory (burnt rubber) = mesial temporal/uncal. Gustatory alone (metallic, bitter) = insular/opercular or temporal. Combined olfactory + gustatory = mesial temporal or insula.
📍 Mesial temporal / Uncus
B · Lateral Temporal (Neocortical) STG / Heschl's / Association Cortex
Simple auditory aura — buzzing, ringing, hissing, tones
Simple, undifferentiated sounds (buzzing, ringing, humming) → primary auditory cortex, Heschl's gyrus, superior temporal plane. Typically brief; may be heard contralaterally or bilaterally.
Key: Simple sounds = primary auditory cortex (lateral temporal). Unilateral buzzing contralateral to focus.
📍 Lateral temporal / Heschl's gyrus
Complex auditory aura — voices, music, environmental sounds
Formed auditory hallucinations (familiar voices, songs, tunes) → superior temporal gyrus auditory association cortex. Often non-dominant (right) temporal. Highly localizing to lateral temporal lobe.
Key: Complex hallucinations = lateral temporal association cortex. Musical auras = often non-dominant (right) temporal.
📍 Lateral temporal / STG
Experiential aura — dreamy state, rich flashbacks, déjà vécu
Complex scene-like flashbacks and déjà vécu ("already lived" — richer than déjà vu) arise from extrahippocampal temporal neocortex, especially lateral temporal association areas. Distinguished from simple déjà vu by the richness and scene-completeness of the recalled experience.
Key: Rich, scene-like flashbacks = lateral/neocortical temporal. Simple déjà vu = mesial temporal (hippocampal).
📍 Lateral temporal (neocortical)
C · Insular Cortex Anterior / Posterior Insula — "The Great Mimicker"
Laryngeal constriction / throat tightness ("hand-to-throat" sign)
Sensation of throat closing, choking, or inability to breathe with laryngeal tightening — characteristic of anterior insular cortex activation. Patients often instinctively reach toward their throat (hand-to-throat sign). Can be mistaken for cardiac or respiratory events. Highly specific when prominent and early.
Key: Laryngeal constriction = anterior insula. Distinguish from opercular (lip/tongue involvement) and temporal (epigastric rising). Hand-to-throat sign is pathognomonic when present.
📍 Anterior insula
Autonomic aura — palpitations, flushing, piloerection, respiratory changes
Prominent autonomic symptoms without other temporal features favor anterior insula. Respiratory changes (tachypnea, sighing, irregular breathing) and heart rate increase specifically suggest anterior insular activation. Piloerection alone is less specific (mesial temporal or insular).
Key: Respiratory/HR changes = anterior insula. Combined visceral + laryngeal tightness = strongly insular. Piloerection alone = mesial temporal or insula.
📍 Anterior insula / Mesial temporal
Bilateral or poorly localized paresthesias (non-dermatomal, widespread)
Non-painful paresthesias that are bilateral, widespread, or not conforming to a cortical or dermatomal distribution suggest middle/posterior insular cortex. This is the key distinguishing feature from parietal: parietal paresthesias are strictly contralateral and somatotopic; insular paresthesias are diffuse or bilateral.
Key: Bilateral or poorly localized = insula (middle/posterior). Strictly contralateral + dermatomal = parietal postcentral gyrus.
📍 Middle / Posterior insula
D · Parietal Lobe Postcentral Gyrus / Parietal Operculum
Unilateral, contralateral somatosensory — tingling or numbness, somatotopic
Tingling/numbness that is clearly contralateral and follows a somatotopic distribution per the sensory homunculus (face, hand, arm, leg in characteristic distributions). May show a Jacksonian march pattern along the sensory strip.
Key: Contralateral + dermatomal/somatotopic = postcentral gyrus. Can spread to motor cortex (Jacksonian) or propagate toward temporal lobe (mimicking TLE).
📍 Postcentral gyrus
Ictal pain — somatosensory, sharp or burning, unilateral
Painful ictal sensations (burning, electric shock quality, stabbing) specifically suggest parietal operculum or posterior insular cortex — the pain matrix. Rare but highly localizing when present. Document quality and distribution carefully.
Key: Ictal pain = parietal operculum or posterior insula. Rare; highly specific when present and clearly unilateral.
📍 Parietal operculum / Posterior insula
Ictal vertigo — true rotational dizziness
True rotational vertigo suggests the temporo-parietal junction (posterior insula, parietal operculum, or posterior superior temporal gyrus). Can also arise from posterior cingulate cortex. Distinguish from nonspecific dizziness that accompanies any seizure.
Key: True rotational vertigo = temporo-parietal junction or posterior cingulate. Vague lightheadedness = nonlocalizing.
📍 Temporo-parietal junction
Body image distortion — limb feels enlarged, floating, absent, or alien
Distorted body schema without actual movement (limb feels huge, floating, not attached, rotated) → non-dominant parietal association cortex (angular/supramarginal gyri, superior parietal lobule). Ask specifically — patients rarely volunteer this symptom.
Key: Body illusion/distortion = non-dominant parietal. Useful when ipsilateral to other right-hemisphere lateralizing signs.
📍 Non-dominant parietal
E · Occipital Lobe Primary Visual / Extrastriate / Temporo-Occipital
Elementary visual — colored lights, circles, flickering, scotoma in hemifield
Flashing lights, colored spots, circles, or moving phosphenes strictly in the contralateral visual hemifield → primary occipital cortex (V1/V2). Usually brief (<2 min) and stereotyped. Ictal blindness (positive or negative) also suggests occipital onset. Distinguish from migraine aura by brevity and stereotypy.
Key: Contralateral hemifield + brief (<2 min) + stereotyped = primary occipital. Duration >5 min with slow spread = migraine more likely.
📍 Primary occipital (V1/V2)
Complex visual — formed images, scenes, people, macropsia/micropsia
Formed visual hallucinations (people, animals, familiar scenes) suggest extrastriate cortex and temporo-occipital junction. Macropsia/micropsia (objects larger or smaller than normal) particularly suggest the parieto-occipital junction and prestriate cortex.
Key: Formed images = temporo-occipital association cortex. Macropsia/micropsia specifically = parieto-occipital junction.
📍 Temporo-occipital junction
F · Non-localizing / Other
Cephalic sensation — head pressure, fullness, lightheadedness
Common but low-specificity aura. Can occur in temporal, frontal, parietal, or posterior insular seizures. Only useful in combination with more specific features; alone it does not localize.
Key: Low specificity in isolation. A cephalic "rising" sensation distinct from epigastric may suggest posterior insula.
📍 Low specificity
No reliable aura / unaware of subjective onset
Absence of a reported aura does not exclude focal onset — many patients have no awareness of subjective onset, especially with mesial temporal and frontal seizures. Proceed with motor and postictal features.
Key: No aura does not equal generalized onset. Continue localization using motor and postictal features.
📍 Non-localizing
Step 3 · Observable Ictal Motor Features
Motor features reflect both onset and propagation — weight early features more heavily. Note that ipsilateral hand automatisms + contralateral limb dystonia is the most powerful dual lateralizing motor pattern in focal epilepsy.
A · Temporal Automatisms Mesial Temporal
Early oroalimentary automatisms — lip smacking, chewing, swallowing, licking
Oral automatisms appearing early (<30s from onset) strongly favor hippocampal/mesial temporal onset. Late oral automatisms occurring after bilateral spread are non-localizing — chronological context is critical. Combined with behavioral arrest and impaired awareness = classic mesial TLE triplet.
Key: Early oral automatisms = mesial temporal. Late (post-spread) oral automatisms = non-localizing.
📍 Mesial temporal
Manual automatisms — fumbling, picking, patting (note which hand)
Hand automatisms (fumbling with clothing, repetitive picking, patting) occur ipsilateral to the seizure focus. When the ipsilateral hand shows automatisms while the contralateral limb shows dystonic posturing, this is the strongest dual lateralizing pattern in focal epilepsy. Document which hand carefully.
Best dual lateralizer: Ipsilateral hand automatisms + contralateral dystonic posturing = highly reliable lateralization. Record which hand shows automatisms vs. dystonia.
📍 Temporal — ipsilateral to focus
B · Frontal / Cingulate Motor Features SMA / Orbitofrontal / Premotor / Cingulate
Hypermotor — thrashing, bicycling legs, pelvic thrusting, violent rocking
Brief, explosive hypermotor behavior originating from orbitofrontal, mesial frontal, or anterior cingulate cortex. Often nocturnal, clustered, brief duration with rapid recovery. Anterior insular and anterior cingulate seizures also produce hypermotor activity. Scalp EEG frequently obscured or nonlocalizing.
Key: Brief + nocturnal + hypermotor + rapid recovery = frontal (orbitofrontal/mesial frontal/cingulate). Distinguish from NREM parasomnia by stereotypy and clustering.
📍 Orbitofrontal / Mesial frontal / Anterior cingulate
Asymmetric bilateral tonic posturing — fencing posture (M2e sign)
SMA pattern: contralateral arm extends, ipsilateral arm flexes (fencer's stance). The M2e/fencing posture strongly suggests mesial frontal/SMA involvement. Lateralizes contralateral to the extended arm. SMA seizures are also characteristically brief with preserved or rapidly recovered consciousness.
Lateralizer: Extended arm = contralateral to focus. Flexed arm = ipsilateral. Can reflect SMA spread from temporal/parietal as well as primary SMA onset.
📍 SMA / Mesial frontal
Forced versive head/eye turning — sustained, tonic, late (just before GTCS)
Late forced version (sustained tonic turning occurring immediately before secondary generalization) = contralateral to focus — reliable lateralizer. Early non-forced head turning is exploratory and non-lateralizing. Eye deviation alone with visual aura suggests occipital onset.
Key: Late forced version (pre-GTCS) = contralateral to focus. Early non-forced turning = non-lateralizing. Eye deviation + visual aura = occipital.
📍 Frontal premotor / Frontal eye fields
Ictal vocalization — cry, scream, grunt, moan (non-speech sounds)
Pure vocalization without speech content (cry, grunt, moan, scream) suggests non-dominant frontal or anterior cingulate cortex. Ictal cry at onset of GTCS is common in mesial frontal/SMA epilepsy. Distinguish from ictal speech (verbal content = variable, often non-dominant).
Key: Non-speech vocalization = non-dominant frontal or anterior cingulate. Ictal cry at GTCS onset = often mesial frontal/SMA. Verbal speech during seizure = non-dominant temporal.
📍 Non-dominant frontal / Anterior cingulate
Complex emotional/behavioral — laughter, crying, natural-seeming gestures, emotional expression
Seizures with complex natural-seeming emotional behaviors suggest cingulate cortex. Anterior cingulate: hypermotor, emotional expression (gelastic/dacrystic), vocalization. Posterior cingulate: tonic/dystonic posturing, somatosensory symptoms, preserved awareness. Fluidity and emotional components decrease from anterior to posterior cingulate.
Key: Emotional naturalness + hypermotor = anterior cingulate. Preserved awareness + somatosensory + tonic = posterior cingulate. Both can mimic temporal or frontal epilepsy.
📍 Cingulate cortex (anterior > posterior)
C · Perirolandic / Motor Cortex
Focal clonic activity — Jacksonian march, rhythmic unilateral jerking
Rhythmic clonic jerking of one body part — classically hand or face — that may march along the motor homunculus (hand → arm → face, or foot → leg). Contralateral to the seizure hemisphere. Highly localizing to primary motor cortex or pericentral region.
Key: Contralateral focal clonic = primary motor cortex. March pattern = perirolandic. Face-hand onset = face/hand representation on motor strip.
📍 Primary motor cortex (precentral gyrus)
Unilateral limb dystonia — sustained contorted posture (not rhythmic)
Sustained, twisted, unnatural posture of a limb (not rhythmic clonic) — one of the most reliable lateralizing signs in focal epilepsy. Generated by seizure-induced basal ganglia activation via subcortical spread. Occurs contralateral to the seizure focus. Particularly reliable in temporal lobe epilepsy.
Best lateralizer: Contralateral dystonic posturing = most reliable single motor lateralizing sign in TLE. Combined with ipsilateral automatisms = exceptional localizing value.
📍 Contralateral to focus (basal ganglia activation)
D · Speech / Behavioral Arrest
Ictal speech arrest — sudden inability to speak, Broca-type aphasia
Inability to produce speech during the seizure with preserved comprehension → dominant frontal operculum/Broca's area. Broader receptive aphasia suggests dominant lateral temporal involvement. Record handedness for mapping.
Key: Ictal speech arrest = dominant hemisphere. Most often dominant frontal opercular or dominant temporal.
📍 Dominant hemisphere — frontal operculum
Behavioral arrest / hypomotor — sudden staring, freezing, cessation of activity
Temporal arrest: followed by automatisms, prolonged postictal confusion, amnesia. Frontal arrest: briefer, more abrupt onset/offset, rapid recovery, less postictal confusion — more common in children. Distinguish from absence epilepsy by preceding aura, longer duration, and postictal state.
Key: Arrest + automatisms + prolonged confusion = temporal. Arrest + rapid recovery = frontal. Arrest + 3 Hz spike-wave on EEG = absence.
📍 Temporal or frontal (context-dependent)
Figure-of-4 sign during tonic-clonic phase
One arm extended and the other flexed during the bilateral tonic-clonic phase. The extended arm is contralateral to the seizure focus; the flexed arm is ipsilateral. Moderate PPV; best observed on video at the onset of the tonic phase.
Key: Extended arm = contralateral to focus. Flexed arm = ipsilateral. Best captured on video at tonic phase onset.
📍 During generalization — lateralizes focus
Step 4 · Hemispheric Lateralizing Signs
These signs determine the hemisphere of seizure onset and are the compass for mapping the lobar localization to a side. Contralateral dystonic posturing is the single most reliable lateralizer in temporal lobe epilepsy. Multiple concordant signs substantially increase confidence.
Dominance mapping: Language-dominant = left in ~95% of right-handers and ~70% of left-handers. Handedness was recorded in Step 1. All "dominant hemisphere" findings will be mapped accordingly in the final hypothesis.
Contralateral limb dystonia —
most reliable single lateralizer in TLE
Sustained, contorted posturing of the arm or leg — contralateral to the seizure focus. Generated by basal ganglia activation during propagation. Most reliable in temporal lobe epilepsy; can also occur in frontal and parietal onset with spread.
Ipsilateral manual automatisms (hand same side as focus)
Hand automatisms occur ipsilateral to the seizure focus. Most valuable when combined with contralateral dystonia. Postictal nose wiping with the same hand also lateralizes ipsilaterally.
Unilateral ictal clonic activity — contralateral to focus
Focal clonic jerking is contralateral to the seizure focus. Most reliable when clearly unilateral and appearing early in the seizure. Late unilateral clonic at end of GTCS (see asymmetric clonic ending) has independent lateralizing value.
Forced versive head/eye turn (late, sustained, just before GTCS)
Late forced sustained version (immediately before secondary generalization) = contralateral to the focus. Head/eyes turn away from the side of onset. Early non-forced exploratory turning is not reliable. The turn is away from the focus hemisphere.
Asymmetric clonic ending — last arm to stop clonic jerking
At the end of a bilateral tonic-clonic seizure, the arm that continues clonic jerking longest is ipsilateral to the seizure focus. A reliable lateralizer when clearly observed on video.
Figure-of-4 sign — extended arm is contralateral to focus
Extended arm = contralateral to the seizure focus. Flexed arm = ipsilateral to the focus. Observe at the onset of the tonic phase for maximum reliability.
Ictal speech preservation vs. ictal aphasia/dysphasia
Preserved ictal speech (patient verbalizes words/phrases during the seizure) → non-dominant hemisphere (the dominant hemisphere is uninvolved, so language output is preserved). Ictal or postictal aphasia/dysphasia → dominant hemisphere involvement.
Visual aura strictly confined to one hemifield
Visual symptoms consistently in the same hemifield across events → contralateral occipital hemisphere. Among the most reliable occipital lateralizers and is consistent across ictal events.
Ictal vomiting / ictal urge to urinate / preserved speech during automatisms
Ictal vomiting, urge to urinate during automatisms, and preserved comprehensible speech during temporal automatisms all tend to lateralize to the non-dominant temporal lobe. Less strong individually than dystonia/version but valuable when concordant with others.
Step 5 · Postictal Features
Postictal signs provide localizing and lateralizing information that may be unavailable during the ictus itself. Systematically assess language, limb function, and recovery speed in the first minutes after the seizure ends.
Postictal nose wiping — ipsilateral hand to focus
The hand used to wipe the nose after a seizure lateralizes ipsilateral to the seizure focus — the ipsilateral hand is least affected by the postictal state. Observe within 2 minutes of seizure end.
Key: Ipsilateral hand wipes nose. Reliable lateralizer. Record immediately after GTCS ends.
📍 Ipsilateral to focus
Todd's paralysis — focal postictal weakness
Transient postictal hemiparesis or monoparesis contralateral to the seizure onset — reflects postictal inhibition of the motor cortex. Duration minutes to hours. Highly localizing to motor cortex or areas with strong motor propagation.
Key: Contralateral weakness = seizure hemisphere. Monoparesis → somatotopic localization on motor strip (face/hand/leg area).
📍 Contralateral to focus
Postictal aphasia or dysphasia
Postictal language difficulty (word-finding, fluency, comprehension) lateralizes to the dominant hemisphere. Test within 1–2 minutes of seizure end: ask name, a simple question, and for object naming. Very reliable when clearly present.
Key: Postictal aphasia = dominant hemisphere involvement. Map to left/right per patient's handedness. Highly reliable.
📍 Dominant hemisphere
Postictal cough
Repetitive postictal coughing immediately after the seizure is associated with temporal lobe epilepsy — thought to reflect ictal/postictal activation of insular/temporal cortex affecting the cough reflex. Non-lateralizing but adds to the temporal lobe pattern.
Key: Postictal cough = temporal lobe pattern. Not lateralizing but supportive of temporal profile.
📍 Temporal lobe (non-lateralizing)
Recovery speed
Rapid recovery (alert within 30–60s, minimal confusion) is a hallmark of frontal lobe seizures — particularly SMA, orbitofrontal, and cingulate. Prolonged postictal confusion and amnesia (minutes to hours, fatigue, headache) strongly suggests mesial temporal onset.
Key: Rapid recovery (<1 min) = frontal pattern. Prolonged confusion + amnesia (>5 min) = mesial temporal pattern.
📍 Frontal (rapid) vs. Temporal (prolonged)
Step 6 · Confounders, Mimics, and Confidence
Select plausible mimic diagnoses and rate overall data quality. Mimics reduce the final confidence grade. A plausible-looking localization built from a poor history may be completely incorrect.
Migraine aura mimic plausible
Migraine aura evolves over 5–60 min with slow spreading positive/negative symptoms (often visual). Occipital seizures are brief (<2 min), stereotyped, and may be followed by headache. Key distinction: migrainous aura spreads slowly without headache until after; ictal symptoms are brief and stereotyped.
Syncope / convulsive syncope mimic plausible
Presyncope, pallor, diaphoresis, positional/vagal trigger, brief myoclonic jerks after LOC, rapid full reorientation within seconds — all favor syncope. Convulsive syncope: duration <30s, hypotonic rather than tonic onset, rapid recovery, no postictal confusion or amnesia.
Parasomnia mimic plausible
Frontal nocturnal seizures: brief, stereotyped, clustered, any sleep stage throughout night. NREM parasomnias: longer, variable behavior, occur from slow-wave sleep in first third of night, not stereotyped, family history common. PSG + video-EEG differentiates.
Functional / non-epileptic event mimic plausible
Features favoring FNEE: prolonged events, variable/inconsistent semiology, asynchronous movements, preserved recall during apparent convulsion, emotional trigger, waxing-waning course, eyes closed during event. Note: frontal lobe seizures can closely mimic FNEE — video-EEG is often required.
Absence epilepsy mimic plausible (vs. temporal hypomotor)
Typical absence: abrupt onset/offset, no aura, no postictal state, <30s, hyperventilation-induced, 3 Hz spike-wave on EEG. Focal temporal hypomotor: preceding aura, variable duration, postictal confusion/amnesia, focal EEG changes. Age of onset and EEG are key.
Cardiac arrhythmia / ictal asystole plausible
Arrhythmia or structural cardiac disease can cause LOC with convulsive activity. Insular seizures (especially right posterior insula) can also trigger bradycardia or asystole — ictal asystole. When there is prominent cardiovascular prodrome or prolonged asystolic pause, consider cardiac monitoring (implantable loop recorder).
Overall Data Quality
How complete and reliable is the semiological information?
High: Patient reliably reports aura + reliable witness or video available + repeated stereotyped events + full chronological account. Medium: Partial history — some features known but chronology incomplete or witness unreliable. Low: Unwitnessed convulsion, retrograde amnesia, no video, no focal onset feature identified.
Step 7 · Localization Hypothesis
Structured semiology impression based on all selected features. Use to guide video review, EEG/MRI interpretation, and epilepsy referral — not as a standalone diagnostic conclusion.
Algorithm framework: Cross-referenced with ILAE focal seizure classification/terminology, focal epilepsy semiology literature (temporal, frontal, parietal, occipital, insular, cingulate), and peer-reviewed probabilistic semiology mapping reviews.

No scoring weights are formally validated by prospective clinical trial — they reflect expert synthesis of semiology literature and are deliberately transparent so a clinician can see and challenge each weighting decision. Tool designed by Daniel Short MD.

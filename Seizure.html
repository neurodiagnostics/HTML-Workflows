Educational Use OnlyThis tool generates a localization hypothesis from seizure history/semiology. It is not a diagnostic device, treatment recommendation, or replacement for clinician assessment, video-EEG, epilepsy-protocol MRI, or epilepsy conference localization.
Step 1 · Is this plausibly a focal epileptic seizure?
Start by separating focal epileptic events from generalized onset, syncope, migraine aura, parasomnia, movement disorder, and functional/nonepileptic events. Garbage in, beautifully styled garbage out.
Principle: Focal seizures originate within networks limited to one hemisphere; semiology can identify the regional network involved in onset and propagation. The first ictal symptom/sign usually carries the most localizing value; later dramatic signs often reflect spread.
A · Event Pattern Focal confidence
Highly stereotyped recurrent events
Focal seizures usually repeat in a stereotyped sequence because the onset network and propagation pattern are consistent. Ask the witness for the first observable sign and the patient for the first subjective symptom.
Clear focal onset feature before bilateral convulsion or impaired consciousness
Examples: rising epigastric sensation, déjà vu, unilateral paresthesia, forced head/eye version, unilateral clonic activity, visual symptoms in one hemifield, speech arrest/aphasia, or unilateral dystonia before a bilateral tonic-clonic seizure.
Chronology available: prodrome → aura/earliest ictal feature → evolution → postictal state
Prodrome = hours-days of nonspecific mood, sleep, headache, irritability; usually not localizing. Aura = focal aware seizure at onset; this is often the best localization clue. Document the chronology verbatim.
Step 2 · Earliest ictal feature / aura
Select the earliest convincing symptom/sign. You can select multiple if the onset is uncertain, but the first feature should get more weight than later propagation.
A · Non-motor onset features aura network
Rising epigastric sensation, autonomic surge, tachycardia, pallor
Classic mesial temporal limbic-autonomic clue. Can also be insular or opercular; context matters.
Déjà vu, jamais vu, experiential memory phenomenon, fear
Mesial temporal, amygdala, hippocampal network is favored. Fear can also arise from orbitofrontal, cingulate, insular, or temporal networks.
Auditory aura: buzzing, voices, music, distorted sound
Lateral temporal / superior temporal gyrus is favored, especially simple auditory hallucination or distortion.
Gustatory aura, throat/laryngeal sensation, nausea, dyspnea
Insular or opercular network clue. Often masquerades as temporal lobe epilepsy because spread is fast and scalp EEG can lie like a resident after night float.
Unilateral paresthesia, electric/crawling sensation, sensory Jacksonian march
Primary sensory cortex/parietal onset favored. Symptoms are typically contralateral to the involved cortex and may march across the sensory homunculus.
Body image distortion: limb enlarged/absent/floating/twisting
Non-dominant parietal network clue. Ask specifically about distorted body schema, levitation, or altered limb posture without movement.
Simple visual aura: colored circles/flashes, scotoma, hemifield symptom
Primary occipital cortex favored. Visual symptom is typically in the visual field contralateral to the seizure hemisphere. Brief duration (<2 min) supports seizure over migraine aura.
Complex visual hallucination/illusion: scenes, people, macropsia/micropsia
Extrastriate occipital or temporo-parieto-occipital junction clue. Macropsia/micropsia particularly supports parieto-occipital junction.
B · Earliest motor / language features
Unilateral focal clonic/tonic movement or motor Jacksonian march
Primary motor cortex or peri-rolandic onset favored; lateralizes contralateral to the moving limb/face when clearly unilateral.
Abrupt hypermotor behavior: bicycling, pelvic thrusting, thrashing, vocalization
Frontal lobe, especially mesial/orbitofrontal/cingulate networks, favored. Often brief, clustered, nocturnal, and may have normal/obscured ictal scalp EEG.
Abrupt asymmetric tonic posturing / fencing posture
Supplementary sensorimotor area involvement. Classically contralateral arm extension with ipsilateral arm flexion; can reflect rapid spread from elsewhere.
Speech arrest, dysphasia, inability to read/write/calculate
Dominant frontal opercular/Broca area or dominant parieto-temporal network depending on associated features. Test naming and comprehension as early as safely possible.
Step 3 · Evolution and lobar pattern
These features refine onset vs propagation. A temporal seizure can look frontal after spread; a frontal seizure can look temporal if it gets polite and slows down. Chronology is king.
A · Temporal limbic pattern
Behavioral arrest → impaired awareness → oro/manual automatisms
Common temporal pattern, especially mesial temporal. Postictal confusion supports temporal over absence/frontal mimics.
Prominent postictal confusion, amnesia, fatigue
Favors temporal focal impaired consciousness seizure when compared with brief frontal seizures or typical absence.
B · Frontal / sleep-related pattern
Very brief events, abrupt onset/offset, clustering, nocturnal predominance
Classic frontal clue. Sleep-related hypermotor epilepsy can be mistaken for parasomnia or PNES; stereotypy and clustering are key.
Axial tonic posturing, sudden falls, explosive vocalization
Frontal/frontopolar/SMA/cingulate networks. Beware rapid spread from parietal/occipital regions.
C · Posterior cortex / operculo-insular pattern
Early eye deviation, epileptic nystagmus, eyelid flutter/forced eye closure with visual aura
Occipital or parieto-occipital junction. Epileptic nystagmus fast phase typically points away from the seizure hemisphere.
Mouth/tongue sensorimotor symptoms, dysarthria, drooling
Fronto-parietal operculum / centrotemporal / sylvian network. Often overlaps with insular autonomic/gustatory/throat sensations.
Step 4 · Hemispheric lateralizing signs
These help choose right vs left/dominant vs non-dominant. They do not replace localization; they give the localization hypothesis a compass.
Dominance assumption: For most right-handed patients, language-dominant hemisphere is left. Confirm with handedness, prior lesion history, fMRI/Wada when relevant. This tool lets you record clinical lateralizers without pretending every brain read the textbook.
Unilateral ictal clonic activity or dystonia
Usually lateralizes to the contralateral hemisphere. Upper limb dystonia in temporal seizures is a particularly useful lateralizing sign.
Early forced head/eye version
Forced version generally lateralizes contralateral to the direction of head/eye turn. Non-forced early turning can be less reliable.
Ictal speech vs aphasia/postictal dysphasia
Ictal speech tends to non-dominant hemisphere; ictal aphasia or postictal dysphasia tends to dominant hemisphere.
Unilateral manual automatisms or postictal nose wiping
Manual automatisms and postictal nose wiping often lateralize ipsilateral to the seizure hemisphere. Less strong than forced version/dystonia but useful in a convergent pattern.
Visual aura confined to one hemifield
Visual symptoms in a hemifield usually lateralize to the contralateral occipital hemisphere.
Ictal vomiting / urge to urinate / preserved speech during automatisms
These tend to suggest non-dominant temporal involvement, not a precise lobe by themselves.
Step 5 · Confounders, mimics, and confidence modifiers
Localization is weaker when onset was unwitnessed, the first remembered symptom occurs late, or mimics are plausible.
Migraine aura mimic plausible
Migraine aura often evolves over 5–60 minutes with spreading positive/negative symptoms; occipital seizures are usually brief, stereotyped, and often <2 minutes.
Syncope/convulsive syncope mimic plausible
Look for presyncope, pallor, diaphoresis, positional trigger, brief myoclonic jerks after LOC, rapid reorientation. Do not localize syncope. The brain did not produce a focal masterpiece; it just ran out of perfusion.
Parasomnia mimic plausible
Frontal nocturnal seizures are brief, stereotyped, clustered, and can occur throughout the night. NREM parasomnias are longer, more variable, and often occur from slow-wave sleep early in the night.
Functional / nonepileptic event mimic plausible
Consider when events are prolonged, variable, asynchronous, fluctuating, or emotionally triggered. Frontal lobe seizures can mimic PNES; video-EEG is often needed.
Confidence modifiers
Overall data quality
Best: patient can report aura + reliable witness/video + repeated stereotyped chronology. Worst: unwitnessed convulsion with amnesia and no focal onset.
Step 6 · Localization hypothesis
This is a structured semiology impression. Use it to guide history-taking, video review, EEG/MRI review, and epilepsy referral—not to tattoo “left temporal” on the patient’s forehead.

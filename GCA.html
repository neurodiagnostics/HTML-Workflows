Step 1 · Critical Context — Classification vs. Diagnosis
The 2022 ACR/EULAR criteria are classification criteria for research, not diagnostic criteria. They must only be applied after a clinical diagnosis of large-vessel or medium-vessel vasculitis has been made and mimics have been excluded.
Key principles before applying these criteria (Figure 1, Ponte et al. 2022):
Classification ≠ Diagnosis: These criteria classify confirmed vasculitis cases into GCA for research purposes. They perform poorly as diagnostic tools when vasculitis mimics have not been excluded (Ann Intern Med 1998).
Prerequisites: (1) A diagnosis of medium-vessel or large-vessel vasculitis must have been made; (2) Alternate diagnoses mimicking vasculitis (infection, malignancy, atherosclerosis) must have been excluded
Validated performance (DCVAS study, n=756 GCA, n=749 comparators): Sensitivity 87.0% (95% CI 82.0–91.0%), specificity 94.8% (95% CI 91.0–97.4%), AUC 0.91 (95% CI 0.88–0.94)
Subtype note: Biopsy-proven GCA: sensitivity 100% (95% CI 99.0–100.0%). Large-vessel GCA (without positive TAB): sensitivity only 55.7% (95% CI 46.5–64.6%) — a known limitation

Comparator group used to derive criteria: TAK (33.5%), other vasculitides mimicking GCA (33.4%), non-vasculitis LVV mimics (33.1%)
Prerequisite confirmation REQUIRED before scoring
Has a diagnosis of LVV or medium-vessel vasculitis been made AND have vasculitis mimics been excluded?
Vasculitis mimics to exclude before applying criteria include:
Infection (bacterial endocarditis, fungal/TB arteritis), malignancy (lymphoma, paraneoplastic), atherosclerosis and fibromuscular dysplasia, amyloidosis, IgG4-related disease, primary CNS vasculitis, sarcoidosis, other inflammatory conditions causing elevated acute-phase reactants (RA, PMR without GCA).

On the 1990 ACR criteria vs 2022: The 1990 criteria applied to the DCVAS validation set showed sensitivity 80.3% (95% CI 74.6–85.1%) and specificity 92.5% (95% CI 88.1–95.7%). Crucially, sensitivity for large-vessel GCA under 1990 criteria was only 37.1% (95% CI 28.6–46.2%), vs 55.7% under 2022 criteria — a major improvement.
GCA epidemiology: Most common form of systemic vasculitis in patients ≥50 years. Mean age at diagnosis 72.2 years (DCVAS cohort). Female predominance: 67.6% of GCA cases. Defined by granulomatous arteritis of large- and medium-sized vessels with predisposition for cranial arteries and aorta/primary branches. PMR coexists in up to 40–50% of patients.
Step 2 · Absolute Requirement + Inflammatory Markers

Absolute requirement DISQUALIFYING IF NOT MET
Age ≥50 years at time of diagnosis
Of 942 confirmed GCA cases in the DCVAS dataset, only 7 (<1%) were diagnosed before age 50. Therefore, age ≥50 years at diagnosis is an absolute requirement — not a scored criterion. Failure to meet this requirement means GCA cannot be classified regardless of score. Note: the TAK criteria require age ≤60 years at diagnosis. The 50–60 year overlap zone requires careful clinical and imaging evaluation: in the DCVAS cohort, of 70 patients with GCA diagnosed ages 50–60, 62.9% met GCA criteria, 12.9% met TAK criteria, and only 2.9% met both.
Inflammatory markers +3 points
Maximum ESR ≥50 mm/hour OR maximum CRP ≥10 mg/L
Values must be from prior to initiation of treatment for vasculitis — these are the maximum values obtained during diagnostic work-up, not values on therapy. In the DCVAS cohort: ESR ≥50 mm/hr was present in 73.8% of GCA cases vs 38.9% of comparators; CRP ≥10 mg/L in 90.3% of GCA cases vs 59.4% of comparators.

Critical caution (tocilizumab patients): Tocilizumab (IL-6 receptor blocker) suppresses CRP and ESR even in the presence of active vascular inflammation — inflammatory markers cannot be used to monitor disease activity in patients on tocilizumab. This is a recognised limitation for monitoring GCA after treatment initiation.
Combined criterion: ESR and CRP were combined into a single item during criteria development because they represent the same biological process (acute-phase response driven by IL-6). This combined item was given +3 points based on its discriminatory value in lasso logistic regression.

Sudden visual loss +3 points
Sudden visual loss (diplopia, amaurosis fugax, or permanent visual loss)
Present in 13.5% of GCA cases (102/756) vs 3.9% of comparators (29/749) in the DCVAS cohort (p<0.001). Sudden visual loss is the most feared complication of GCA — ischaemic optic neuropathy (anterior or posterior) is most common. Once visual loss occurs, recovery is rare. Ophthalmic emergency: high-dose glucocorticoids should be initiated immediately when GCA with visual involvement is suspected — do not wait for biopsy results. Note: IV methylprednisolone (e.g. 500 mg–1 g/day × 3 days) is preferred over oral prednisone alone in acute visual loss to maximise chance of preventing fellow eye involvement.
Important: Use of IV glucocorticoids was an exclusion criterion in the GiACTA trial, meaning patients with visual loss were systematically excluded from the main tocilizumab evidence base. Clinical management decisions for GCA with visual involvement must account for this evidence gap.
Morning stiffness +2 points
Morning stiffness in shoulders or neck
Present in 23.0% of GCA cases (174/756) vs 3.1% of comparators (23/749) in DCVAS (p<0.001). Morning stiffness in shoulders/arms was selected over stiffness in neck/torso (which was present in only 11.6% of GCA cases) as the more discriminatory item. This feature overlaps with polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR), which coexists in GCA in up to 40–50% of patients. Shoulder/neck stiffness is a PMR-type feature within GCA.
Jaw or tongue claudication +2 points
Jaw claudication or tongue claudication (fatigue/pain with mastication or tongue use)
Jaw claudication was present in 47.1% of GCA cases vs 2.5% of comparators — among the most discriminating individual features in the dataset. Tongue claudication was present in only 2.8% of GCA cases, so both were combined into a single item (+2). The combination reflects ischaemia of the external carotid artery territory (maxillary artery, lingual artery). Tongue or jaw necrosis, though rare, represents a severe manifestation of GCA.
New temporal headache +2 points
New persistent temporal headache
Present in 62.8% of GCA cases vs 12.0% of comparators in DCVAS. Temporal headache is the most common presenting symptom of GCA. The item 'new persistent headache, occipital or cervical' showed statistical significance but decreased overall specificity of the model when tested against comparators and was therefore removed from the final criteria. Only new temporal headache is scored. Pre-existing or non-temporal headache does not qualify.
Scalp tenderness +2 points
Scalp tenderness
Present in 34.4% of GCA cases vs 3.3% of comparators in DCVAS (p<0.001). Scalp tenderness typically reflects ischaemia in the territory of the superficial temporal and occipital arteries. May be associated with scalp necrosis in severe cases. Often reported by patients as pain while combing hair or wearing a hat. Assessed on clinical examination.

Temporal artery vascular examination +2 points
Temporal artery abnormality on vascular examination
Examination criteria (footnote 1, Figure 1): Any ONE of the following:
— Absent or diminished temporal artery pulse
— Temporal artery tenderness on palpation
— Hard 'cord-like' appearance of the temporal artery

Present in 46.8% of GCA cases (354/756) vs 4.7% of comparators (35/749) in DCVAS (p<0.001). Vascular examination of the temporal arteries should include bilateral palpation of the superficial temporal arteries and their frontal and parietal branches. If ultrasound shows a halo sign on the temporal artery, physical examination findings should be correlated. A nodular, hard, or pulseless temporal artery strongly increases clinical suspicion.
Step 5 · Imaging and Biopsy Criteria
Positive TAB or temporal artery halo sign carries the highest score (+5). Bilateral axillary involvement and FDG-PET activity throughout the aorta each contribute +2. Multiple items can be scored independently.

Temporal artery biopsy or halo sign +5 points
Positive temporal artery biopsy (TAB) OR halo sign on temporal artery ultrasound
These two items were combined into a single +5 criterion because during model derivation, both TAB positivity and temporal artery halo sign individually dominated the model as near-perfect predictors of GCA. Combining them maintains face validity while allowing the other criteria to have discriminatory value.

TAB (footnote 3): Definite vasculitis defined by submitting physician. No specific histopathological criteria required — but presence of giant cells, mononuclear leukocyte infiltration, and fragmentation of internal elastic lamina were independently associated with histopathological interpretation of definitive vasculitis in DCVAS. ~20% of 'definitive vasculitis on TAB' cases did not have specific histopathological findings. Positive TAB in 44.3% of GCA cases vs 0.1% of comparators.

Ultrasound halo sign (footnote 3): Defined as presence of homogenous, hypoechoic wall thickening on ultrasound (OMERACT definition). Halo sign on temporal artery ultrasound in 27.9% of GCA cases vs 0.1% of comparators. Depending on clinical setting, a non-compressible halo sign of a temporal ± axillary artery may replace the need for TAB.
TAB yield considerations: Skip lesions can cause false-negative TAB. A minimum biopsy length of ≥1–2 cm is recommended. Contralateral biopsy adds ~5% yield. Glucocorticoids reduce biopsy yield but positive biopsy results can be obtained up to 2–4 weeks after treatment initiation in most cases. Temporal artery ultrasound should ideally be performed before glucocorticoid initiation (halo sign resolves quickly with treatment).
Bilateral axillary involvement +2 points
Bilateral axillary involvement on imaging
Definition (footnote 4): Luminal damage — stenosis, occlusion, or aneurysm — on angiography (CT, MR, or catheter-based) or ultrasound; OR halo sign on ultrasound; OR abnormal FDG uptake on PET. Bilateral involvement required for this criterion.

Present in 7.5% of GCA cases vs 1.6% of comparators in DCVAS (p<0.001). Identified via K-means cluster analysis as a specific GCA imaging pattern distinct from TAK. Axillary artery involvement reflects the predilection of GCA for the subclavian/axillary territory in the large-vessel GCA phenotype. Bilateral involvement greatly increases specificity for GCA over TAK.
FDG-PET activity throughout the aorta +2 points
FDG-PET activity throughout the aorta (descending thoracic + abdominal)
Definition (footnote 5): Abnormal FDG (18F-fluorodeoxyglucose) uptake in the arterial wall (greater than liver uptake by visual inspection) throughout the descending thoracic and abdominal aorta on PET.

Present in 6.9% of GCA cases vs 1.2% of comparators in DCVAS. Identified via K-means cluster analysis as a specific GCA imaging pattern. PET was not available in many centres treating LVV patients in DCVAS — this criterion may be underutilised and its performance in real-world practice depends on PET availability and expertise.

GCA aortic involvement: Aortic involvement occurs in 30–50% of GCA patients and is associated with increased risk of aortic aneurysm, dissection, and aortic insufficiency in long-term follow-up. PET has superior sensitivity for detecting active aortic inflammation compared to CT and MR angiography. FDG-PET should be performed before glucocorticoid initiation or early in treatment for optimal sensitivity.
Step 6 · Differential Diagnosis & Treatment Planning
Identify any features that suggest an alternative diagnosis. Also document treatment intent — the evidence base has expanded significantly since tocilizumab (2017) and upadacitinib (2025) FDA approvals.
Mimics and red flags — check all that are present
✕
Age <50 years
GCA almost never occurs before 50 — consider TAK, primary CNS vasculitis, or other vasculitides
✕
Upper extremity BP discrepancy or limb claudication in younger patient
Consider Takayasu arteritis (TAK) — especially if age ≤60
✕
Fever of unknown origin / constitutional symptoms dominating
Consider infection (bacterial endocarditis, TB) or lymphoma before attributing to GCA
✕
No response to glucocorticoids within 2–3 weeks
Typical GCA responds rapidly to prednisone — non-response raises alternative diagnoses
✕
Multiorgan involvement suggesting IgG4-RD or sarcoidosis
Peri-aortic mass, salivary gland enlargement, uveitis, pulmonary nodules, hypercalcaemia
✕
ANCA positivity or renal involvement
Suggests AAV (GPA, EGPA, MPA) — not typical of GCA
✕
Unilateral/focal axillary or carotid involvement without cranial features
Consider isolated aortitis or focal LVV rather than typical GCA
✕
B symptoms + lymphadenopathy + elevated LDH
Consider lymphoma — can mimic GCA with elevated ESR/CRP and constitutional symptoms
Treatment planning CLINICAL MANAGEMENT
Intended initial treatment approach
Glucocorticoids (first-line — all patients):
Oral prednisone 40–60 mg/day (1 mg/kg/day, max 60 mg) for initial treatment. For visual involvement or imminent visual loss: IV methylprednisolone 500 mg–1 g/day × 3 days before oral prednisone (not studied in RCTs, based on expert consensus). Taper over 12–18 months targeting prednisone discontinuation; rapid tapering increases relapse risk. Relapse occurs in ~40–50% of GCA patients despite glucocorticoids.

Tocilizumab (IL-6R blocker) — FDA-approved May 2017 for GCA:
GiACTA trial (Stone et al. NEJM 2017, n=250): TCZ 162 mg SC weekly + 26-week prednisone taper → 56% sustained glucocorticoid-free remission at 52 weeks vs 14% placebo + 26-week taper (p<0.001). Every-other-week TCZ: 53% vs 18% placebo + 52-week taper (p<0.001). Cumulative median prednisone dose: 1862 mg in TCZ groups vs 3296 mg in placebo. Strong evidence for glucocorticoid-sparing. ACR/EULAR guidelines suggest 12 months TCZ + 6 months GC as preferred first-line for newly diagnosed GCA.

Upadacitinib (JAK1 inhibitor, oral) — FDA-approved 2025 for GCA:
SELECT-GCA trial (Blockmans et al. EULAR 2024; NEJM 2025, n=428): UPA 15 mg once daily + 26-week prednisone taper → 46% sustained remission weeks 12–52 vs 29% placebo + 52-week taper (p=0.0019). 100 sites across 24 countries. 9/11 secondary endpoints also positive. First oral JAK inhibitor approved for GCA. FDA boxed warnings: MACE, VTE, malignancy — cardiovascular risk factors and prior malignancy must be assessed. Oral route advantageous vs SC tocilizumab for some patients; no head-to-head comparison.
Monitoring note: Both tocilizumab and upadacitinib suppress CRP and ESR, masking inflammatory flares. Clinical and imaging-based monitoring (PET, MRI/CTA) is essential on targeted therapies. Methotrexate (MTX) has shown inconsistent efficacy as a GC-sparing agent (3 small RCTs); the ACR/EULAR guidelines list it as an alternative to tocilizumab, not first-line. TNF inhibitors are not effective in GCA (multiple studies).

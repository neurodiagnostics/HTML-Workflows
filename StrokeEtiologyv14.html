Step 1 · Patient Age and Presenting Blood Pressure
Patient age and initial BP at stroke presentation. Age is one of the strongest modifiers of pre-test probability for specific etiologies — it shifts the differential significantly, particularly for uncommon causes like APLS, PFO-related paradoxical embolism, and genetic arteriopathies.
Clinical relevance of presenting BP:
Markedly elevated BP (>220/110): Supports hypertensive small vessel disease (lipohyalinosis, arteriolosclerosis) particularly with perforator-territory infarcts (basal ganglia, pons, internal capsule, thalamus). The vascular centrencephalon transmits systemic pressure with minimal dampening — lacunar infarction in these territories is the direct result of hypertensive arteriolar injury.

Moderately elevated BP (180–220): Hypertension is the dominant modifiable risk factor for both ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke; consistent with SVD pathophysiology but non-specific.

Normal or mildly elevated BP: Does not support primary SVD; other etiologies (CE, large vessel disease) more likely if other features absent.
Patient age REQUIRED — modifies etiology probability
Patient age at time of stroke
Age is one of the most powerful pre-test probability modifiers in stroke etiology classification. In young patients (<50y), the prior probability of cardioembolic, large vessel atherosclerotic, and SVD etiologies is substantially lower — uncommon causes (dissection, APLS, PFO-paradoxical embolism, genetic arteriopathy, vasculitis, hypercoagulable state) account for a disproportionately large share. Atrial fibrillation as the underlying mechanism is also less common in young patients, though not impossible.

In older patients (≥65y), atrial fibrillation, large vessel atherosclerosis, and small vessel disease together account for the majority of strokes. Cryptogenic stroke in this group should prompt especially aggressive prolonged cardiac monitoring — paroxysmal AF detection rates increase substantially with longer monitoring duration (CRYSTAL-AF: 12.4% at 30 days vs. 2% with standard monitoring).

The 45–65y range is transitional — all mechanisms are common, and the clinical picture must guide the workup. SVD from hypertension becomes increasingly prevalent in this group.
Age <50y with cryptogenic stroke: dissection is the single most common etiology (consider CTA/MRA neck + vessel wall MRI). APLS: typically diagnosed <50y, often with prior thrombotic events. PFO-paradoxical embolism: RoPE score strongly favors attribution in younger patients (age <45y, no vascular risk factors).
Systolic BP at presentation REQUIRED
Presenting systolic blood pressure (SBP)
Acute ischemic stroke frequently presents with reactive hypertension from catecholamine surge, Cushing reflex, and pain/anxiety — not necessarily reflecting chronic hypertensive burden. However, severely elevated BP (>220 mmHg) at presentation, especially in the absence of a known cardioembolic or large vessel atherosclerotic source, strongly supports hypertensive small vessel disease as the mechanism. BP should not be treated unless SBP >220 or DBP >110 mmHg, unless the patient is a thrombolysis candidate (treatment threshold >185/110).
Ref: AHA/ASA Acute Ischemic Stroke Guidelines. Owens WB. Blood pressure control in acute cerebrovascular disease. J Clin Hypertens 2011;13(3):205–211. StatPearls — Lacunar Stroke (NBK563216). Management threshold: permissive hypertension until SBP >220 mmHg or DBP >110 mmHg; lower by 15% in first 24h if exceeded.
Diastolic BP at presentation SUPPLEMENTARY
Presenting diastolic blood pressure (DBP)
Antihypertensive medications at time of BP measurement AFFECTS INTERPRETATION
Was the patient on antihypertensive medications or BP-lowering agents when this BP was recorded?
The presenting BP may not reflect the patient's true hypertensive burden if antihypertensive medications have already been given. This includes:
Home medications: ACE inhibitors, ARBs, beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, diuretics — patients who take these regularly will present with lower BP than their underlying disease burden would predict.
Procedural/anesthetic agents: Propofol (can cause significant BP drop), etomidate, midazolam, fentanyl, general anesthesia — if the patient underwent any procedure prior to this BP measurement, BP may be artificially lowered.
Acute administration: IV labetalol, hydralazine, nicardipine given in the field or ED will lower BP before it is formally recorded.
If any of these apply, a normal or mildly elevated presenting BP may underestimate the patient's true hypertensive severity, and should be interpreted with caution for SVD attribution.
Permissive hypertension threshold in acute ischemic stroke: SBP >220 mmHg or DBP >110 mmHg (AHA/ASA guidelines). Treatment threshold for IV tPA eligibility: >185/110 mmHg. If BP was treated prior to recording, true untreated BP may have been higher.
Step 2 · Infarct Size and Number
DWI-MRI characteristics of the acute infarct(s). Size and multiplicity are critical for TOAST classification — they directly inform whether SVD or an embolic mechanism is more likely.

⚠ If acute stroke intervention was performed (thrombectomy or IV tPA with successful recanalization): base your answers on the estimated infarct size and territory if intervention had not improved outcome — i.e., the territory at risk based on pre-intervention DWI core, CT perfusion maps, or the territory of the occluded vessel. A small residual DWI infarct after successful M1 thrombectomy should be classified by the M1 territory, not the residual infarct size.
TOAST size criterion: The traditional lacunar size cutoff is <15 mm diameter (Adams 1993). Some authors use <20 mm; classical Fisher lacunes are <15 mm. Infarcts ≥15 mm are inconsistent with pure lipohyalinosis and may require explanation beyond SVD.

Multiplicity: Multiple infarcts in different vascular territories — particularly bilateral or anterior + posterior — carry OR 2–3× for cardioembolic source vs. large vessel disease (LVD). Multiple small lesions in a single territory suggest proximal artery-to-artery embolism. Multiple DWI lesions in clinically lacunar syndromes are associated with an embolic source in up to 27% of cases (Kang DW et al., Stroke 2003;34:2599).
Largest infarct diameter on DWI REQUIRED
Maximum single infarct diameter on DWI-MRI
Punctate (<5 mm): Tiny lesions often seen as part of an embolic shower — multiple small cortical or subcortical DWI foci scattered across one or more vascular territories. Punctate infarcts in multiple different territories are characteristic of a proximal embolic source (cardioembolic or hypercoagulable state) and should NOT be equated with SVD lacunes, even though each individual lesion is small. Select "Punctate" if the infarcts are tiny (<5 mm) and multiple; select "<15 mm" only for a single or few lacunar-sized infarcts suggestive of perforator disease.

15 mm threshold: Distinguishes classical lacunar infarcts (perforator occlusion from SVD) from territorial infarcts. Infarcts 15–20 mm are a grey zone. TOAST criteria specify <15 mm. Infarcts >15 mm in perforator territory may represent branch atheromatous disease (BAD).
Ref: Adams HP et al. Stroke. 1993;24:35–41. Lacune definition: <15 mm; Fisher CM original series: 0.5–15 mm diameter. Punctate embolic shower: Kang DW et al. Stroke 2003;34:2599 — multiple small DWI lesions strongly associated with embolic source (CE or hypercoagulable) rather than SVD.
Number and distribution of acute DWI lesions REQUIRED
Number and territorial distribution of acute DWI infarcts
DWI multiplicity is one of the most diagnostically useful imaging features. Multiple infarcts in different vascular territories are a strong signal for a central cardiac or proximal aortic embolic source. Multiple infarcts in the same territory with a cortical pattern suggest artery-to-artery embolism from a proximal stenosis or unstable plaque. Bilateral anterior circulation infarcts or anterior + posterior combination argue strongly for cardioembolism over large vessel disease (LVD).
Ref: Yaghi S et al. Patterns of infarction on MRI in patients with acute ischemic stroke and cardio-embolism: systematic review. Front Neurol. 2020. Kang DW et al. Stroke. 2003;34:2599–2602 — embolic source in 27% with multiple DWI lesions vs. 6% with single lacunar lesion.
Step 3 · Infarct Topography
Location of the infarct on DWI. Topography is among the strongest imaging predictors of stroke mechanism — cortical location, watershed pattern, and multiterritory scatter each carry specific etiologic implications.
Evidence: Systematic review (Yaghi S et al. Front Neurol 2020) — cortical infarcts are 2× more frequent in cardioembolic stroke. Lacunar infarcts (OR 0.49, 95% CI 0.31–0.80) are significantly less likely with a cardioembolic source.

Watershed/border-zone: The radiologic hallmark of hemodynamic ischemia from high-grade carotid stenosis — linear subcortical or cortical "beading" in the ACA/MCA or MCA/PCA border zones.
Strictly perforator territory (striatocapsular, pons, thalamus): Classic SVD signature when <15 mm and isolated. Branch atheromatous disease (BAD) produces similar topography but larger infarcts.
Bilateral cortical scatter: Argues strongly for central cardiac embolism over any unilateral atherosclerotic source.

Step 4 · Parent Vessel Atherosclerotic Disease
CTA or MRA findings on vessels supplying the infarct territory. TOAST LVD criteria require ≥50% stenosis or occlusion of a relevant major artery — mild atherosclerosis does not qualify.
TOAST Large Vessel Disease (LVD) definition (Adams 1993): Significant atherosclerosis (>50% stenosis or occlusion) of a major brain artery or branch cortical artery, presumably due to atherosclerosis. Clinical and brain imaging findings must be consistent with the stenotic vessel's territory.

Critical error: Assigning LVD to mild stenosis (<50%) is the most common TOAST classification error — clinician-rendered LVD has positive predictive value of only 13% in the GWTG-Stroke registry (Prabhakaran S et al., Front Neurol 2024). Mild atherosclerosis is not a TOAST LVD criterion.

ICAD (intracranial atherosclerotic disease): Stenosis of intracranial parent artery (MCA M1, basilar, intracranial ICA) — a distinct and common mechanism in Asian populations; mechanism may be thromboembolism or hemodynamic.
Degree of disease on parent vessel REQUIRED
Atherosclerotic stenosis on the vessel supplying the infarct territory (CTA/MRA)
Relevant parent vessels: ipsilateral ICA (for anterior circulation), ipsilateral vertebral or basilar (for posterior circulation), MCA M1/M2, PCA, ACA. Stenosis graded by NASCET criteria for extracranial ICA (see Step 5). Intracranial vessels graded by WASID criteria. Occlusion of a large artery ipsilateral to the infarct territory may represent either embolic occlusion (thrombus from another source) or in-situ thrombosis on atherosclerotic plaque — CTA appearance and collateral pattern help differentiate.
Ref: Prabhakaran S et al. TOAST stroke subtype classification in clinical practice: GWTG-Stroke. Front Neurol 2024. LVD sensitivity 33%, PPV 13% — suboptimal due to over-attribution of mild stenosis.
Special vessel findings SELECT ALL THAT APPLY
Arterial dissection — wall hematoma or dissection flap on vessel wall imaging / CTA
Dissection is a distinct "Other Determined Etiology" per TOAST — not LVD. Most common: ICA or vertebral artery. CTA findings: intimal flap, double lumen, tapered occlusion ("flame sign"), pseudoaneurysm, mural hematoma (hyperintense on fat-sat T1). MRA: tapered stenosis or occlusion. Vessel wall MRI: intramural hematoma. Mechanism: artery-to-artery embolism from thrombus on the dissected intima.
Young patients (<50y), neck trauma, connective tissue disorders (EDS, Marfan), fibromuscular dysplasia — high suspicion for dissection. Headache or neck pain at stroke onset is characteristic.
Intracranial atherosclerotic disease (ICAD) — stenosis of intracranial parent artery (MCA, basilar, intracranial ICA)
Carotid web ESUS / YOUNG CRYPTOGENIC STROKE
Carotid web — shelf-like intraluminal filling defect at the posterior ICA bulb (intimal fibromuscular dysplasia variant)
What it is: Carotid web (CaW) is an intimal variant of fibromuscular dysplasia — a thin, shelf-like protrusion from the posterior wall of the ICA bulb that causes flow disruption, blood stasis, and mural thrombus formation, leading to ipsilateral artery-to-artery thromboembolism. It does NOT cause flow-limiting stenosis (<50% by NASCET in virtually all cases). It is a non-atherosclerotic lesion.

Who to suspect it in: Young patient (<60y), female sex, Black or Afro-Caribbean ancestry (disproportionately affected), ESUS or cryptogenic ipsilateral anterior circulation stroke, absence of conventional vascular risk factors. Prevalence ~9.4% in cryptogenic stroke (Coutinho 2019) and 2.5% in large vessel occlusion (MR-CLEAN post-hoc, Compagne 2018).

How to identify it: CTA of the neck (thin-slice, multiplanar reconstruction) is the most reliable modality — look for a shelf-like filling defect projecting posteriorly into the ICA bulb lumen. DSA is the gold standard. May be missed on MRA or if CTA is not specifically reviewed for it. Can be bilateral in 58% of symptomatic cases (Sajedi 2019).

Stroke risk data:
  • Prevalence in ESUS ipsilateral to large intracranial artery occlusion is higher than in patients with an identified embolic source (Coutinho 2019, ESO).
  • Recurrence rate on medical therapy alone: 6.9%/year; cumulative 10.8% at 1 year, 19.8% at 2 years, 27.3% at 5 years (Joux 2019, Stroke). This is substantially higher than ESUS trials (3–5%/year).
  • Recurrent stroke adjusted RR: 4.38 (95% CI 1.38–13.85) vs. patients without carotid web undergoing thrombectomy (Takasugi 2024, J Neurol).
  • In high-risk angioarchitecture (≥2 high-risk web angles): OR 5.91 (95% CI 4.25–8.24) per additional high-risk angle for stroke (preprint 2024).
  • 29% of symptomatic webs have superimposed thrombus (Sajedi 2019).
  • CEA or carotid stenting appear effective — 0 recurrences in 23 surgically/stented patients over median 39.8 months (Joux 2019). ESVS 2023 guidelines recommend CEA or CAS for symptomatic carotid web when no other stroke cause is identified after detailed workup.
Key refs: Joux J et al. Stroke 2019;50:3447 — recurrence 6.9%/year, surgery effective. Sajedi SA et al. Stroke 2019;50:3143 — bilateral in 58%, thrombus in 29%. Compagne KCJ et al. Stroke 2018 — 2.5% in MR-CLEAN LVO. Coutinho JM et al. Int J Stroke 2019 — 9.4% in cryptogenic stroke. Takasugi J et al. J Neurol 2024 — RR 4.38 for recurrence. ESVS 2023 guidelines.
Step 5 · ICA Stenosis — NASCET Criteria
Applicable to anterior circulation strokes — degree of ipsilateral ICA stenosis by NASCET criteria. NASCET = (1 − [residual lumen ÷ distal normal ICA diameter]) × 100%.
NASCET risk data:
Symptomatic ≥70% stenosis: ~13%/year ipsilateral stroke risk on medical therapy alone. NASCET trial: absolute risk reduction from CEA = 16% at 5 years (NNT ~6). CEA benefit requires surgical risk <6% perioperative mortality/stroke.
Symptomatic 50–69%: Absolute benefit from CEA ~5% at 5 years (NNT ~20) — marginal; not universally recommended.
Asymptomatic 70–99%: Modern pooled annual ipsilateral stroke rate = 0.88% (95% CI 0.62–1.02) after 2010 with intensive medical therapy (Howard DPJ et al., Lancet Neurol 2021). 70–99% carries 2–3× higher risk than 50–69% in all decades studied (OR 2.1, 95% CI 1.7–2.5).

Key principle: Symptomatic stenosis is defined as prior ipsilateral TIA or stroke — this is the current presentation, so the question is whether the stenosis is ipsilateral and plausibly causal.
Ipsilateral ICA stenosis by NASCET ANTERIOR CIRCULATION ONLY
Degree of stenosis — ipsilateral ICA to the infarct territory (NASCET method)
NASCET criteria are specific to extracranial ICA stenosis. The residual lumen is divided by the normal ICA diameter distal to the bulb — a critical point because ECST and NASCET grade the same anatomic lesion differently. A 70% NASCET stenosis ≈ 82% ECST stenosis. For symptomatic carotid disease, the ACC/AHA guidelines recommend CEA or CAS for 70–99% stenosis with surgical risk <6%. For 50–69%, CEA benefit is marginal and decision should be individualized.
Ref: NASCET Collaborators. N Engl J Med 1991;325:445–453. Howard DPJ et al. Lancet Neurol 2021;20(3):193–202 — pooled asymptomatic stroke rates stratified by decade and stenosis degree.
Step 6 · Carotid Plaque Morphology and High-Risk Features
Plaque composition and vulnerability features from CT/CTA, ultrasound, or dedicated MRI plaque imaging. High-risk features substantially change the pre-test probability of a carotid source independent of stenosis degree.
High-risk plaque (HRP) features — MRI-validated (Plaque-RADS 2023; AJNR 2023):
Intraplaque hemorrhage: OR 25.2 (p<0.001) for carotid-source stroke; hazard ratio 5.7 (95% CI 3.0–10.9) for future cerebrovascular events across meta-analyses. Best established biomarker of plaque vulnerability.
Intraluminal thrombus: OR 103.6 (p<0.001) — strongest single predictor (see Step 7).
LRNC (lipid-rich necrotic core): Independently associated with ipsilateral acute cerebral infarction — OR 1.30 per unit increase (CARE-II study, Zhao XQ et al.).
Fibrous cap rupture / ulceration: Surface disruption indicates vulnerability; contrast penetration into plaque on CTA = ulceration.
Macrocalcification (dense/homogeneous): Generally associated with stable plaque; patients with calcified plaques more likely to be asymptomatic (Hunt et al. AJNR 2023). Macrocalcifications do not prominently increase stroke risk alone (JACC 2021).
Dominant plaque composition SELECT ONE
Overall plaque composition on available imaging (CT/CTA, ultrasound, or MRI)
CT/CTA: calcified plaque = hyperdense (>130 HU); soft plaque = low density (20–60 HU). Ultrasound: echogenicity — hypoechoic (soft), heterogeneous (mixed), hyperechoic (calcified). MRI: calcifications "jet black" on all sequences. Note that superficial spotty microcalcifications may paradoxically associate with IPH and increased vulnerability — the protective effect is primarily for bulk macrocalcification. Plaque-RADS (2023) establishes a standardized reporting framework for carotid plaque components.
Ref: Hunt TS et al. AJNR 2023;44(8):880. Van den Bouwhuijsen QJA et al. JACC 2021. Carotid Plaque-RADS: Gupta A et al. JACC Cardiovasc Imaging 2023.

High-risk plaque features — MRI / CTA / ultrasound SELECT ALL THAT APPLY
Intraplaque hemorrhage
What it looks like on imaging:
Vessel wall MRI (most sensitive — dedicated plaque sequences): T1-hyperintense "bright halo" or crescent within the plaque on MPRAGE or T1-weighted fat-saturated sequences. Signal brighter than adjacent sternocleidomastoid muscle. The MPRAGE sequence (magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo) is the most validated technique for IPH detection at 1.5T and 3T.
CTA (neck CTA / CT angiography): Intraplaque hemorrhage itself is not directly visible on standard CTA. However, its indirect sign is a heterogeneous or irregular plaque with a low-density (soft tissue) core adjacent to the lumen — sometimes described as a "napkin ring sign" of the plaque. CTA underestimates IPH burden compared to vessel wall MRI.
Carotid ultrasound (duplex): Hypoechoic (dark/echolucent) plaque core, particularly when large and central, correlates with IPH/LRNC on histology. "Gray-scale median" analysis can help quantify echogenicity.
OR 25.2 for carotid-source stroke (Balu N et al. Stroke 2014). HR 5.7 for future events (meta-analysis, Gupta A et al. Stroke 2013). Intraplaque hemorrhage is the best-established single biomarker of plaque vulnerability.
Lipid-rich necrotic core (LRNC)
What it looks like on imaging:
Vessel wall MRI: On multi-contrast sequences (T1, T2, TOF), LRNC appears as a region isointense or hypointense to muscle on T1 and markedly hypointense on T2 ("dark" on T2). It is non-enhancing on contrast-enhanced vessel wall MRI sequences, which distinguishes it from the fibrous cap (which can enhance).
CTA (neck CTA / CT angiography): LRNC appears as a low-density (hypodense) area within the plaque, typically 30–60 HU, adjacent to or beneath the lumen surface. A large hypo-attenuating plaque core on CTA is the closest CT surrogate for LRNC. Calcification (>130 HU) may be adjacent.
Carotid ultrasound (duplex): Predominantly hypoechoic (echolucent) plaque — the "soft plaque" appearance on B-mode ultrasound. A predominantly echolucent plaque correlates with a large LRNC on histology.
Ref: CARE-II study (Zhao XQ et al. J Cardiovasc Magn Reson 2019) — LRNC volume OR 1.30 per unit for ipsilateral acute cerebral infarction. LRNC >40% of plaque volume has highest risk association.
Plaque ulceration / surface disruption
What it looks like on imaging:
CTA (neck CTA / CT angiography — most reliable for ulceration): Contrast material extending into the plaque body — a "crater" or outpouching filled with contrast within the plaque substance. Defined as a contrast-filled niche ≥1 mm depth projecting into the plaque from the lumen. Visible on axial source images and coronal/sagittal multiplanar reformats. This is the most reliable and commonly available modality for identifying ulceration.
Vessel wall MRI: Surface irregularity or focal defect in the fibrous cap on high-resolution vessel wall MRI. Enhancement extending through the cap surface indicates disruption.
Carotid ultrasound (duplex): Irregular plaque surface with an echogenic "crater" or surface defect on B-mode. More difficult to characterize reliably than CTA; surface irregularity (vs. smooth plaque surface) is itself an independent stroke risk marker.
Thinned or ruptured fibrous cap (MRI plaque imaging)
What it looks like on imaging:
Vessel wall MRI (primary modality — only reliable method): On contrast-enhanced vessel wall MRI, an intact thick fibrous cap appears as a dark (hypointense) band separating the lumen from the LRNC, with a thin line of enhancement at the lumen surface on post-contrast sequences. A thinned cap appears as focal thinning of this dark band. A ruptured cap shows focal discontinuity or enhancement extending through the cap into the plaque interior. This is the most specific finding for plaque vulnerability but requires dedicated high-resolution vessel wall MRI sequences (not standard MRA).
CTA (neck CTA / CT angiography): The fibrous cap cannot be directly resolved on CTA. A very thin or absent soft-tissue rim between the contrast-filled lumen and the low-density necrotic core may be a CT correlate, but standard CTA cannot reliably assess fibrous cap integrity; vessel wall MRI is required for this assessment.
Carotid ultrasound (duplex): Not reliably assessable. A "juxtaluminal" hypoechoic zone immediately below the echogenic surface can suggest cap thinning, but this requires expert interpretation and is not validated for routine use.
Step 7 · Intraluminal Thrombus
Evidence of atherothrombotic plaque rupture with superimposed thrombus — the strongest single imaging predictor of carotid-source stroke. Presence effectively confirms artery-to-artery atherothromboembolism as the dominant mechanism.
Critical evidence: Intraluminal thrombus carries an OR of 103.6 (p<0.001) for carotid-source stroke in multivariable analysis — the most powerful imaging predictor of carotid etiology, exceeding intraplaque hemorrhage (OR 25.2), stenosis as a continuous variable (AUC 0.770), and stenosis cutoffs of 50% or 70% (AUC 0.669). Its presence confirms atherothrombotic plaque rupture.

Model discriminatory value with intraluminal thrombus + intraplaque hemorrhage + thickness + smoking: AUC 0.862 vs. AUC 0.770 for stenosis alone (Balu N et al. Stroke 2014; Balu N et al. Stroke 2015).
Intraluminal thrombus on CTA / MRA / DSA STRONGEST IMAGING PREDICTOR
Intraluminal filling defect at the site of atherosclerotic plaque — consistent with thrombus superimposed on ruptured plaque
CTA appearance: filling defect within the lumen at or just distal to the plaque; may show heterogeneous or low-density material within the contrast column. Differentiate from: (a) embolic thrombus at a non-plaque location (suggests CE origin), (b) in-situ thrombosis from hypercoagulable state, (c) artefact from dense calcification. DSA or high-resolution vessel wall MRI can confirm. The presence of thrombus attached to a plaque is pathognomonic of plaque rupture — atherothrombosis mechanism.
Ref: Balu N et al. Stroke 2014;45 (STROKEAHA.114.006286) — OR 103.6 (p<0.001) for carotid-source stroke. Balu N et al. Stroke 2015 (1.5T/3T MRI study) — PR 3.60 (95% CI 2.23–5.81, p<0.001).
Step 8 · Cardiac Sources and Other Determined Etiologies
Evaluation for high-risk cardioembolic sources per TOAST criteria, and other determined etiologies. All ischemic stroke patients receive a standard transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE), ECG, and telemetry. Select what has been found and what higher-level testing has been done.
TOAST cardioembolism (CE) — high-risk sources require:
(1) A cardiac source identified, AND (2) clinical and imaging features consistent with embolism, AND (3) no evidence of a competing etiology sufficient to explain the stroke.

If both a large vessel source AND a cardiac source are identified, TOAST classifies as Undetermined — Multiple Etiologies — not CE and not LVD. The competing etiology must be adjudicated clinically.

Cardiac imaging hierarchy: TTE (transthoracic echo) is the default first-line study — evaluates EF, wall motion, valves, pericardium, and gross intracardiac thrombus. TEE (transesophageal echo) provides superior resolution for left atrial appendage (LAA) thrombus, patent foramen ovale (PFO), aortic arch atheroma, and valvular vegetations — pursue when TTE is non-diagnostic and cardioembolism remains on the differential. CT heart / cardiac MRI can evaluate LAA thrombus, cardiac masses, and myocardial scar — useful when TEE is not tolerated.
Tier 1 — Standard workup (all patients) TTE + ECG + TELEMETRY
Atrial fibrillation / flutter — paroxysmal or persistent, confirmed on ECG, continuous telemetry, or Holter monitor
Reduced EF or recent MI — TTE: EF <35%, regional wall motion abnormality, or recent MI (<4 weeks) on history / troponins
High-risk valvular disease on TTE — mechanical prosthetic valve, rheumatic mitral stenosis, infective endocarditis, marantic endocarditis
Which valve diseases are high-risk for stroke per TOAST?
High-risk (TOAST-defined): Mechanical prosthetic heart valve (any position), rheumatic mitral stenosis (regardless of whether AF is also present), infective endocarditis with vegetations, marantic (non-bacterial thrombotic) endocarditis (often in malignancy), Libman-Sacks endocarditis (antiphospholipid syndrome).

Moderate-risk (not independently TOAST high-risk, but relevant): Calcific aortic stenosis — calcific emboli are a rare mechanism; not a standalone TOAST CE criterion but may contribute. Mitral valve prolapse with redundant leaflets — historically included as low-risk CE but contemporary evidence is weak; not independently sufficient for TOAST CE classification without other features.

Valve regurgitation alone (aortic or mitral) is generally NOT a high-risk embolic source — regurgitant lesions do not generate thrombus on the valve leaflets in the same way as stenotic or infected valves. Severe mitral regurgitation may cause atrial enlargement and subsequent AF, but the regurgitation itself is not a direct embolic mechanism. Exception: flail mitral valve leaflets or those with attached vegetations (endocarditis) — these do carry embolic risk.
Tier 2 — Advanced cardiac imaging TEE / CT HEART / CARDIAC MRI
Intracardiac thrombus — LV thrombus or left atrial appendage (LAA) thrombus on TEE, cardiac CT, or cardiac MRI
TTE sensitivity for left atrial appendage thrombus is only ~40–60% — TEE is the gold standard (~95–99% sensitivity with spontaneous echo contrast). Cardiac CT is an alternative with ~96% sensitivity when TEE is not tolerated. LV thrombus: TTE sensitivity ~17–33% for small/mural thrombus — cardiac MRI (delayed gadolinium enhancement) is most sensitive. Pursue TEE or cardiac CT when: (a) non-diagnostic TTE, (b) cryptogenic stroke with AF or structural heart disease, (c) suspected endocarditis, or (d) PFO evaluation requiring precise shunt sizing.
Patent foramen ovale (PFO) BUBBLE STUDY / TEE
PFO — patent foramen ovale on agitated saline (bubble) echocardiography
PFO is present in ~25% of the general population. Attribution as a stroke mechanism requires: (1) exclusion of other causes (cryptogenic stroke), (2) large right-to-left shunt (≥20 microbubbles at rest or with Valsalva), and/or (3) concurrent atrial septal aneurysm (redundant interatrial septum with ≥10 mm excursion). RoPE score (Risk of Paradoxical Embolism) quantifies attribution probability. CLOSE, REDUCE, and DEFENSE trials showed benefit of PFO closure for cryptogenic stroke patients under 60 with high-risk PFO features.

Hypercoagulable state — antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (APLS), protein C/S deficiency, factor V Leiden, JAK2 mutation, malignancy-associated coagulopathy
Hypercoagulable testing is not routine for all stroke patients — it is most indicated in: young patients (<55y) with cryptogenic stroke, history of recurrent venous/arterial thrombosis, known autoimmune disease, prior miscarriages (consider APLS), or active malignancy. Testing ideally done off anticoagulation. Antiphospholipid antibodies (anticardiolipin IgG/IgM, anti-β2GP1, lupus anticoagulant) require confirmation on repeat testing ≥12 weeks apart for a formal APLS diagnosis. If not yet tested and the clinical picture is suggestive, the verdict will flag this as a recommendation.
Genetic arteriopathy — CADASIL (NOTCH3), Fabry disease (GLA gene), MELAS (mitochondrial)
Genetic arteriopathies are rare but should be considered in: young patients with white matter disease + lacunar infarcts (CADASIL — look for anterior temporal pole and external capsule T2 signal), recurrent cryptogenic strokes in young adults, family history of early stroke/dementia, or in specific demographics (Fabry disease — consider in young men with cryptogenic stroke + neuropathic pain + angiokeratoma). Testing is not routine in standard acute stroke workup — select "Low suspicion" if the clinical picture raises a possibility without definitive testing, and "Not suspected / not tested" in routine cases.
Post-infectious vasculopathy RECENT INFECTION HISTORY
Post-infectious vasculopathy — VZV (varicella/herpes zoster), COVID-19, other preceding infection within 6 months
Mechanism: Several infections can trigger inflammatory vasculopathy as a delayed or concurrent complication — distinct from direct septic embolism. The vessel wall is injured by direct viral invasion, immune-mediated inflammation, or hypercoagulable state induced by infection.

Varicella Zoster Virus (VZV) / Herpes Zoster: Most well-established post-infectious vasculopathy. VZV travels transaxonally from cranial ganglia (especially trigeminal) to cerebral artery adventitia, causing transmural granulomatous inflammation → intimal hyperplasia, thrombosis, and arterial stenosis/occlusion. Stroke risk is 1.3–4× higher after herpes zoster, peaking in the first 6 months (Gilden et al.; Langan 2014). Risk is highest in herpes zoster ophthalmicus (ophthalmic division → ipsilateral anterior circulation). Can affect both large and small vessels. Treat with IV acyclovir + corticosteroids.

COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2): Associated with stroke through several mechanisms: hypercoagulable state (elevated D-dimer, antiphospholipid antibodies in ~50% of severe COVID-19), endothelial injury and ACE-2-mediated vascular inflammation, cardioembolism from COVID-19 myocarditis/cardiomyopathy, and direct CNS vasculitis (rare). Thromboinflammatory pathway predominates (Romero et al. 2021).

Other: Bacterial meningitis (septic vasculitis), Lyme disease, HIV vasculopathy, rickettsia, mycoplasma, influenza (rare). Consider in appropriate clinical and geographic context.
Key refs: Gilden DH et al. VZV vasculopathy. Neurology 2019. Langan SM et al. Lancet 2014 — 1.3× stroke risk after zoster, highest 1st year. Romero JR et al. J Neurol Sci 2021 — COVID-19 thromboinflammatory stroke mechanisms. VZV stroke risk in <40y: OR up to 4× in first 6 months post-zoster.
Step 9 · Transcranial Doppler (TCD)
TCD provides real-time intracranial hemodynamic data and microembolic signal detection that can critically distinguish between competing etiologies — particularly carotid vs. cardiac sources, PFO vs. local arterial disease, and ICAD vs. proximal stenosis. Enter results if TCD was performed.
Key TCD applications in stroke etiology:
HITS/MES distribution: High-intensity transient signals (HITS / microembolic signals / MES) detected simultaneously in both MCAs or across anterior + posterior circulations strongly implicate a central (cardiac or aortic) embolic source — a single carotid plaque, carotid web, or unilateral ICAD source cannot produce bilateral simultaneous emboli. Unilateral ipsilateral-only HITS favour a local arterial source (carotid stenosis, carotid web, ICAD).

MES characteristics: Cardiac-source MES tend to be longer duration, higher total signal power, and occur more frequently in diastole. Carotid artery disease MES are predominantly systolic and shorter (Ringelstein 1998, Ann Neurol). Automated monitoring over 30–60 min improves detection sensitivity.

TCD bubble study (PFO): Agitated saline IV with bilateral MCA monitoring — HITS within 5–25 cardiac cycles confirms intracardiac right-to-left shunt (PFO). Spencer Grade ≥3 (≥31 HITS) or "curtain" pattern = large shunt. TCD bubble sensitivity ~90–95% (comparable to TEE with Valsalva maneuver).

ICA stenosis hemodynamics: Severe ICA stenosis (>70%) causes decreased ipsilateral MCA velocity + pulsatility, increased contralateral ACA collateral flow, and potentially reversed ophthalmic artery direction. Impaired vasomotor reactivity (VMR <8% response to breath-hold) signals exhausted autoregulatory reserve — supports hemodynamic (watershed) rather than embolic mechanism.

ICAD velocities: MCA mean velocity >80 cm/s or focal peak systolic velocity >120 cm/s suggests ≥50% stenosis. Lindegaard ratio (MCA/extracranial ICA) >3 distinguishes ICAD from hyperemia.

Carotid web vs. PFO: Carotid web causes minimal flow restriction — no hemodynamic TCD abnormality expected. HITS if present should be ipsilateral and unilateral only. Bilateral or multiterritory HITS with a known carotid web strongly argues the carotid web is NOT the primary source — favour cardiac/PFO etiology instead.

HITS / Microembolic Signals (spontaneous emboli monitoring) HIGHEST ETIOLOGIC VALUE
Spontaneous HITS / MES on bilateral MCA monitoring (30–60 min)
Position TCD probes bilaterally over MCAs. Record spontaneous embolic signals for 30–60 minutes. Key interpretive rules:

Bilateral / multiterritory HITS: Strongly implicates a central embolic source (cardiac, aortic arch). A unilateral carotid plaque or web cannot produce simultaneous bilateral MCA HITS. This finding effectively rules out carotid web or unilateral carotid atherosclerosis as the primary mechanism — even if a carotid web is present on imaging.

Unilateral ipsilateral HITS only: Favours a local arterial source — ipsilateral carotid stenosis/plaque, carotid web, or ICAD. Supports LVD etiology in the symptomatic territory.

MES signal characteristics (if detailed analysis available): Cardiac-source MES have longer duration, higher total signal power, and occur predominantly in diastole. Arterial-source MES (carotid, ICAD) are predominantly systolic and shorter in duration (Ringelstein 1998).
Key refs: Ringelstein EB et al. Ann Neurol 1998;44:186 — cardiac vs. arterial MES differentiation. CARESS trial (Markus 2005, Lancet Neurol) — MES as therapeutic monitoring in carotid stenosis. Abbott AL et al. Stroke 2005 — asymptomatic carotid stenosis: >2 MES/hour ipsilateral MCA = 3.6% vs. 0.7% 2-year stroke risk.
TCD Bubble Study — Right-to-Left Shunt IF PFO SUSPECTED
Agitated saline injection with bilateral MCA TCD monitoring — right-to-left shunt / PFO size grading
Method: 9 mL normal saline + 1 mL air agitated × 10 between two syringes, injected IV while monitoring bilateral MCAs at rest and during Valsalva. HITS within 5–25 cardiac cycles = intracardiac RLS (PFO). HITS after 25 cycles = intrapulmonary shunt (pulmonary AVM or hepatopulmonary syndrome).

Spencer grading scale (shunt size):
Grade 1: 1–10 HITS (small)  ·  Grade 2: 11–30  ·  Grade 3: 31–100 (large)  ·  Grade 4: 101–300  ·  Grade 5: >300 / "curtain" (massive)

Grade ≥3 or curtain pattern = large shunt → increases PFO attribution probability (higher RoPE score contribution). TCD bubble study sensitivity for PFO ~90–95%, comparable to TEE with Valsalva.
Refs: Spencer MP et al. J Ultrasound Med 2004 — grading scale. Di Tullio M et al. Stroke 1993 — TCD bubble study sensitivity. Mas JL et al. CLOSE trial — PFO closure benefit in Grade ≥3 shunt + atrial septal aneurysm.
Intracranial Flow Velocities ICAD / HEMODYNAMIC COMPROMISE
Intracranial flow velocities and collateral patterns
Focal velocity elevation suggesting ICAD: MCA mean velocity >80 cm/s (or peak systolic >120 cm/s), basilar >100 cm/s, ACA/PCA >80 cm/s, with focal turbulence. Lindegaard ratio (ipsilateral MCA/extracranial ICA) >3 = ICAD vs. hyperemia.

Reduced velocity / hemodynamic compromise from ICA stenosis: Ipsilateral MCA mean velocity reduced >50% vs. contralateral = significant hemodynamic compromise. Reversed ophthalmic artery flow = severe ICA stenosis with orbital collateral. Impaired vasomotor reactivity (breath-hold index <8%) = exhausted autoregulatory reserve — supports watershed/hemodynamic mechanism (Silvestrini 2000, HR ~3.7 for ipsilateral stroke at 2 years).

Collateral flow patterns: Increased contralateral ACA velocity (cross-filling via anterior communicating artery), reversed ipsilateral ACA, or increased PCA velocity (posterior communicating collateral) — indicate severe proximal ICA stenosis/occlusion.
Lindegaard KF et al. Stroke 1986 — Lindegaard ratio. Silvestrini M et al. Neurology 2000 — impaired VMR predicts stroke in asymptomatic ICA stenosis.
Step 9 · Inflammatory Arteriopathy Assessment
CNS vasculitis and inflammatory arteriopathies are rare and almost never confirmed in the acute inpatient setting — this step captures clinical suspicion level and supporting features, which feeds directly into the verdict and workup recommendations.
Why this is its own step: CNS vasculitis rarely gets confirmed acutely because definitive diagnosis requires brain biopsy (sensitivity ~75%, often deferred or never performed), or a highly specific pattern on vessel wall MRI + clinical context. In practice, the clinician almost never presses "confirmed" — but clinical suspicion level is still a meaningful input that changes the classification and triggers appropriate workup recommendations.

Clinical suspicion triggers: Young patient (<55y) with cryptogenic multiterritory infarcts and no other explanation + any of: prominent headache at onset, CSF pleocytosis or elevated protein, "beading" / segmental irregularity on catheter angiography or vessel wall MRI, known systemic autoimmune disease (SLE, polyarteritis nodosa, Sjögren, Behçet), or fever/constitutional symptoms without infection. Note: GCA (giant cell arteritis) is a specific consideration in patients >50y with new headache, jaw claudication, elevated ESR/CRP, and posterior circulation stroke.
Clinical suspicion for inflammatory arteriopathy SELECT ONE
Vasculitis / inflammatory arteriopathy — overall clinical suspicion level
How to assess suspicion level:
High suspicion: Young cryptogenic stroke + at least 2 of: multiterritory infarcts unexplained by cardiac or large vessel source, prominent headache, CSF pleocytosis, elevated inflammatory markers (ESR/CRP/ANA/ANCA), known systemic autoimmune disease, "beading" on vessel wall MRI or catheter angiography. Or: posterior circulation stroke in patient >50y with new headache and elevated ESR (consider GCA).

Low–moderate suspicion: One clinical feature suggesting vasculitis but otherwise explainable by another mechanism. Cryptogenic stroke in a young patient with no other supportive features.

Not suspected / low prior probability: Standard older patient with typical vascular risk factors; or another clear etiology fully explains the stroke.

Key diagnostic steps if suspected: Vessel wall MRI (concentric enhancement in vasculitis vs. eccentric in atherosclerosis), catheter angiography (beading / irregularity), CSF analysis, inflammatory serology (ESR, CRP, ANA, ANCA, complement, antiphospholipid panel), and rarely brain/meningeal biopsy.
Confirmed CNS vasculitis requires tissue diagnosis (brain biopsy) or highly characteristic vessel wall MRI + clinical picture — this is almost never established acutely. Selecting "high suspicion" here flags vasculitis as a differential to pursue in the outpatient/follow-up setting and adjusts the verdict accordingly.

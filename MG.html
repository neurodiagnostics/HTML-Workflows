Step 1 · Clinical Features and MGFA Classification
MG is an autoimmune disorder of the neuromuscular junction (NMJ) characterized by fatigable, fluctuating skeletal muscle weakness — typically worse with exertion and later in the day, improving with rest. The hallmark is fatigability: weakness that worsens with sustained or repeated activity. Prevalence ~20/100,000; bimodal onset (young women 2nd–3rd decade; older men 6th–7th decade).
Cardinal features of MG — always ask about fatigability pattern:
Ocular (most common onset ~50%): Ptosis (asymmetric, variable, Cogan's lid twitch sign), diplopia (painless, variable — no fixed pattern like CN III palsy)
Bulbar: Dysarthria (nasal quality, worsens with prolonged speech), dysphagia (solids and liquids), fatigable chewing (jaw drop with prolonged eating), dysphonia
Limb / axial: Proximal > distal weakness; neck flexor weakness (chin-on-chest test); deltoid/hip flexor fatigability
Respiratory: Dyspnea on exertion, orthopnea (diaphragm weakness), inability to lie flat

Key differentiating features from other NMJ / motor disorders: Sensory systems entirely spared; reflexes normal; no autonomic dysfunction (distinguishes from Lambert-Eaton); weakness worsens with sustained activity (distinguishes from Lambert-Eaton where strength briefly improves with exercise — "facilitation"); no fasciculations (distinguishes from ALS).
Muscle groups affected — select all present SELECT ALL THAT APPLY
Ocular weakness — ptosis (unilateral/bilateral, variable), diplopia (painless, intermittent or persistent)
Ptosis in MG is characteristically variable and asymmetric. Enhanced ptosis sign: manually elevating one ptotic lid increases ptosis of the opposite lid (disinhibition of the levator). Cogan's lid twitch: upward twitch of the lid when returning gaze from downgaze to primary position — sensitive for MG-related ptosis. Fatigable ptosis: sustained upgaze worsens ptosis within 30–60 seconds. Diplopia in MG does not conform to a single cranial nerve distribution — it is variable, intermittent, and may change with sustained lateral gaze.
Pupil is always spared in MG. Pupillary involvement rules out MG and points to CN III compression (aneurysm), Horner's syndrome, or pharmacologic dilation. Always examine pupils in diplopia presentations.
Bulbar weakness — nasal dysarthria, dysphagia, fatigable chewing, dysphonia, jaw drop with prolonged eating
Bulbar MG carries the highest risk of aspiration and respiratory compromise. Nasal speech ("hot potato voice"): Velopharyngeal incompetence causes nasal escape — worsens during sustained counting to 50 or longer sentences. Dysphagia: both liquids and solids — aspiration risk especially for thin liquids. Jaw drop: bilateral masseter fatigue causes inability to keep mouth closed during prolonged chewing — ask patients if they need to support their jaw with their hand when eating. MuSK-antibody positive MG has a predilection for severe bulbar and facial involvement with relatively preserved limb strength.
MuSK-MG clinical features: MuSK-positive MG (5–10% of generalized MG) typically presents with prominent bulbar, facial, and respiratory weakness, often with tongue and facial muscle atrophy in advanced disease. Ocular involvement is less prominent. Edrophonium test is frequently negative or equivocal in MuSK-MG. RNS may show decrement predominantly in facial muscles.
Limb or axial weakness — proximal > distal; neck flexor weakness; deltoid/hip flexor fatigability; arm abduction count test
Limb weakness in MG is predominantly proximal — difficulty rising from a chair, climbing stairs, raising arms above head. Arm abduction fatigue test: sustained arm abduction at 90° — time to fatigue <60 seconds is abnormal. Neck flexor weakness (chin-to-chest test): inability to lift the head off the pillow or hold chin to chest against resistance — correlates with respiratory muscle weakness and is an important warning sign. Reflexes are normal. Distal strength is usually preserved.
Neck flexor and respiratory correlation: Neck flexor weakness in MG correlates with diaphragmatic weakness — if a patient has difficulty with neck flexion, respiratory muscle assessment (FVC, NIF) should be performed urgently. Together, neck flexion weakness and bulbar involvement in an MG patient at risk for crisis should prompt ICU evaluation.
Respiratory weakness — dyspnea on exertion, orthopnea, short sentences, tachypnea, paradoxical breathing, reduced breath count
Respiratory muscle involvement in MG can progress rapidly without warning. Breath count test (single breath count, SBC): patient counts aloud in one breath in a normal speaking voice — normal ≥25; ≤15 suggests significant respiratory weakness. Single breath hold time (SBHT): inability to hold breath >10 seconds is abnormal. FVC (forced vital capacity): most sensitive bedside respiratory metric — normal ≥20 mL/kg; FVC <20 mL/kg prompts ICU admission. NIF (negative inspiratory force): <−30 cmH₂O indicates impending failure. Critical thresholds (20/30 rule): FVC <20 mL/kg → ICU; NIF worse than −30 cmH₂O → BiPAP consideration; NIF worse than −20 cmH₂O → intubation threshold.
Do NOT rely on SpO₂ or ABG for early warning in MG crisis. Hypoxemia and hypercapnia develop only immediately before respiratory arrest in neuromuscular failure — they are very late signs. Serial FVC and NIF measurements every 4–6 hours are required in admitted MG patients with any respiratory symptoms.

Step 2 · Bedside Diagnostic Tests
Several bedside tests can support the diagnosis of MG before or in addition to serology and electrophysiology. The ice pack test and fatigable ptosis test are especially useful in acute settings where formal testing is unavailable. The edrophonium (Tensilon) test has largely been superseded but remains in use in some centers.
Fatigability test principle: All bedside tests exploit the defining feature of MG — neuromuscular transmission failure worsens with sustained or repeated activity and partially recovers with rest or cooling.

Sustained upgaze test: Ask patient to maintain upgaze for 60–90 seconds — ptosis worsens (sensitivity ~75% for ocular MG)
Simpson fatigability (sustained upgaze 2 min): Positive if ptosis worsens within 2 minutes of sustained upgaze
Curtain sign: Manual elevation of one ptotic lid increases ptosis in the other — bilateral levator inhibition mechanism
Ice pack test PREFERRED BEDSIDE TEST — SAFE, NO MEDICATIONS
Ice pack test — ice applied over closed ptotic eye for 2 minutes; positive if ptosis improves ≥2 mm
Mechanism: Cooling reduces acetylcholinesterase activity, increasing ACh availability at the NMJ. Cooling may also directly improve neuromuscular transmission efficiency. Technique: Measure baseline ptosis (MRD1 — distance from midpupil to upper lid margin). Apply ice pack over closed eyelid(s) for 2 minutes. Immediately recheck MRD1. Positive: ≥2 mm improvement in ptosis.

Performance: Meta-analyses report sensitivity ~77–94% and specificity ~96–100% for ocular MG. The high specificity makes it very useful — a positive result strongly supports MG. The test is essentially risk-free (no medications, no cardiovascular risk). Preferred first bedside test in any patient with ptosis of uncertain cause.
Pitfall: False positives can occur with Horner's syndrome (cold-induced sympathomimetic effect on Müller's muscle) and rarely other conditions. If the test is positive without other features of MG, confirm with serology and electrophysiology.
Fatigability tests — sustained upgaze, arm abduction, neck flexion PERFORM AT BEDSIDE
Fatigability demonstrated on examination — sustained upgaze (ptosis), sustained arm abduction, or neck flexor weakness on hold testing
Sustained upgaze (60–120 sec): Worsening ptosis confirms fatigability. Record time to ptosis onset.
Arm abduction (shoulder at 90°, 60–120 sec): Time to arm drop — abnormal if <60 seconds, strongly abnormal if <30 seconds.
Neck flexion hold: Patient lifts head off pillow against resistance — sustained head lift weakness or rapid fatigue is characteristic. Neck flexors are more sensitive than neck extensors in MG.
Speech fatigability: Ask patient to count to 50 or read aloud — nasal quality and dysarthria worsen progressively.
Edrophonium (Tensilon) test — if performed USE WITH ATROPINE READY — CARDIOVASCULAR MONITORING REQUIRED
Edrophonium test (IV short-acting AChE inhibitor, 2 mg test dose then 8 mg) — assess for unequivocal improvement in a clearly defined, measurable weakness
Sensitivity 83.5%, specificity 87.7% (Finsterer et al. 2026, n=182 confirmed MG patients and 324 controls — retrospective case-control). Adverse events in 11.5% (91.4% mild — fasciculations, nausea, lacrimation, bradycardia). Serious events are rare but include bronchospasm, severe bradycardia, and syncope — atropine 0.4 mg IV must be immediately available.

Test protocol: (1) Establish a clear, measurable endpoint (ptosis degree by ruler, diplopia improvement, arm abduction time). (2) IV access + cardiac monitor. (3) 2 mg test dose. (4) If no response after 45 seconds and no severe cholinergic reaction: 8 mg (may repeat in 2 mg increments). (5) Observe for 5 minutes for definite improvement.

MuSK-MG caveat: Edrophonium sensitivity is lower in MuSK-antibody positive MG — negative test does NOT exclude MG if serology or SFEMG is pending.
Practical point: The edrophonium test has largely been supplanted by serology (AChR/MuSK antibodies) and SFEMG for formal diagnosis. However, it remains clinically useful for rapid bedside confirmation in acute settings, particularly for ptosis or diplopia that can be objectively measured. Do not use it as the sole diagnostic test.
Step 3 · Serological Testing — Antibody Panel
Antibody testing is the cornerstone of MG diagnosis — positive results are highly specific and define immunologic subtype, which guides therapy (especially complement inhibitors, B-cell therapies). A structured sequential antibody testing approach is recommended: AChR first (most common), then MuSK if AChR-negative, then LRP4 if both negative.
MG antibody prevalence (from pooled data):
AChR antibodies: ~85% of all MG (RIPA gold standard); 80–85% sensitivity for generalized MG; 50–70% for purely ocular MG. Specificity essentially 100% — false positives are extremely rare (may occur in thymoma without clinical MG, D-penicillamine use, rarely Lambert-Eaton). Three subtypes: binding (most sensitive), blocking, and modulating.
MuSK antibodies: ~40–70% of AChR-negative generalized MG (5–10% of all MG). IgG4 predominantly. Predilection for bulbar/facial/respiratory phenotype. Negative RNS more common. Responds to rituximab. RIPA standard; CBA more sensitive.
LRP4 antibodies: ~1–5% of double-seronegative (AChR-/MuSK-) MG. Clinical utility of commercial LRP4 testing is debated (lower specificity). Consider in double-seronegative MG with confirmed NMJ disorder on SFEMG.
Seronegative MG (true triple-negative): ~2–5% after standard RIPA. Higher-sensitivity cell-based assay (CBA) for clustered AChR/MuSK detects antibodies in ~40–50% of RIPA-seronegative cases. Repeat AChR-binding at 12 months (15% seroconvert within 1 year).
AChR antibody (acetylcholine receptor) FIRST TEST — HIGHEST YIELD
AChR binding antibody (RIPA) — standard gold standard. If negative, also consider AChR-modulating and AChR-blocking antibodies.
AChR-binding antibody by radioimmunoassay (RIPA) is the standard diagnostic test — sensitivity 80–85% for generalized MG, 50–70% for purely ocular MG. Positive result is essentially pathognomonic for MG (>99% specificity). Titer does not correlate with disease severity — do not use titer alone to guide treatment intensity. However, falling titers may be a useful marker of response to long-term immunosuppression or thymectomy at the population level.

Cell-based assay (CBA) for clustered AChR: More sensitive than RIPA for seronegative cases — detects antibodies in some RIPA-negative MG patients. Available at specialized labs (Mayo, Oxford).
Titin and RyR antibodies: Anti-titin (striated muscle antibody) — positive in ~25–30% of MG overall; in MG patients <60 years, strongly predicts thymoma. Anti-ryanodine receptor (RyR) antibody — also predicts thymoma and is associated with more severe disease. These are not diagnostic but are important for thymoma risk stratification.
MuSK antibody (muscle-specific kinase) — test if AChR-negative SECOND TEST — IF AChR NEGATIVE
MuSK-IgG4 antibody (RIPA or CBA) — predominantly bulbar/facial/respiratory phenotype; lower edrophonium sensitivity
MuSK-MG clinical profile: predominantly female; prominent bulbar (nasal voice, severe dysphagia), facial, and neck weakness; tongue atrophy and facial muscle wasting may develop; limb weakness milder; respiratory failure more abrupt. Crisis risk is high relative to MGFA class. Edrophonium test often negative or equivocal — AChE inhibitors may worsen some MuSK-MG patients. RNS shows decrement more reliably in facial muscles than limb muscles. Treatment: MuSK-MG responds better to rituximab than to standard immunosuppressants; eculizumab is also effective.
MuSK testing is not indicated if AChR is positive. MuSK antibodies very rarely coexist with AChR antibodies. Test sequentially: AChR first, MuSK second if AChR negative. Some institutions also test for MuSK CBA (live cell-based assay) which has higher sensitivity than RIPA for MuSK-IgG.
LRP4 and seronegative MG workup IF AChR AND MuSK BOTH NEGATIVE
LRP4 antibody, CBA testing, and plan for seronegative confirmed MG (electrodiagnosis required)
In double-seronegative (AChR−/MuSK−) MG: (1) Confirm NMJ disorder by SFEMG — essential. (2) Consider LRP4 CBA — clinical utility debated; clinical evidence for specificity in seronegative MG is limited. (3) Repeat AChR-binding antibody at 12 months — ~15% seroconvert. (4) Higher-sensitivity RIPAs and CBA for clustered AChR/MuSK at specialized labs (e.g., Oxford Neuroimmunology, Mayo). (5) Consider congenital myasthenic syndrome (CMS) genetic panel if onset in childhood, family history, or refractory to immunosuppression.
Agrin antibodies: Present in some triple-negative MG patients — investigational but promising biomarker. Not available for routine clinical testing. Titin, agrin, cortactin, and Kv1.4 antibodies are all associated with subsets of MG but are research-level tests.
Step 4 · Electrophysiological Testing — RNS and SFEMG
Electrophysiology confirms a primary postsynaptic NMJ disorder when antibody testing is negative or equivocal. SFEMG is the most sensitive test for MG (80–100% for generalized, 93% for ocular). RNS is less sensitive but more widely available. Both tests have important limitations and must be interpreted in clinical context.
Electrophysiologic tests compared:
RNS (repetitive nerve stimulation): Stimulate nerve at 2–5 Hz for a train of stimuli; measure CMAP amplitude decrement. Significant decrement: ≥10% decline from 1st to 4th response. Sensitivity: 50–70% for generalized MG; ~20–50% for purely ocular MG. Specificity: high for postsynaptic NMJ disorder. Test proximal muscles (trapezius, nasalis, orbicularis oculi) to improve sensitivity.
SFEMG (single-fiber EMG): Records jitter (variability in interpotential interval between pairs of muscle fibers from the same motor unit). Increased jitter (+/- blocking) is the most sensitive measure of NMJ dysfunction. Sensitivity 80–100% for generalized MG; 93% for ocular MG. Highly operator-dependent and technically demanding. Normal SFEMG in a clinically affected muscle essentially excludes a NMJ disorder.
Repetitive nerve stimulation (RNS) WIDELY AVAILABLE — TEST PROXIMAL + FACIAL MUSCLES
RNS at 2–5 Hz — CMAP decrement ≥10% between 1st and 4th response in at least one muscle
Technique: Stimulate at 3 Hz (or 2–5 Hz) for a train of 5–10 stimuli; measure amplitude/area of the 4th response relative to the 1st. A ≥10% decrement is abnormal. Repeat after brief exercise (10 seconds) — post-exercise facilitation (amplitude increase) confirms NMJ disorder. Distal muscles (ADM, hypothenar) have lower sensitivity — test trapezius, nasalis, frontalis, deltoid for better yield in MG.

Sensitivity by disease extent: Generalized MG ~50–70%; ocular MG ~20–50%. Normal RNS does not exclude MG — negative RNS with suspected MG should proceed to SFEMG.
Post-exercise CMAP facilitation: In Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS), RNS shows abnormal decrement at low rate AND dramatic facilitation after brief exercise or high-frequency stimulation (≥100% CMAP amplitude increase). This pattern distinguishes LEMS from MG and should be specifically sought when the pretest probability of LEMS is considered (middle-aged smoker, males, proximal limb weakness, reduced/absent reflexes that recover post-exercise).
Single-fiber EMG (SFEMG) MOST SENSITIVE TEST — REQUIRED IF RNS NORMAL AND AB NEGATIVE
SFEMG — increased jitter (mean consecutive difference >55 µs) ± blocking in clinically affected muscle; or abnormal jitter in ≥2 of 20 pairs
SFEMG records action potential discharge from individual muscle fibers of the same motor unit. Jitter: variability (standard deviation) of the interpotential interval (IPI) between two simultaneously firing single fibers — reflects NMJ transmission safety factor. Normal mean jitter ~15–55 µs depending on age and muscle. Blocking: complete failure of one fiber to discharge with a given stimulus — indicates severe NMJ transmission failure. Present in higher-grade MG.

Key rules: SFEMG must be performed in a clinically weak or affected muscle. Normal SFEMG in the clinically affected muscle essentially rules out MG at that moment. Test the frontalis (for ocular MG) or EDC (extensor digitorum communis) for generalized MG. Sensitivity for generalized MG: 80–100%; for ocular MG: 93%.
SFEMG is positive in many other neuromuscular disorders — it is sensitive but not specific for MG alone. Increased jitter also occurs in ALS, myopathy, Lambert-Eaton, congenital myasthenic syndromes, and other NMJ disorders. Clinical context determines interpretation. A normal SFEMG in a clinically affected muscle is the most powerful negative finding in the MG workup.
Step 5 · Thymic Imaging, Osserman Staging, and Subgroup Classification
Thymic pathology is present in ~90% of AChR-positive MG patients — thymic hyperplasia in ~70% and thymoma in ~10–15%. Chest CT or MRI is mandatory in all newly diagnosed MG. The Osserman classification (modified) remains historically important and is still used in some centers alongside MGFA.
Osserman classification (modified Osserman & Genkins 1971) — historical but widely used:
Stage I: Ocular only — limited to ptosis and diplopia
Stage IIA: Mild generalized — slow onset, no crisis; predominantly limb
Stage IIB: Moderate generalized — skeletal, bulbar involvement; no crisis; no respiratory
Stage III: Acute fulminant — respiratory crisis; high thymoma association
Stage IV: Late severe — develops from stages I–IIB; respiratory ± severe generalized

MGFA classification (Jaretzki 2000) is the current standard for clinical research and trial enrollment. Osserman staging is preserved here for historical context and clinical familiarity.
Chest CT / MRI — thymic imaging MANDATORY IN ALL NEWLY DIAGNOSED MG
CT chest (or MRI mediastinum) — thymic hyperplasia, thymoma, or normal thymus
Thymic hyperplasia: Diffuse symmetric enlargement of thymus. Present in ~70% of AChR-positive MG, especially in young women. CT: smooth symmetric enlargement, no mass. Lymphofollicular hyperplasia — germinal centers with B cells are the proposed source of AChR autoantibodies. Thymectomy is recommended for non-thymomatous generalized AChR-positive MG in patients 18–65 (MGTX trial showed significantly better outcomes vs. immunosuppression alone over 3 years).

Thymoma: Soft tissue mass in anterior mediastinum. Present in ~10–15% of MG. CT sensitivity ~75%, specificity ~62% (CT has significant inter-observer variability in distinguishing thymoma from hyperplasia). Thymectomy is mandatory for all thymoma regardless of MG severity — risk of malignant transformation and local invasion. Thymoma WHO classification (A, AB, B1, B2, B3, C) predicts prognosis. Anti-titin antibody in MG patients <60 years strongly predicts thymoma.
MRI for thymic imaging: Chemical shift MRI (in-phase vs. out-of-phase) has better sensitivity for distinguishing thymoma from hyperplasia than CT alone — fat suppression with signal drop-off on out-of-phase imaging favors hyperplasia; no signal drop = thymoma. Consider MRI if CT is equivocal. FDG-PET is reserved for invasive or recurrent thymoma evaluation.
MG subgroup classification — guides treatment selection CRITICAL FOR THERAPY CHOICE
MG immunologic subgroup — determines treatment priorities (thymectomy, complement inhibition, B-cell therapy)
AChR-positive, non-thymomatous, early-onset (<50y, predominantly female): Strong thymic hyperplasia association. Thymectomy recommended. Responds to standard immunosuppression (pyridostigmine, steroids, azathioprine/mycophenolate). Eculizumab (anti-C5) and ravulizumab FDA-approved.

AChR-positive with thymoma: Thymectomy mandatory (all stages). MG may persist or worsen after thymectomy. Anti-titin/RyR antibodies common. Require long-term immunosuppression.

AChR-positive, late-onset (>50y, M=F): Thymic atrophy (no hyperplasia). Thymectomy benefit is less clear in this group. Complement inhibitors effective.

MuSK-positive: Thymectomy NOT routinely recommended (no thymic pathology). Rituximab preferred immunosuppressant. Complement inhibitors may be less effective (different pathomechanism — IgG4, not complement-mediated).

Seronegative (triple-negative): Diagnosis requires SFEMG. Management as per clinical phenotype.
Step 6 · Crisis Assessment — Myasthenic vs. Cholinergic Crisis
Myasthenic crisis (MC) occurs in ~15–20% of MG patients and requires ICU admission, respiratory monitoring, and rapid immunotherapy. Distinguishing MC from cholinergic crisis (excess pyridostigmine) is critical — they have opposite treatments. Both can cause respiratory failure and bulbar weakness, making differentiation essential.
Myasthenic crisis vs. Cholinergic crisis — key differentiators:
Myasthenic crisis: Caused by under-stimulation (severe MG exacerbation). Worsening weakness + bulbar + respiratory. NO excess cholinergic signs. Treat: hold pyridostigmine during intubation (secretions), PLEX or IVIG, steroids after airway secured.
Cholinergic crisis: Caused by excess pyridostigmine (≥120 mg q3h is suspicious threshold). Weakness AND: miosis (small pupils), excessive secretions (SLUDGE: salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation, GI cramps, emesis), bradycardia, muscle fasciculations, diaphoresis. Treat: hold all cholinesterase inhibitors; atropine for muscarinic symptoms; supportive care / ventilation.

Common MC triggers (memorize): Infection (URI most common), aspiration pneumonia, medication changes (dose reduction), drugs that worsen MG (aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, β-blockers, magnesium, neuromuscular blocking agents), surgery/anesthesia, stress, thyroid disease, tapering steroids too quickly, pregnancy.
Crisis status assessment EMERGENCY — ASSESS IMMEDIATELY IF RESPIRATORY SYMPTOMS
Current respiratory and functional status — is this a crisis presentation?
Impending crisis — admit to ICU: FVC <20 mL/kg OR NIF worse than −30 cmH₂O OR serial decline in FVC/NIF OR tachypnea + accessory muscle use + inability to count to 20 in one breath.

Intubation thresholds: NIF worse than −20 cmH₂O + respiratory distress. FVC <10–15 mL/kg + distress. Increasing work of breathing, hypercapnia, or inability to protect airway. Do NOT use SpO₂ or ABG as primary criteria — these are late signs.

RSI for intubation in MG: Use non-depolarizing NMB (rocuronium 0.6 mg/kg — ~50% reduced dose vs. standard). Avoid succinylcholine — patients are RESISTANT (due to reduced receptor density; require ~2.6× normal dose) and prolonged blockade is unpredictable. Avoid magnesium (worsens NMJ block). Sugammadex available for rapid reversal of rocuronium.
Treatment of myasthenic crisis: (1) Secure airway if indicated. (2) Hold pyridostigmine (reduces secretions during intubation; can resume pre-extubation). (3) PLEX (5 sessions over 7–10 days; 40–60 mL/kg/session) OR IVIG (2 g/kg over 2–5 days) — equivalent efficacy; PLEX acts faster (3–5 days) vs. IVIG (5–10 days). (4) Steroids after airway secured (IV methylprednisolone or oral prednisone). (5) Treat precipitating cause. (6) Avoid drugs that worsen NMJ transmission.
Drugs to avoid in MG — has the patient received any? CRITICAL SAFETY CHECK
High-risk medications that can precipitate or worsen MG crisis — review medication list
HIGH RISK — may precipitate crisis / strongly avoid:
• Aminoglycosides (gentamicin, tobramycin, neomycin) — potent NMJ blockade; avoid if possible
• Fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, moxifloxacin) — multiple reports of MG exacerbation
• Magnesium sulfate (IV) — directly impairs presynaptic ACh release; dangerous in MG (tocolysis, eclampsia)
• Neuromuscular blocking agents (vecuronium, atracurium) — dramatically prolonged blockade; reduce dose by ~50%
• D-penicillamine — can induce de novo drug-induced MG (AChR antibodies)
• Beta-blockers (systemic) — may worsen NMJ transmission
• Chloroquine / hydroxychloroquine — controversial; case reports of worsening
MODERATE RISK — use with caution: Macrolides (azithromycin), procainamide, quinine, lithium, phenytoin, ACE inhibitors (cough can worsen aspiration risk)
SAFE TO USE: Standard corticosteroids, IVIG, pyridostigmine, azathioprine, mycophenolate, rituximab, eculizumab, efgartigimod, rozanolixizumab
Checkpoint inhibitor (immune checkpoint) immunotherapy: PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors (pembrolizumab, nivolumab, atezolizumab) can precipitate de novo MG or dramatically worsen pre-existing MG — often severe, refractory, and rapidly fatal. Report cases involving ocular/bulbar/respiratory crisis. If oncologic therapy is required in an MG patient, neurology co-management is essential.
